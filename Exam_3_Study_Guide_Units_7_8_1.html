Psychosocial Status Assessment

USLO 1 · Recognize & Analyze Cues
▾

USLO 1 asks you to examine physiological and psychosocial status together — the "E" in BUBBLE-HE is only a start. The first 3 months postpartum are the most emotionally vulnerable window for any mother, shaped by birth experience, parity, age, marital status, and financial/family stability. Differentiating the three puerperal mood presentations is a high-yield NCLEX distinction.

Priority Interventions — Blues
  • Support and reassurance — this is a normal, expected reaction to hormone withdrawal (estrogen, progesterone, cortisol) plus the demands of new motherhood
  • Proactively educate the woman and family before it happens so it isn't mistaken for failure or illness
  • Assess resources available at home: food, clothing, shelter, transportation, relationship support
  • Connect to community resources (church groups, Mother's Day Out, La Leche League) so she knows she isn't alone
Priority Interventions — Depression
  • Prompt referral for evaluation and treatment — do not "wait and see"
  • Use a validated screening tool (e.g., Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale) at postpartum visits
  • Assess directly for suicidal ideation in severe presentations
  • Involve support system and schedule close follow-up
Priority Interventions — Psychosis

Psychiatric emergency. Ensure immediate safety of mother and infant, notify the provider/psychiatric team, do not leave the mother unsupervised with the infant if judgment is impaired. Any patient with a history of mood disorder or prior depression should be flagged for community mental-health referral and follow-up before hospital discharge — don't wait for symptoms to appear.

Fatigue — Expected Physiological/Psychological Response

Postpartum tiredness is not something that simply resolves once home — the combined demands of infant care plus recovery from birth compound physical and mental exhaustion, and mothers still tend to carry the primary caregiving load even when partners are involved. This is a normal expected finding, but ongoing/worsening sleep disturbance should also prompt a mood-disorder screen — not just be dismissed as "new-parent tired."

Patient Education — Psychosocial
  • Normalize the emotional rollercoaster; explain the difference between expected blues and reportable symptoms
  • Teach the family to recognize warning signs (persistent sadness beyond 10 days, hopelessness, disinterest in the infant, thoughts of self-harm) and report them
  • Encourage enlisting help from partner/family/friends for rest and household tasks
  • Provide contact information for follow-up and community/mental-health resources before discharge

Postpartum Recovery & Self-Care

USLO 4: holistic nursing care for the postpartum woman. These are the four pillars of promoting recovery, straight from the "Promoting Recovery and Self-Care" content in your deck.

1 Activity & Rest
  • Balance early ambulation (reduces VTE risk) with adequate rest periods
  • Cluster care to protect uninterrupted rest windows
  • Encourage naps when the infant sleeps, especially during Taking-in
2 Nourishment
  • Breastfeeding mother: increased caloric and fluid needs to support milk production
  • Formula-feeding mother: standard postpartum nutrition, no added lactation demands
  • Iron-rich foods to support recovery from blood loss
3 Elimination
  • Voiding: monitor pattern closely — a distended bladder displaces and inhibits uterine tone
  • Bowel: monitor return of function; risk of constipation after perineal trauma or cesarean
  • Encourage fluids, fiber, and early ambulation to support both
4 Perineal Care
  • Perineal hygiene: front-to-back cleansing, peri-bottle after toileting
  • Topical medications as ordered for pain/healing
  • Ice packs: first 24 hours — reduces edema
  • Sitz baths: after 24 hours — promotes healing and comfort

Care of the Postpartum Surgical Patient

Cesarean Birth
  • Full BUBBLE-HE assessment still applies — surgical status adds to, not replaces, routine postpartum assessment
  • Incisional care: monitor REEDA at the incision site
  • Recovery from anesthesia: monitor level of consciousness, return of sensation/motor function (regional) or airway/respiratory status (general)
Tubal Ligation
  • Post-operative care principles apply on top of routine postpartum recovery
  • Monitor for typical minor-surgery complications (bleeding, infection at the site)
  • Provide clear counseling that the procedure is permanent
Respiratory Care
  • Incentive spirometry to prevent atelectasis after anesthesia
  • Early ambulation as soon as cleared — reduces VTE and supports lung expansion
  • Pain control adequate enough to support deep breathing and mobility
Patient Education — Holistic Discharge Teaching
  • Cover infant feeding and bathing, breastfeeding, perineal hygiene, physical activity, rest, and expected emotional changes before discharge — a self-assessment checklist helps identify gaps
  • Teach how to locate/palpate the fundus and explain that prone positioning postpartum is comfortable and beneficial (supports involution by tipping the uterus forward)
  • Nutrition: adequate protein for tissue repair, balanced diet with fruit/vegetables/whole grains for bowel regularity
  • Weight expectations: ~10–12 lb lost immediately, another ~5 lb from diuresis/diaphoresis over days 2–5; most women return to prepregnancy weight by 6–8 weeks
  • Exercise: start with Kegel exercises; modified sit-ups and knee/leg rolls once cleared (especially helpful with diastasis recti)
  • Early discharge (within 48 hrs) requires strict criteria be met (stable vitals, adequate voiding/ambulation, Hgb >10, no significant bleeding) — reinforce follow-up visit timing and what to report at home

Uterine Atony (Early Postpartum Hemorrhage)

Leading cause of early PPH · SBAR framing — acute complication
Pathophysiology

Failure of the uterine myometrium to contract/retract after birth. Normally, muscle fibers clamp down around the spiral arteries at the placental site (a "living ligature"). Without this contraction, the vessels stay open — no hemostasis, ongoing blood loss.

Etiology
  • Overdistended uterus (multiples, hydramnios, macrosomia)
  • Prolonged or precipitous labor
  • Retained placental fragments
  • Oxytocin induction/augmentation
  • Chorioamnionitis
  • Anesthetic agents that relax the uterus (halogenated agents, magnesium sulfate, nifedipine)
Signs & Symptoms
  • Soft, boggy uterus filled with clots/blood
  • Dark red blood, often with clots
  • Fundus may be difficult to locate or deviated
  • Excessive/continuous bleeding despite a delivered placenta
Risk Factors
  • Grand multiparity (stretched musculature)
  • Previous atony/PPH history
  • Operative birth (forceps, vacuum, cesarean)
  • Prolonged/mismanaged 3rd stage
Complications
  • Hemorrhagic shock
  • DIC
  • Need for transfusion / hysterectomy in severe cases
  • Postpartum anemia, fatigue impairing bonding/recovery
Labs / Diagnostics
  • CBC (H&H trend), coagulation panel (PT/PTT, fibrinogen)
  • Type & crossmatch
  • Quantitative blood loss measurement (weighed pads/drapes)
  • Bimanual exam to distinguish atony (boggy) vs. laceration (firm, midline uterus with bright bleeding)
Priority Nursing Interventions (in order)
  • 1. Fundal massage — stimulate contraction first
  • 2. Empty the bladder — distension displaces/inhibits contraction
  • 3. Reassess — if still boggy, escalate
  • 4. Uterotonic administration — oxytocin first-line; methergine/hemabate/misoprostol per contraindications (e.g., avoid methergine in hypertension, avoid hemabate in asthma)
  • 5. Notify provider and prepare for possible bimanual compression or OR if refractory
Treatment

Uterotonics, IV fluids, possible blood product transfusion, manual removal of retained fragments, surgical intervention (uterine artery ligation, B-Lynch suture, hysterectomy) if uncontrolled.

Patient Education
  • Report saturating a pad in under an hour
  • Report large clots or dizziness/lightheadedness
  • Rest and avoid overexertion during recovery
  • Follow up promptly if bleeding increases after discharge

Breast Engorgement

Physiological — days 3–5 postpartum
Pathophysiology

Increased blood flow and milk production cause congestion/overdistention of the ductal system and obstruction of lymphatic drainage. Back-pressure on full milk glands inhibits further milk production.

Etiology
  • Infrequent feeding
  • Ineffective emptying of the breasts
  • Physiologic response to milk "coming in"
Signs & Symptoms
  • Mild: fullness, discomfort
  • Severe: pain, tenderness, hardness, warmth
  • Shiny, taut breast skin; swelling clavicle → axilla → lower ribs
  • Flattened nipples → latch difficulty
Risk Factors
  • Delayed/infrequent breastfeeding
  • Poor latch/ineffective emptying
  • Abrupt weaning
Complications
  • Decreased milk supply if unrelieved
  • Progression to mastitis (milk stasis)
  • Breastfeeding cessation from pain/frustration
Labs / Diagnostics

Clinical diagnosis — no labs typically needed. Rule out mastitis if fever/redness develop.

Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Frequent feeding or pumping to relieve stasis (most important intervention)
  • Warm compress before feeding to encourage let-down; cold compress between feedings to reduce swelling
  • Encourage well-fitting, supportive (non-underwire) bra
Treatment

Milk removal (breastfeeding/pumping) is the definitive treatment. NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen) for pain — most effective when taken before feeding in anticipation of post-feeding discomfort.

Patient Education
  • Typically resolves in ~24 hours with regular removal
  • Don't skip feedings to "rest" the breast — worsens engorgement
  • Wear supportive bra; avoid compressive/underwire bras

Ineffective Breastfeeding (Sore, Cracked, Flat, or Inverted Nipples)

SOAPIE framing — ongoing management
Pathophysiology

Improper latch (nipple-only, not areola) causes repetitive friction/trauma to the nipple tissue. Flat or inverted nipples reduce the infant's ability to draw tissue deep into the mouth for an effective seal.

Etiology
  • Poor positioning/latch technique
  • Anatomic nipple variant (flat/inverted)
  • Prolonged non-nutritive sucking
Signs & Symptoms
  • Nipple pain beyond initial latch
  • Cracks, fissures, bleeding
  • Infant fussy/unlatching frequently
  • Poor infant weight gain if intake inadequate
Risk Factors
  • First-time breastfeeding mothers (primiparity)
  • No lactation support/education
  • Nipple anatomic variants
Complications
  • Nipple trauma → portal of entry for infection (mastitis)
  • Early breastfeeding cessation
  • Inadequate infant intake
Labs / Diagnostics

Clinical/observational — direct observation of a feeding session is the primary "diagnostic" tool.

Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Directly observe and correct latch (areola, not just nipple, in mouth)
  • Vary positioning to redistribute pressure points
  • Breast shells for flat/inverted nipples — help nipples become more protuberant and protect from clothing friction
  • Break suction with a clean finger before removing infant from breast
Treatment

Lactation consultant referral, lanolin for cracked nipples after ensuring latch is corrected first (latch correction is the priority — creams alone don't fix the mechanical cause).

Patient Education
  • Air-dry nipples after feeding
  • Avoid soap on nipples (dries skin, removes protective oils)
  • Call for help early — most latch problems are fixable with support

Mastitis

Infection of the mammary glands · ~2–4 weeks postpartum
Pathophysiology

Bacteria (most commonly Staphylococcus aureus) enter through a crack/fissure/abrasion in the nipple, invading the ductal system. Causes inflammatory edema, enlarged axillary lymph nodes, and engorgement with obstruction of milk flow. Usually unilateral.

Etiology
  • Milk stasis / plugged duct
  • S. aureus most common organism (also E. coli, Strep, MRSA)
  • Entry via nipple trauma from poor latch
Signs & Symptoms
  • Fever, chills, malaise, generalized aches
  • Unilateral tender, hardened, warm, reddened area
  • Early sign: triangular flush under the affected breast
  • Cracked/fissured nipples, purulent nipple drainage possible
  • Enlarged axillary lymph nodes on affected side
Risk Factors
  • Milk stasis, infrequent breastfeeding
  • Nipple trauma/cracks
  • Fatigue, primiparity
Complications
  • Breast abscess (especially with MRSA — more likely to abscess)
  • Sepsis if untreated
  • Premature weaning
Labs / Diagnostics
  • Usually clinical diagnosis (S/S based)
  • Breast milk culture if not responding to first-line therapy or MRSA suspected
  • CBC if systemic infection suspected
Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Continue breastfeeding/emptying the breast — do not stop; start on the affected side first each feeding
  • Frequent feeding or pumping every 2–4 hours (2–3 hrs if too painful to nurse directly — express instead)
  • Vary infant positioning to drain all ducts/lobes evenly
  • Heat or cold application for comfort
  • Encourage rest and increased fluids
Treatment

Antibiotics compatible with breastfeeding (e.g., cephalosporins). Complete the full course even after feeling better. MRSA-associated mastitis unresponsive to first-line therapy may need trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, clindamycin, or rifampin, ± incision and drainage of abscess. Breastfeeding continues even with MRSA — it's not transmitted through milk, only through direct contact with an open lesion/drainage.

Patient Education
  • Complete the entire antibiotic course
  • Avoid breast creams — they block Montgomery tubercle secretions
  • Report signs early: fever, redness, hardened tender area
  • Dry the breast after feeding; change wet breast pads/bra promptly

Episiotomy / Incision & Hemorrhoids

Perineal trauma · REEDA framework · normal healing vs. hematoma
Pathophysiology

An episiotomy (surgical incision) or spontaneous laceration disrupts perineal tissue integrity during the second stage of labor. Normal healing proceeds by primary intention — edges fuse within the first 24 hours. Hemorrhoids are distended rectal veins that become engorged from the sustained pressure of the fetal head/pushing during second-stage labor.

Etiology
  • Episiotomy (midline or mediolateral) or spontaneous perineal/vaginal/cervical laceration
  • Operative birth (forceps, vacuum) increases laceration risk
  • Hemorrhoids: venous pressure from prolonged pushing in 2nd stage; pre-existing pregnancy hemorrhoids can worsen
Signs & Symptoms
  • Normal: mild edema/bruising, edges fused by 24 hrs, minimal drainage
  • Pain with sitting, walking, bending, urinating, or defecating
  • Hemorrhoids: visible/palpable rectal swelling, tenderness, itching
  • Abnormal (REEDA-positive): redness, edema, ecchymosis beyond expected, purulent discharge, edges not approximated
Risk Factors
  • Operative/instrument-assisted birth
  • Large infant/prolonged 2nd stage (both laceration and hemorrhoid risk)
  • Poor perineal hygiene technique
  • Constipation/straining (worsens both episiotomy tension and hemorrhoids)
Complications
  • Perineal/vulvar/vaginal/ischiorectal hematoma — a blood-filled swelling from vessel damage; can cause severe hemorrhage
  • Wound infection/dehiscence
  • Painful defecation → stool withholding → constipation → further strain on the site
Labs / Diagnostics

Primarily a visual/physical exam finding — Sims' (side-lying) position with adequate lighting; gently lift the buttock to visualize. No routine labs unless infection or significant hemorrhage is suspected (then CBC/H&H as for any bleeding workup).

Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Assess using REEDA — Redness, Edema, Ecchymosis, Discharge, Approximation; document findings each shift

  • Ice pack to the perineum first 24–48 hours (local anesthesia, vasoconstriction, reduces edema/bleeding)
  • Moist heat (sitz bath, 100–105°F/37.8–40.5°C, 20 min, 3–4×/day) after 24–48 hours

Treatment

Topical anesthetic (e.g., lidocaine spray) after sitz bath; witch hazel pads (Tucks) for hemorrhoid comfort; stool softeners to prevent straining. Severe/refractory hemorrhoids may need manual repositioning (patient can be taught this) — most resolve within a few weeks postpartum. A confirmed hematoma requires prompt provider notification for possible incision/evacuation.

Patient Education
  • Front-to-back peri-care after every void/BM; change pad front-to-back
  • Ice first 24–48 hrs, then sitz baths
  • Report pain that suddenly worsens or feels disproportionate — this is the hematoma red flag, not "normal" postpartum soreness
  • Stool softeners and fluids/fiber to avoid straining, which can tear sutures
  • Hemorrhoids from pregnancy typically resolve within a few weeks — reassurance is appropriate alongside comfort measures

Parenting Process, Parental Tasks & Responsibility

USLO 5 · Take Action
▾

The transition to parenthood is a developmental process, not a single event. It can be especially difficult for first-time mothers with limited infant-care experience or those facing social isolation.

Common Struggles in the Transition
  • Anxiety and feelings of inadequacy about parenting skills
  • Lack of knowledge/confidence in baby care
  • Emotional concerns, low mood, and detachment toward the infant
  • Social isolation, especially for primiparous mothers
Nursing Goal

Create a supportive teaching environment that builds the parents' knowledge and confidence in caring for themselves and their infant — grounded in family-centered care.

How the Nurse Facilitates the Transition to Parenthood
  • Perform a needs assessment to identify the parents' knowledge/skill deficits
  • Use active listening and good communication skills to provide support
  • Empower parents by helping them recognize their own strengths
  • Facilitate the parents' participation in decision-making
  • Provide learning opportunities that move parents from dependence → independence and self-reliance
Parental TaskWhat It Looks Like
NurturingMeeting the infant's physical and emotional needs through holding, feeding, touching, and interacting
ProtectingBuilding a foundation of safety and security; responding consistently to infant cues
Interpreting infant cuesLearning that crying is communication — recognizing distinguishable cries for hunger, pain, tiredness, fussiness, or attention-seeking
Fostering self-soothingUnderstanding that newborns have a built-in capacity to console themselves (sucking, motion, distraction) and supporting that

Attachment Influences

USLO 5 · Recognize & Analyze Cues
▾

Bonding = the promotion of a unique, powerful relationship between parent and infant. Attachment = the reciprocal tie that exists between parent and infant, built through that bonding process. Both begin at pregnancy confirmation and continue through birth and beyond.

Maternal Attachment
  • En face position — mother's and infant's faces ~8 inches apart, on the same plane, seeking eye-to-eye contact
  • Exploration pattern: fingertips → extremities → full trunk, with soft stroking
  • Early skin-to-skin and breastfeeding in the first 30 minutes support this window
Paternal Attachment
  • Engrossment — intense interest, absorption, and preoccupation with the newborn
  • Observed as prolonged, almost hypnotic gazing at the infant
  • Fostered by delivery-room presence, skin-to-skin, diapering, and bottle-feeding involvement
Sibling Adjustment
  • Hurt, jealousy, sibling rivalry, and behavioral regression are common (e.g., a toilet-trained toddler asking for diapers again)
  • Sibling classes (ages 2–8) help ease the transition
  • Nurse-taught strategies: validate feelings, give the older child a "special job," praise age-appropriate behavior without criticizing regression
Grandparent Adjustment
  • Grandparents often exert strong influence on child-rearing practices
  • Grandparent classes focus on defining a supportive role (helping with siblings, cooking, household tasks) rather than a parenting role
  • Cover current recommendations on positioning, feeding, and responding to behavior cues
Assessing Attachment
  • Does the mother seem eager to care for her infant?
  • Is she touching her baby, making skin-to-skin contact?
  • What is her response when the baby cries?
  • Does she make eye contact when holding/feeding?
  • Any warning signs of appearing dazed or detached?
Interruptions to the Bonding Process
  • Infant separation for medical/surgical reasons (e.g., NICU admission)
  • Financial stress, chaotic home life, lack of family support
  • Substance use, concerns about returning to work/childcare
Promoting Attachment When Separation Occurs
  • Early and frequent access to the infant whenever medically possible
  • NICU staff encourage touching, speaking to, and skin-to-skin holding as soon as safe
  • Send photos and make frequent calls if the mother can't visit
  • Reassure the mother that bonding is ongoing — lack of early contact does not doom the relationship
Expected Attachment Findings
  • En face positioning, eye contact, soft voice ("baby talk"), progressive exploratory touch
  • Responds promptly and appropriately when the infant cries
  • Calls the infant by name; uses "we" language including the baby in the family
Unexpected Attachment Findings
  • Appearing dazed, detached, or disinterested when handling the infant
  • Avoiding eye contact or physical contact beyond what's required for care
  • Referring to the infant in impersonal terms ("it," "the baby") persistently, not just early exhaustion
  • Rough handling or a delayed/absent response to crying
Priority Interventions & Patient Education
  • Facilitate early, uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and rooming-in whenever safe
  • Model and narrate infant cues (hunger, tiredness) to build parental confidence in reading them
  • Include partner/siblings/grandparents in care tasks appropriate to their role rather than sidelining them
  • Document and report unexpected attachment findings — these warrant follow-up, not judgment, and may reflect fatigue, pain, or an emerging mood disorder rather than "not loving the baby"
  • Teach families in advance what normal attachment behaviors look like so they can recognize their own progress

Rubin's Maternal Tasks in Pregnancy

USLO 6 · Rubin (1975)
▾

Rubin (1975) described three phases a woman moves through as she assumes the mothering role. Timing below reflects Rubin's original framework — note that with today's shortened hospital stays, women often move through these phases faster and phases can overlap.

PhaseTimingMaternal Focus / BehaviorNursing Implication
Taking-inFirst 1–2 daysDependent on others for physical needs; physical exhaustion; elation, excitement, and/or anxiety; reliving the labor/birth events verbally and mentallyProvide physical care, let her rest and process the birth — not yet the teaching window
Taking-hold2nd–3rd day (through discharge)Initiates action; asks for help with self-care, begins caring for the baby, may be anxious about her mothering abilities; eagerly wants infant-care informationPrime teaching window — discharge teaching sticks best here; closely observe mother-infant interaction for poor bonding signs
Letting-goFirst 2–6 weeks postpartumMoves beyond mother-infant symbiosis, sees infant as an emerging individual; refocuses on partner, other children, and family issuesSupport family role adjustment; watch for maladjustment (an overprotective mother who resists help or excludes her partner)
Sample-question logic: A mother frequently touching baby's fingers/toes, asking many questions about her own recovery, and relying on staff to bring the baby = Taking-in — self-focused, dependent, exploratory touch, but not yet independently initiating care (that shift is Taking-hold).
Expected Progression
  • Movement Taking-in → Taking-hold → Letting-go in sequence, at a pace roughly matching the timing ranges above
  • Increasing independence and confidence in infant care over the first days to weeks
  • Eventual reintegration of partner, other children, and outside roles into the family unit
Unexpected / Maladjustment Findings
  • Prolonged dependency — still in "Taking-in" behavior well past 2 days, avoiding infant care
  • Excessive anxiety about mothering ability that doesn't improve with teaching/support
  • Overprotectiveness that resists help or excludes the partner ("Letting-go" failure)
  • Flat affect, disinterest, or difficulty bonding at any phase — screen for a mood disorder (see Psychosocial Status section)
Priority Interventions & Patient Education Across Phases
  • Taking-in: Prioritize physical care and rest; let the mother process/re-tell the birth story without rushing her into infant-care teaching
  • Taking-hold: This is your window — deliver discharge teaching here (feeding, bathing, cord/circumcision care, warning signs) while she's receptive and motivated; observe and gently coach mother-infant interaction
  • Letting-go: Support the family's redefinition of roles; involve partner and siblings; educate that grief over the "fantasy birth" or loss of pre-baby identity/routine is a normal part of this phase, not a sign of poor bonding
  • Across all phases: Educate the family in advance about what each phase looks like so slow independence in Taking-in isn't mistaken by the family for "not loving the baby enough"

Hemorrhage & Trauma

PPH = blood loss >500 mL vaginal / >1,000 mL cesarean, or a 10% Hct/Hgb drop. Early = first 24h (highest risk in the first hour). Late = 24h–12 weeks.

▾
4 T's: Tone (atony) · Trauma (lacerations/hematoma) · Tissue (retained placenta) · Thrombin (coagulopathy)
1

Uterine Atony

Leading cause of EARLY postpartum hemorrhage — "Tone"
▾
Pathophysiology

Myometrium fails to contract/retract after birth. Normally, interlacing muscle fibers act as "living ligatures," compressing the spiral arteries at the placental site. Without firm contraction, vessels stay open and bleed freely.

Etiology

Overdistended uterus, prolonged/precipitous labor, oxytocin induction/augmentation, chorioamnionitis, mismanaged 3rd stage.

Signs & Symptoms
  • Soft, boggy fundus, often displaced above umbilicus/off midline
  • Excessive bright red bleeding, large clots
  • ↑ HR then ↓ BP (late sign)
Risk Factors
  • Macrosomia, multiples, polyhydramnios
  • High parity, previous atony/PPH
  • Forceps/vacuum/cesarean birth
  • Magnesium sulfate, halogenated anesthesia
  • Retained placental fragments
Complications
  • Hypovolemic shock
  • DIC
  • Hysterectomy
  • Death if unrecognized
Labs / Diagnostics
  • Quantitative blood loss (weigh pads, graduated drapes; 1g=1mL)
  • Trending Hgb/Hct (10% drop = diagnostic)
  • Type & crossmatch, coags if ongoing
🎯 Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Fundal massage immediately
  • Empty bladder (full bladder worsens atony)
  • Continuous VS monitoring, 2nd IV access
  • Notify provider if unresolved with massage
Treatment
  • Uterotonics: oxytocin first-line; methylergonovine (avoid in HTN); carboprost (avoid in asthma); misoprostol
  • Bimanual compression
  • Surgical: D&C, B-Lynch suture, uterine artery ligation, hysterectomy if refractory
Patient Education

Teach self fundal massage before discharge (increases sense of control, decreases anxiety). Report a fundus that feels boggy, heavy bleeding (pad soaked <1 hr), or passing large clots after discharge — this is a late-PPH warning sign.

2

Genital Tract Lacerations

Firm uterus + continued bleeding — "Trauma"
▾
Pathophysiology

Soft tissue injury to cervix, vagina, or perineum during second stage. Unlike atony, the uterus itself remains firm and midline — bleeding is from a vascular tear, not a flaccid uterus.

Etiology

Rapid/precipitous labor, operative vaginal birth, episiotomy, macrosomia, nulliparity.

Signs & Symptoms
  • Firm, midline fundus with persistent bright red bleeding
  • Visible laceration on speculum exam
  • Steady trickle rather than atony's boggy gush
Risk Factors
  • Forceps/vacuum-assisted birth
  • Episiotomy
  • Precipitous birth, macrosomia
Complications
  • Ongoing hemorrhage
  • Hematoma formation
  • Hemodynamic instability
Labs / Diagnostics
  • Direct visual/speculum inspection
  • Hgb/Hct trend
🎯 Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Frequent VS
  • Notify provider promptly for repair
  • Monitor for evolving hematoma
Treatment
  • Suture repair
  • Direct pressure, ice
Patient Education

Perineal hygiene (front to back), ice then sitz baths for comfort, increase dietary fiber/fluids and use stool softeners to avoid straining sutures, report signs of wound infection.

3

Retained Placental Tissue / Subinvolution

Most common cause of LATE postpartum hemorrhage — "Tissue"
▾
Pathophysiology

Placental or membrane fragments remain in the uterus after birth, preventing the myometrium from fully contracting. Subinvolution = failure of the uterus to return to its prepregnant size and position.

Etiology

Incomplete placental separation, mismanaged third stage of labor, placenta accreta spectrum.

Signs & Symptoms
  • Boggy uterus
  • Prolonged or excessive lochia
  • Late PPH (24h–12 weeks postpartum)
  • Possible fever if infected
Risk Factors
  • Placenta accreta spectrum
  • Prior uterine surgery
  • Manual placental removal, multiparity
Complications
  • Late hemorrhage
  • Endometritis
  • Need for D&C or hysterectomy
Labs / Diagnostics
  • Pelvic ultrasound (visualizes retained tissue)
  • Trending Hgb/Hct
🎯 Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Monitor lochia amount/pattern each shift
  • Fundal height/tone assessment
  • Report return to lochia rubra after it has progressed — red flag
Treatment
  • Uterotonics
  • D&C / curettage
  • Antibiotics if infected
Patient Education

Call the provider immediately for heavy bleeding, foul odor, or lochia that reverts to bright red after already lightening — these signal retained tissue or infection, not a normal fluctuation.

4

Coagulation Disorders (DIC, vW Disease, ITP)

Bleeding without an obvious focal source — "Thrombin"
▾
Pathophysiology

DIC: widespread clotting-factor consumption causes simultaneous microclotting and uncontrolled hemorrhage. Von Willebrand disease: inherited factor VIII deficiency (autosomal dominant). ITP: autoimmune platelet destruction.

Etiology

DIC is secondary to abruption, amniotic fluid embolism, HELLP, sepsis, or fetal demise. vW disease and ITP are pre-existing hereditary/autoimmune conditions.

Signs & Symptoms
  • Oozing from IV sites/gums, petechiae, bruising
  • Bleeding that won't clot
  • Hemorrhage with no clear anatomic source
Risk Factors
  • Abruption, AFE, severe preeclampsia/HELLP
  • Sepsis, fetal demise
  • Family/personal history of bleeding disorder
Complications
  • Massive hemorrhage
  • Multi-organ failure
  • Death
Labs / Diagnostics
  • ↓ Platelets, ↓ fibrinogen
  • ↑ PT/PTT, ↑ D-dimer
  • ↓ Factor VIII (vW disease)
🎯 Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Continuous hemodynamic monitoring
  • Minimize venipuncture/trauma
  • Prepare for rapid blood product administration
Treatment
  • Treat underlying cause
  • Blood component therapy: FFP, cryoprecipitate, platelets
  • Desmopressin (vW disease), TXA
Patient Education

Acute management is inpatient-focused: explain the reason for frequent labs/monitoring. For women with a known hereditary bleeding disorder, counsel on long-term hematology follow-up and bleeding-risk awareness for up to 4 weeks postpartum.

5

Hematoma

Concealed bleeding — pain out of proportion is the hallmark
▾
Pathophysiology

Blood collects in perineal, vulvar, or vaginal soft tissue from an injured vessel without adequate external hemostasis — bleeding is trapped rather than visible.

Etiology

Vessel injury during laceration repair, episiotomy, or operative vaginal birth.

Signs & Symptoms
  • Severe, unremitting perineal/rectal pain out of proportion to exam (hallmark finding)
  • Discoloration, edema of perineum
  • Rectal pressure
  • Possible palpable bulging mass
Risk Factors
  • Genital tract laceration, episiotomy
  • Operative vaginal birth
  • Prolonged 2nd stage, nulliparity
Complications
  • Hypovolemic shock (concealed blood loss)
  • Infection
  • Need for surgical evacuation
Labs / Diagnostics
  • Clinical exam (visual + palpation)
  • Trending Hgb/Hct if hematoma is large
🎯 Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Treat pain complaints as a real finding, not "normal" postpartum soreness
  • Frequent VS
  • Notify provider promptly
Treatment
  • Ice x 12 hours, then sitz baths
  • Analgesics
  • Incision & drainage if >12 cm or symptomatic
Patient Education

Report unrelieved or worsening perineal pain, especially pain that escalates rather than improves with routine comfort measures, and signs of infection at any drainage site.

6

Uterine Inversion

Obstetric emergency — fundus not palpable abdominally
▾
Pathophysiology

The uterine fundus collapses into the uterine cavity and turns inside out — can be incomplete (palpable through the cervix), complete (mass in the vagina), or prolapsed (mass protrudes outside the introitus).

Etiology

Excessive umbilical cord traction with a fundally attached placenta, or fundal pressure applied to an atonic uterus.

Signs & Symptoms
  • Sudden hemorrhage, shock, and severe pain
  • Fundus not palpable abdominally — a depression is felt where the fundus should be
  • Large red mass may be visible at/outside the introitus
Risk Factors
  • Fundal placental implantation
  • Manual placental extraction
  • Short umbilical cord
  • Uterine atony, leiomyomas
Complications
  • Severe hemorrhagic shock
  • Death if not rapidly corrected
Labs / Diagnostics

Clinical/visual diagnosis — this is an emergency identified on exam, not by lab result.

🎯 Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Call for immediate help — obstetric emergency
  • Do not attempt aggressive fundal massage before repositioning
  • Prepare for fluid resuscitation and repositioning
Treatment
  • Tocolytics or halogenated anesthetics to relax the uterus before manual replacement
  • Oxytocics given after the uterus is repositioned
  • Broad-spectrum antibiotics; laparotomy if manual replacement fails
Patient Education

This is an acute inpatient emergency with no separate patient-teaching phase during the event; after stabilization, education focuses on the same PPH recovery and follow-up teaching as other hemorrhage causes.

7

Uterine Rupture

Rigid abdomen + absent FHR = surgical emergency
▾
Pathophysiology

Complete or incomplete tearing of the uterine wall from the stress of labor. Complete rupture creates direct communication between the uterine and peritoneal cavities; incomplete rupture stays contained within the peritoneum covering the uterus.

Etiology

Labor after a previous cesarean section, an overdistended uterus, or abdominal trauma.

Signs & Symptoms
  • Sudden abdominal pain/tenderness, possible chest pain
  • Contractions stop or fail to progress
  • Rigid abdomen
  • Absent fetal heart rate
  • Signs of maternal shock; fetus may be palpated outside the uterus (complete rupture)
Risk Factors
  • Labor after a previous cesarean section (VBAC)
  • Overdistended uterus (multiples, hydramnios)
  • Abdominal trauma
Complications
  • Fetal demise
  • Massive hemorrhage/shock
  • Hysterectomy
Labs / Diagnostics

Clinical presentation drives diagnosis; continuous FHR monitoring shows sudden loss of pattern.

🎯 Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Monitor for and treat signs of shock: O2, IV fluids, blood products
  • Prepare immediately for emergency cesarean birth
  • Provide emotional support to client and partner
Treatment
  • Emergency cesarean delivery
  • Possible hysterectomy if repair is not feasible
Patient Education

Post-event: counseling on the cause, implications for future pregnancies (repeat cesarean typically required), and emotional processing of an emergency birth experience.

8

Hemorrhagic (Hypovolemic) Shock

BP drop is a LATE sign — don't wait for it
▾
Pathophysiology

Any of the above hemorrhage causes can progress to inadequate organ perfusion. Compensatory catecholamine release constricts arterioles/venules in skin, lungs, GI tract, liver, and kidneys to protect the heart and brain — this is why skin and urine output change before blood pressure drops.

Etiology

Any uncontrolled postpartum hemorrhage source: atony, laceration, retained tissue, coagulopathy, inversion, or rupture.

Signs & Symptoms — Staged Assessment
  • Respirations: rapid and shallow
  • Pulse: rapid, weak, irregular
  • Blood pressure: decreasing — this is a LATE sign, don't wait for hypotension to act
  • Skin: cool, pale, clammy
  • Urinary output: decreasing
  • Level of consciousness / mental status: lethargy → coma; anxiety → coma
  • Central venous pressure: decreased
Risk Factors

Same as any PPH risk factor (see Uterine Atony, Lacerations, Retained Tissue, Coagulopathy cards above) — shock is the downstream consequence, not an independent condition.

Complications
  • Multi-organ failure
  • Death
Labs / Diagnostics
  • Continuous VS and O2 sat monitoring
  • Hgb/Hct, coags, type & crossmatch
🎯 Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Summon assistance/activate the hemorrhage protocol immediately
  • Two large-bore IV lines for fluid resuscitation
  • Ensure patent airway, administer oxygen
  • Continuous reassessment — don't wait for BP to drop to intervene
Treatment
  • Crystalloid infusion (3 mL infused per 1 mL estimated blood loss)
  • Packed RBCs if still actively bleeding after crystalloids
  • FFP if coagulation studies are abnormal
Patient Education

Post-stabilization: explain the reason for close monitoring and repeated labs; support with lactation consultants and mental health referral, as PPH survivors are at higher risk for delayed lactogenesis and postpartum depression.

Thromboembolic Complications

Pregnancy and the postpartum period are hypercoagulable states — venous stasis + vessel trauma from birth increase clot risk.

▾
1

Thrombophlebitis / DVT

Do NOT massage the affected leg
▾
Pathophysiology

Venous stasis plus the postpartum hypercoagulable state (and vessel wall injury from birth) allow clot formation, typically in a superficial or deep lower-extremity vein.

Etiology

Postpartum hypercoagulability, immobility, venous stasis, vascular trauma from birth.

Signs & Symptoms
  • Unilateral calf tenderness
  • Leg pain, swelling
  • Redness, warmth (heat)
Risk Factors
  • Cesarean birth, immobility, obesity
  • Prior VTE history, smoking
  • Advanced maternal age, prolonged labor
Complications
  • Pulmonary embolism
  • Chronic venous insufficiency
  • Recurrent VTE risk
Labs / Diagnostics
  • D-dimer
  • Doppler ultrasound (first-line)
  • VQ scan, MRI
🎯 Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Elevate affected extremity
  • Apply compression stockings
  • Never massage the affected leg
  • Monitor closely for signs of PE
Treatment
  • Anticoagulation: heparin acutely → oral anticoagulant
  • NSAIDs, moist warm packs
  • Rest, increased fluids
Patient Education

Safe anticoagulant practices (soft toothbrush, electric razor, avoid injury), dietary consistency with vitamin K–containing foods if on warfarin, follow-up coagulation labs, breastfeeding is safe with heparin/warfarin, and estrogen-containing contraceptives are contraindicated — needs a non-estrogen method. Report calf pain/swelling or any sudden chest pain/shortness of breath immediately.

2

Pulmonary Embolism

Medical emergency — sudden dyspnea + chest pain
▾
Pathophysiology

A dislodged venous thrombus travels to the pulmonary vasculature, obstructing blood flow and causing V/Q mismatch and right heart strain.

Etiology

Complication of an untreated or undetected DVT.

Signs & Symptoms
  • Sudden dyspnea, tachypnea
  • Chest pain, tachycardia
  • Anxiety/sense of impending doom
  • Hypoxia, possible hemoptysis
Risk Factors

Same as DVT: cesarean birth, immobility, obesity, prior VTE, smoking.

Complications
  • Cardiopulmonary arrest
  • Death
Labs / Diagnostics
  • CT pulmonary angiogram, VQ scan
  • ABGs (hypoxemia)
  • D-dimer
🎯 Priority Nursing Interventions
  • STAT provider notification / rapid response
  • High-Fowler's position, O2
  • Continuous cardiopulmonary monitoring, IV access
Treatment
  • Anticoagulation, thrombolytics if severe
  • Oxygen support, possible ICU care
Patient Education

Post-acute: same anticoagulant safety teaching as DVT, emphasis on recognizing recurrence symptoms, and counseling on VTE risk for future pregnancies and contraceptive choice.

Puerperal Infections

Any infection of the reproductive tract within 28 days of birth. Diagnostic definition: temp ≥100.4°F (38°C) on 2 successive days of the first 10 postpartum days, excluding the first 24 hours.

▾
1

Endometritis

Most common puerperal infection
▾
Pathophysiology

Infection begins at the placental implantation site and spreads across the endometrial lining; can extend to tubes, ovaries, or pelvic connective tissue.

Etiology

Ascending, often polymicrobial infection (streptococcal and anaerobic organisms).

Signs & Symptoms
  • Temp >100.4°F, tachycardia, chills
  • Pelvic pain, uterine tenderness
  • Foul-smelling, heavy lochia (or scant/odorless with strep)
  • Anorexia, malaise
Risk Factors
  • Cesarean birth (10–15% vs. 2% vaginal)
  • Prolonged labor/ROM, multiple vaginal exams
  • Retained placental fragments
  • Low socioeconomic status, poor nutrition
Complications
  • Sepsis
  • Pelvic abscess
  • Septic pelvic thrombophlebitis
Labs / Diagnostics
  • ↑ WBC (leukocytosis), ↑ ESR
  • Blood/uterine cultures (limited clinical utility)
🎯 Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Monitor fever curve/VS trend
  • Assess lochia amount, color, odor each shift
  • Initiate IV antibiotics promptly per order
Treatment
  • IV broad-spectrum antibiotics (clindamycin + gentamicin, cephalosporins)
  • Hydration, rest, analgesia
  • D/C antibiotics ~24h after afebrile/asymptomatic
Patient Education

Complete the full antibiotic course, perineal hygiene, signs of worsening infection to report, and support for maintaining breastfeeding/mother–infant interaction during treatment.

2

Wound Infection

Cesarean incision or perineal repair site
▾
Pathophysiology

Bacterial invasion of the cesarean incision or perineal repair site disrupts normal wound healing.

Etiology

Skin-flora contamination, poor hygiene, hematoma formation, impaired healing from obesity/diabetes.

Signs & Symptoms
  • Fever, erythema, edema, warmth
  • Pain, tenderness
  • Seropurulent drainage, wound separation/dehiscence
Risk Factors
  • Obesity, diabetes, hypertension, immunosuppression
  • Poor nutrition, anemia, hemorrhage
  • Prolonged labor/ROM, chorioamnionitis, hematoma
Complications
  • Cellulitis, abscess
  • Dehiscence
  • Rectovaginal fistula (with infected 3rd/4th degree laceration)
  • Necrotizing fasciitis (rare, severe)
Labs / Diagnostics
  • Wound culture
  • ↑ WBC
🎯 Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Wound assessment every shift
  • Temp/VS monitoring
  • Comfort measures: sitz baths, analgesics
Treatment
  • IV antibiotics
  • I&D if purulent; irrigation/packing
  • Healing by secondary intention; wound VAC in select cases
Patient Education

Hand hygiene, front-to-back perineal care, dressing-change technique, signs of worsening infection to report, and the importance of keeping follow-up wound-check appointments.

3

Urinary Tract Infection

Can be asymptomatic — screen proactively
▾
Pathophysiology

Bacterial colonization of the urinary tract, usually ascending from the urethra; postpartum bladder hypotonia and urinary retention raise risk.

Etiology

Catheterization, birth trauma, epidural effects blunting bladder sensation.

Signs & Symptoms
  • Can be asymptomatic
  • Dysuria, frequency, urgency
  • Flank pain, fever (suggests pyelonephritis)
  • Hematuria (catheter sample)
Risk Factors
  • Catheterization, multiple vaginal exams
  • Epidural/spinal anesthesia
  • Genital tract trauma, cesarean birth
  • History of UTI
Complications
  • Pyelonephritis
  • Urosepsis
  • Recurrent UTI
Labs / Diagnostics
  • Urinalysis
  • Urine culture & sensitivity (catheterized sample if needed)
🎯 Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Encourage voiding q2–4h
  • Monitor I&O
  • Assess for bladder distention (also worsens uterine atony)
Treatment
  • Antibiotics per culture/sensitivity
  • Increased fluid intake
Patient Education

Complete the full antibiotic course, stay well hydrated, maintain proper perineal hygiene, void promptly rather than holding urine, and report flank pain or fever right away — signs of pyelonephritis.

4

Mastitis

Continue breastfeeding on the affected side
▾
Pathophysiology

Infection/inflammation of the mammary ductal system, usually unilateral, arising from milk stasis plus bacterial entry through a nipple fissure or crack.

Etiology

Most commonly Staphylococcus aureus introduced from the infant's mouth via nipple trauma; MRSA is increasingly common.

Signs & Symptoms
  • Fever, chills, malaise, generalized aching
  • Unilateral warm, tender, hard/reddened area
  • Triangular flush pattern (early sign)
  • Enlarged axillary lymph nodes
  • Cracked/fissured nipple, possible purulent discharge
Risk Factors
  • Milk stasis, plugged duct
  • Infrequent/incomplete emptying
  • Nipple trauma, fatigue, primiparity
Complications
  • Breast abscess (especially MRSA)
  • Impaired breastfeeding
  • Recurrent mastitis
Labs / Diagnostics
  • Usually clinical diagnosis
  • Breast milk culture if not responding to first-line antibiotics
🎯 Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Encourage continued breastfeeding/pumping on the affected side
  • Frequent emptying q2–4h
  • Teach proper latch and positioning
Treatment
  • Antibiotics compatible with breastfeeding (cephalosporins; trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole/clindamycin/rifampin if MRSA)
  • I&D if abscess forms
  • Heat/cold for comfort
Patient Education

Complete the full antibiotic course even after feeling better, avoid breast creams that block Montgomery gland secretion, practice good hand hygiene, start feeds on the affected breast, vary infant positioning to empty all ducts, change damp breast pads promptly. MRSA is not transmitted through milk — breastfeeding may continue, but avoid direct contact with any open lesion.

Postpartum Psychiatric Complications

A spectrum, not three unrelated conditions — severity and safety risk escalate sharply from blues to psychosis.

▾
Progression: Blues blows over (self-limiting, <2 wks) → Depression drags on (>2 wks, impairs functioning) → Psychosis is a panic-button emergency (hospitalize now)
1

Postpartum Blues

~70% of postpartum women (course slide figure; texts cite 50–80%) — self-limiting
▾
Pathophysiology

Transient mood lability attributed to the abrupt postpartum withdrawal of estrogen and progesterone, compounded by fatigue and adjustment stress. Not a psychiatric disorder.

Etiology

Hormonal withdrawal, fatigue, role adjustment.

Signs & Symptoms
  • Tearfulness, mood swings, anxiety
  • Fatigue, sadness, insomnia
  • Forgetfulness — functioning remains intact
Risk Factors

Nearly universal; not linked to psychiatric history specifically.

Complications

Usually none; can progress to PPD if it persists beyond 2 weeks or worsens.

Labs / Diagnostics

None required — clinical/EPDS screening used to differentiate from PPD.

🎯 Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Reassurance, therapeutic listening
  • Encourage extra rest
  • Monitor for progression beyond 2 weeks
Treatment

No medical treatment typically needed — supportive care only.

Patient Education

Normalize the experience as common and self-limiting. Teach the warning signs that indicate progression to postpartum depression and when to seek help.

2

Postpartum Depression

Lasts >2 weeks — screen with EPDS
▾
Pathophysiology

Multifactorial — neuroendocrine shifts interacting with genetic/psychiatric vulnerability and psychosocial stress.

Etiology

Strongest risk factor: personal/family history of psychiatric disorder (up to 70% recurrence). Possible contributors: vitamin D/B12/folate/omega-3 deficiency, thyroid disease (must be ruled out).

Signs & Symptoms
  • Pervasive sadness, labile mood, lasts >2 weeks
  • Fatigue, sleep/appetite disturbance, irritability, anxiety
  • Detachment/guilt toward infant
  • Intrusive thoughts of harm without acting on them
Risk Factors
  • Prior/family psychiatric history
  • Abuse/IPV, lack of social support
  • Low SES, financial hardship, unmarried status
  • Age <24 or >35, unintended pregnancy
  • Pregnancy complications (preeclampsia, cesarean, preterm birth)
Complications
  • Impaired maternal–infant bonding/attachment
  • Chronic depression
  • Marital/family strain
  • Risk of self-harm in severe cases
Labs / Diagnostics
  • EPDS (Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale) screening
  • Rule out thyroid disease and bipolar disorder
🎯 Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Screen with EPDS
  • Ask directly about safety/suicidal or harm ideation
  • Nonjudgmental listening; involve support system
Treatment
  • SSRIs/SNRIs, therapy/counseling
  • Social work/psych referral
  • Treat comorbid thyroid disease if present
Patient Education

PPD is common and treatable — not a reflection of being a "bad mother." Most SSRIs are compatible with breastfeeding (fluoxetine flagged by the FDA as not recommended for nursing mothers). Emphasize follow-up and involving partner/family in recognizing symptoms.

3

Postpartum Psychosis

Psychiatric EMERGENCY — hospitalize
▾
Pathophysiology

Acute psychiatric emergency most commonly associated with underlying bipolar disorder; presents with mania, depression, or mixed features accompanied by psychosis.

Etiology

Strongest association: pre-existing bipolar disorder or psychosis. Rapid postpartum hormonal shifts are thought to trigger an episode in a vulnerable individual.

Signs & Symptoms
  • Acute onset, classically days 3–6 postpartum, within first 6 months
  • Auditory/visual hallucinations (may command harm to infant)
  • Delusions, often about the infant
  • Disorientation, confusion, extreme impulsivity, rapid mood swings
  • May initially present as fatigue, insomnia, tearfulness before progressing
Risk Factors
  • Pre-existing bipolar disorder or psychosis
  • Primiparity
  • Family history
Complications
  • Suicide
  • Infanticide
  • Highest-acuity postpartum psychiatric emergency
Labs / Diagnostics
  • Emergent psychiatric evaluation
  • Rule out organic causes (thyroid, infection, substance-induced)
  • EPDS as an adjunct only
🎯 Priority Nursing Interventions
  • Ensure immediate safety — never leave mother and infant alone together unsupervised
  • Do not leave the patient alone
  • Obtain emergent psych consult; hospitalize
Treatment
  • Psychiatric hospitalization
  • Mood stabilizers (e.g., lithium), antipsychotics
  • Close inpatient monitoring
Patient Education

To family, post-crisis: this is a medical emergency requiring treatment, not a moral failing. Discuss long-term pharmacotherapy needs, safety planning, and the elevated risk of recurrence in future pregnancies.

Normal Postpartum Parameters — Quick Reference

Know these exact numbers cold
▾
Temperature

May rise to 100.4°F (38.0°C) in the first 24h from dehydration of labor — not automatically infection. Any higher, or persisting past 24h, must be reported.

Pulse

Normal puerperal bradycardia: can drop to 50 bpm and still be normal. Pulse >100 bpm may indicate excessive blood loss or infection.

Blood Pressure

Should remain at the woman's normal baseline. A decrease suggests hypovolemia — remember, BP drop is a late sign of hemorrhage.

Respirations

Rarely change. A significant increase should raise suspicion for pulmonary embolism, uterine atony, or hemorrhage.

Lochia Amount Classification (per pad in 1 hour)

Scant: <1 inch (2.5 cm) stain  |  Light: <4 inches (10 cm)  |  Moderate: <6 inches (15 cm)  |  Heavy: pad saturated in 1 hour  |  Excessive: pad saturated in 15 minutes — report immediately.

1

Fundus & Uterine Involution

The "B" (Breast) is separate below — this is uterine tone
▾
Nurse Assesses

Fundal height relative to umbilicus, consistency (firm vs. boggy), position (midline vs. deviated).

Normal / Expected

Firm, midline; at the umbilicus on day 1, descending about 1 fingerbreadth (1 cm)/day, not palpable by ~2 weeks.

Patient Teaching

How to palpate her own fundus and massage technique; empty the bladder before assessment since a full bladder displaces the uterus.

🚩 Report to Provider

Boggy fundus unresponsive to massage, fundus above the umbilicus after day 1, or deviation to one side.

Priority Nursing Actions

Assess fundus with every vital sign check in the early postpartum period; correlate findings with lochia amount.

Special Considerations

After cesarean birth, palpate gently near the incision and consider timing assessment with analgesia.

2

Lochia

Rubra → Serosa → Alba
▾
Nurse Assesses

Amount, color, odor, consistency, and progression pattern.

Normal / Expected

Rubra (days 1–3, red) → Serosa (days 4–10, pink-brown) → Alba (day 10–14+, white/yellow); fleshy, non-offensive odor.

Patient Teaching

Expected progression pattern, pad counting, use peri-pads (not tampons).

🚩 Report to Provider

Foul odor, return to rubra after progressing, saturating a pad in under an hour, or passing large clots.

Priority Nursing Actions

Quantify lochia (weigh pads if concerned) and correlate with fundal tone.

Special Considerations

Lochia still occurs after cesarean birth — the uterine lining sheds regardless of birth route.

Hygiene & Incision Care

▾

Perineum or cesarean incision using REEDA; presence of drainage or separation.

Normal / Expected

Well-approximated edges, minimal edema/bruising, no purulent drainage.

Peri-bottle front-to-back, frequent pad changes, hand hygiene; ice first 24h then sitz baths for perineal comfort; keep cesarean incision clean and dry.

Increasing redness/warmth, foul drainage, wound separation, or fever.

Inspect the perineum or incision every shift.

1

Fertility Awareness Methods & Withdrawal

Nonhormonal, natural family planning
▾
Mechanism

Avoidance of intercourse during the fertile window, identified through cycle tracking (calendar rhythm, BBT, cervical mucus, symptothermal) or withdrawal before ejaculation.

How It's Used

Daily monitoring/charting; no devices or hormones required.

Advantages

No cost, no hormones, acceptable for religious/personal preference, builds body awareness.

Disadvantages / Risks

High user-error failure rate, requires consistent daily tracking, no STI protection, less reliable postpartum while cycles are still irregular.

Contraindications / Precautions

Irregular menstrual cycles make the fertile window harder to predict; postpartum and lactational cycles are especially unpredictable.

🎯 Patient Teaching

Chart BBT daily before rising; note cervical mucus changes (thin, clear, stretchy = fertile); abstain or use a backup method during the fertile window. Counsel patients to wait until regular cycles resume postpartum before relying on these methods alone.

2

Barrier Methods

Condom, diaphragm, cervical cap, sponge, spermicide
▾
Mechanism

Physical or chemical block preventing sperm from reaching the cervix.

How It's Used

Condom with each act of intercourse; diaphragm/cervical cap fitted by a provider, used with spermicide, inserted before intercourse; sponge is OTC, moistened before insertion, effective up to 24 hours.

Advantages

OTC availability (condom, sponge), no hormones, condoms are the only method besides abstinence that also protects against STIs.

Disadvantages / Risks

User-dependent and can interrupt spontaneity; diaphragm/cap often need refitting after childbirth.

Contraindications / Precautions

Latex allergy (non-latex options available); risk of toxic shock syndrome (TSS) with prolonged sponge or diaphragm wear.

🎯 Patient Teaching

Diaphragm/cervical cap must be refit postpartum due to cervical and vaginal changes from birth. Sponge: leave in place at least 6 hours after intercourse, remove by 24–30 hours — longer wear raises TSS risk. Combine spermicide with other barrier methods for adequate efficacy.

3

Combined Hormonal (COC / Patch / Ring)

Estrogen + progestin — not first-line while breastfeeding
▾
Mechanism

Estrogen + progestin suppress FSH/LH to inhibit ovulation; thicken cervical mucus; alter the endometrium.

How It's Used

COC: oral, daily, same time each day. Patch: applied weekly x 3 weeks, then 1 week off. Ring: worn 3 weeks, out 1 week.

Advantages

Highly effective when used correctly; regulates the cycle; reduces menstrual flow and cramping.

Disadvantages / Risks

Requires daily/weekly adherence; no STI protection; estrogen can reduce milk supply.

Contraindications / Precautions

<6 weeks postpartum (especially if breastfeeding), history of VTE/thromboembolic disorder, smoker >35 y/o, migraine with aura, HTN ≥160/100, breast cancer, diabetes >20 years with vascular disease.

🎯 Patient Teaching

Teach the ACHES warning signs — stop the method and call the provider. Effectiveness is reduced by certain anticonvulsants, rifampin, and some antifungals. Not recommended for breastfeeding mothers due to the risk of reduced milk supply — progestin-only methods are preferred while nursing.

4

Progestin-Only (Mini-pill, DMPA, Nexplanon)

Preferred while breastfeeding
▾
Mechanism

Thickens cervical mucus, thins the endometrium, and inhibits some (not all) ovulatory cycles.

How It's Used

Mini-pill: oral, same time daily, within a strict 3-hour window. DMPA: IM/SQ injection every 11–13 weeks. Nexplanon: subdermal implant, effective up to 3 years.

Advantages

Safe during lactation (no estrogen); implant can be inserted immediately postpartum; long-acting options (DMPA, implant) reduce user error.

Disadvantages / Risks

Irregular bleeding is common; mini-pill is less effective than COCs and requires strict timing; DMPA is linked to bone density loss with prolonged use and delayed return to fertility (up to 18 months).

Contraindications / Precautions

DMPA: do not massage the injection site — this hastens absorption and shortens the effective window.

🎯 Patient Teaching

Mini-pill must be taken at the same time daily — more than 3 hours late requires a backup method for 48 hours. DMPA is due every 11–13 weeks; counsel on calcium intake and weight-bearing exercise for bone health. Nexplanon does not affect milk supply and can be placed before discharge.

5

Intrauterine Devices (Copper & Levonorgestrel)

Long-acting reversible contraception (LARC)
▾
Mechanism

Copper IUD: spermicidal/toxic to sperm, nonhormonal. Levonorgestrel IUD: thickens cervical mucus and thins the endometrium.

How It's Used

Inserted by a provider. Copper IUD effective up to 10 years; levonorgestrel IUD per your course materials is listed as effective for 1 year — confirm this figure with Dr. Morris, as other references cite longer durations.

Advantages

Long-acting, reversible, highly effective; the copper IUD doubles as emergency contraception.

Disadvantages / Risks

Requires provider insertion/removal; possible cramping or spotting initially; risk of expulsion.

Contraindications / Precautions

Active pelvic infection, undiagnosed abnormal vaginal bleeding.

🎯 Patient Teaching

Teach the patient to check IUD strings periodically. Report severe pain, fever, or abnormal discharge — signs of infection or expulsion.

6

Emergency Contraception

Copper IUD is the most effective option
▾
Mechanism

Delays or prevents ovulation; does not disrupt an already-implanted pregnancy.

How It's Used

Levonorgestrel (Plan B): within 72h (some efficacy to 120h). Ulipristal (Ella): up to 120h, prescription only. Copper IUD insertion: within 120h — most effective option and provides ongoing contraception.

Advantages

LNG available OTC (≥17 y/o with ID); copper IUD adds years of ongoing contraception.

Disadvantages / Risks

Less effective than routine contraception; nausea (especially with the Yuzpe combined-pill regimen); pill-based methods don't protect future acts of intercourse.

Contraindications / Precautions

No medical contraindications except confirmed pregnancy and undiagnosed abnormal bleeding.

🎯 Patient Teaching

Take as soon as possible after unprotected intercourse. If no period within 21 days, evaluate for pregnancy. Ulipristal is more effective than levonorgestrel in obese women; copper IUD effectiveness is unaffected by body weight/BMI.

7

Sterilization

Considered permanent
▾
Mechanism

Permanent surgical interruption of the fallopian tubes (tubal ligation) or vas deferens (vasectomy).

How It's Used

Tubal ligation is often done postpartum (within 24–48h of birth) or as an interval procedure; vasectomy is an outpatient male procedure.

Advantages

Permanent, highly effective, one-time procedure — no ongoing user action needed.

Disadvantages / Risks

Considered irreversible (reconstruction/reanastomosis is possible but not guaranteed to restore fertility); no STI protection.

Contraindications / Precautions

Should only be pursued with certainty about future fertility desires; requires full informed consent.

🎯 Patient Teaching

Discuss permanence thoroughly before consent. Vasectomy is not immediately effective — a backup method is needed until a semen analysis confirms sterility.

8

Abortion

Perry Ch. 5 — related reproductive planning content
▾
Mechanism

Purposeful interruption of pregnancy before 20 weeks gestation.

How It's Used

First-trimester: surgical (aspiration/suction curettage) or medical (mifepristone + misoprostol, or methotrexate + misoprostol). Second-trimester: dilation and evacuation (D&E) with cervical preparation using prostaglandins.

Advantages

N/A — this is a medical option rather than a contraceptive method.

Disadvantages / Risks

Bleeding, infection, and risk of incomplete evacuation are possible with any method.

Contraindications / Precautions

Gestational age limits and legal availability vary by method and by state.

🎯 Patient Teaching

Provide nonjudgmental, holistic care regardless of the reason for the procedure (CSLO 1). Address emotional considerations directly and ensure a clear follow-up care plan.

Uterotonics — Postpartum Hemorrhage

Oxytocin first-line, then escalate
▾
Oxytocin (Pitocin)

10–40 units IV in 500–1,000 mL crystalloid, or 10–20 units IM. Contraindication: rare hypersensitivity. Never give undiluted IV bolus — causes hypotension/arrhythmias. Watch for water intoxication.

Methylergonovine (Methergine)

0.2 mg IM q2–4h, up to 5 doses. Contraindicated in HTN, preeclampsia, cardiovascular disease. Check BP before every dose. Keep refrigerated; never mix in syringe with other drugs.

Carboprost (Hemabate)

250 mcg IM or intrauterine q15–90 min, max 8 doses. Contraindicated in asthma. Don't give if patient is in shock — poor absorption.

Misoprostol (Cytotec)

600–1,000 mcg rectal, sublingual, or oral. Contraindication: hypersensitivity to prostaglandins. Stable at room temperature.

Dinoprostone (Prostin E2)

20 mg vaginal/rectal suppository q2h. Contraindicated in hypotension.

Tranexamic Acid (TXA)

1 g slow IV over 10 minutes. Contraindicated with history/risk of thromboembolic disease. Used when blood loss ≥1,500 mL or hemorrhage is refractory to other uterotonics; monitor for thromboembolic events.

2

Anticoagulants — DVT / PE

Antidote for heparin = protamine sulfate
▾
Unfractionated Heparin (UFH)

IV bolus 5,000 units, then continuous infusion titrated to aPTT (1.5–2.5x control). Contraindicated with active bleeding/HIT. Antidote: protamine sulfate.

LMWH — Enoxaparin (Lovenox), Dalteparin

Enoxaparin 1 mg/kg SQ BID or 1.5 mg/kg once daily. Self-injectable at home; monitor platelets/anti-Xa levels; teach injection site rotation.

Warfarin (Coumadin)

PO, dosed to INR (therapeutic range 2.0–3.5). Contraindicated in pregnancy (Category X) — teratogenic, needs reliable non-estrogen contraception. Overlaps with heparin until therapeutic. Teach vitamin K–consistent diet, soft toothbrush/electric razor, avoid NSAIDs/aspirin. Safe in breastfeeding.

Antibiotics — Postpartum Infections

Matched to the organism and site
▾
Endometritis

IV clindamycin + gentamicin; cephalosporins/penicillins as alternatives. Continue 24h after afebrile/asymptomatic.

Chorioamnionitis / Post-Cesarean Coverage

Ampicillin (or penicillin) + gentamicin; add clindamycin or metronidazole (Flagyl) for anaerobic coverage after cesarean.

Pyelonephritis

IV ceftriaxone (Rocephin), or ampicillin + gentamicin. Outpatient completion: amoxicillin-clavulanate (Augmentin) or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (Bactrim DS) for a 14-day course.

Mastitis

Dicloxacillin (Dynapen), cephalexin (Keflex), or clindamycin (Cleocin). Continue breastfeeding on the affected side; complete the full course.

MRSA Mastitis

Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, clindamycin, or rifampin if no response to first-line therapy. May need I&D of abscess + breast milk culture. MRSA is not transmitted through milk — avoid contact with any open lesion only.

Psychotropics — PPD & Psychosis

Sertraline/paroxetine preferred while breastfeeding
▾
SSRIs (1st-line for PPD)

Sertraline, escitalopram, fluoxetine, fluvoxamine, paroxetine, citalopram. Sertraline & paroxetine preferred (lowest breast milk concentration). FDA: fluoxetine not recommended for nursing mothers.

SNRIs

Venlafaxine, duloxetine, desvenlafaxine. Detected in most infant plasma but no proven adverse effects.

TCAs

Amitriptyline, nortriptyline, imipramine. Useful if sleep disturbance is prominent.

Lithium

Drug of choice for postpartum psychosis, often with underlying bipolar disorder. Present in breast milk — monitor infant for hypotonia, lethargy, feeding problems.

Antipsychotics

Quetiapine; chlorpromazine, thioridazine, trifluoperazine in acute psychosis. May cause infant sedation via milk.

Benzodiazepines

Short-acting preferred: lorazepam (Ativan), alprazolam (Xanax) — over longer-acting diazepam (Valium)/clonazepam (Klonopin).

Contraceptive Medications

▾
Combined Hormonal

Estrogen + progestin (COC, patch = Ortho Evra, ring = NuvaRing). Contraindicated with VTE history, smokers >35, migraine with aura. Reduces milk supply — avoid while breastfeeding.

Progestin-Only

Mini-pill; DMPA (Depo-Provera); Nexplanon (etonogestrel implant). Safe during lactation. DMPA — do not massage the injection site.

Emergency Contraception

Levonorgestrel (Plan B) within 72h; ulipristal (Ella) within 120h; combined estrogen-progestin (Yuzpe); copper IUD within 120h (most effective, unaffected by weight).

Abortion Medications

Mifepristone + misoprostol, or methotrexate + misoprostol (1st-trimester medical); dilation & evacuation with prostaglandin cervical prep (2nd trimester).
