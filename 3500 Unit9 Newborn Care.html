Factors Initiating the First Breath
▸
Mnemonic: "CaMeTS breathes" — Cahemical, Mechanical, Thermal, Sensory
  • Chemical: Labor contractions → transient fetal hypoxia/hypercarbia → cumulative ↓PO2, ↑PCO2, ↓pH → stimulates medulla respiratory center. Cord clamping also drops an inhibitory prostaglandin.
  • Mechanical: Chest compression through birth canal, then release → negative intrathoracic pressure draws air in. Crying creates positive pressure keeping alveoli open.
  • Thermal: Drop in environmental temperature stimulates skin thermoreceptors → respiratory center.
  • Sensory: Handling, suctioning, drying, light/sound/smell stimulate the respiratory center.
Key application pointScheduled cesarean birth (no labor) skips the pre-labor catecholamine surge that normally clears lung fluid → increased risk for Transient Tachypnea of the Newborn (TTN).

Surfactant & Lung Maturity
▸
Mechanism

Type II alveolar cells produce surfactant, which lowers surface tension and prevents alveolar collapse on exhalation, maintaining lung compliance.

Maturity marker

L/S (lecithin/sphingomyelin) ratio of 2:1 indicates mature lungs, typically by 35 weeks gestation.

Three Stages of Transition
▸
Overview & significance

The major adaptations from intrauterine to extrauterine life occur during the first 6-8 hours after birth, following a predictable sequence first described by Desmond, Rudolph, and Phitaksphraiwan (1966) that is still considered valid today. This sequence is mediated by the sympathetic nervous system and produces changes in heart rate, respirations, temperature, and GI function. This entire window represents a period of vulnerability — nurses must know what's normal at each stage in order to recognize what's abnormal and warrants intervention.

Clinical/teaching application
  1. Use the 1st period of reactivity as the priority window to initiate breastfeeding — the infant is alert and showing feeding-readiness cues (rooting, sucking) right when a mother is often also most alert following delivery.
  2. Do NOT interpret the period of decreased responsiveness as lethargy or a concerning finding — a normal, difficult-to-arouse sleep state at this specific timepoint is expected, not a red flag on its own.
  3. During the 2nd period of reactivity, anticipate and watch for meconium passage and transient vital sign swings; use this window to complete any remaining assessments while the infant is naturally more alert again.
  4. Remember that a very preterm infant will not show a typical 2nd period of reactivity — its absence in an extremely preterm infant is expected due to immaturity, not itself a warning sign, but should be interpreted in the context of the infant's overall gestational age.
Behavioral states underlying these stages

Newborn behavior fluctuates through six recognized states, and understanding them helps explain what's happening during each transition stage:

  • Deep/quiet sleep: eyes closed, no eye movement, regular/even breathing; difficult to arouse, delayed response to stimuli
  • Active REM sleep: irregular respirations, REM visible through closed eyelids, irregular sucking motions; minimal activity but stimuli can trigger a startle/state change
  • Drowsy: transitional between sleep and awake; fluttering eyelids, semi-dozing appearance, slow extremity movement; delayed response to stimuli
  • Quiet alert: infant fixates/follows faces or objects, intense gaze — the optimal time to feed and to promote bonding
  • Active alert: eyes open but less fixated, intense motor activity, thrusting limb movements; environmental stimuli increase activity further
  • Crying: often follows active alert when needs go unmet; jerky motor movements with intense, continuous crying

Expected heart rate/sounds
  • Term newborn HR: 120-160 bpm awake; can be 80-100 in deep sleep, up to 180 with crying
  • HR persistently >160 or <100 should be reevaluated within 30-60 min or with a change in infant activity
  • PMI (point of maximal impulse) at the 4th intercostal space, left of midclavicular line — often visible given thin chest wall
  • A soft murmur at the left sternal border, 2nd intercostal space, in the first 72 hours can reflect a not-yet-fully-closed ductus arteriosus — not automatically pathologic, but should be documented and monitored
  • Irregular heart rate/sinus dysrhythmia is common in the first few hours but should be evaluated if it persists
Notify provider if
  • Decreased strength of femoral pulses compared to brachial pulses (possible coarctation of the aorta)
  • Capillary refill >3 seconds (possible peripheral shunting/poor perfusion)
  • Central cyanosis (not acrocyanosis) at any point
  • A new or persistent murmur beyond the expected early-transition window

Respiratory Assessment (Priority #1)
▸
Why this comes firstRespiratory assessment is the literal first priority item on the CPE checklist and the first NCJMM "Recognize Cues" step for any newborn — airway/breathing is assessed before anything else in every newborn encounter, consistent with the priority-ranking logic used throughout this guide.
Assessment sequence & technique
  • With the infant calm/at rest (ideally before other exam steps that might agitate the infant), observe respiratory rate, rhythm, and symmetry of chest rise and fall for a full 60 seconds — count the full minute given normal newborn irregularity.
  • Note whether breathing is abdominal in character (normal — newborn respiration is largely diaphragmatic) with the chest and abdomen rising together, not in a seesaw pattern.
  • Auscultate anterior and posterior lung fields, moving the stethoscope systematically side-to-side and top-to-bottom for side-to-side comparison. The actual respiratory rate should be counted from auscultation, not just from watching abdominal movement.
  • Auscultate the nose separately to help distinguish upper airway congestion (mucus/amniotic fluid noise) from true lower airway congestion.
  • Determine the need for a bulb syringe based on excessive oral/nasal secretions, confirming nasal patency bilaterally (occlude one naris at a time with mouth closed; chest should still rise, confirming the other side is patent).

Signs of respiratory distress — the full list
  • Nasal flaring — widening of the nostrils with each breath, increasing airflow
  • Retractions — in-drawing of soft tissue as the infant works harder to breathe. Location matters: suprasternal or subclavicular retractions with stridor/gasping suggest upper airway obstruction, while intercostal/subcostal retractions are more general distress signs. Retraction depth correlates with severity — deeper retractions mean worsening disease, since newborn chest wall compliance/immaturity means any real respiratory effort shows up as visible retraction
  • Grunting on expiration
  • Central cyanosis (not just acrocyanosis)
  • Seesaw/paradoxic respirations
  • Use of accessory muscles
  • Lethargy or excessive sleepiness — a subtler sign parents should also be taught to recognize
The Silverman-Andersen Index

A structured tool that scores 5 components of respiratory effort — upper chest movement, lower chest (retractions), xiphoid retractions, nasal flaring, and expiratory grunt — each rated 0 (none), 1 (mild/just visible or audible with stethoscope only), or 2 (marked/audible to naked ear). Higher total scores indicate more severe respiratory distress. This gives you an objective, reproducible way to trend whether an infant's respiratory status is improving or worsening over time, rather than relying on a single subjective impression.

Priority nursing actions if distress is identified
  1. If bulb syringe suctioning clears the secretions and retractions resolve afterward — this confirms upper airway congestion was the cause and no further action is needed beyond continued observation.
  2. If retractions and distress signs persist despite suctioning, this is a life-threatening event requiring immediate investigation — do not simply re-suction repeatedly and wait.
  3. Correlate with skin color and capillary refill — both provide additional information about oxygenation status alongside the respiratory exam itself.
  4. Continue cardiorespiratory monitoring and reassess trend, not just a single point-in-time finding.
Notify provider ifRR persistently >60-70/min at rest; any true apneic episode >20 seconds; nasal flaring, grunting, or retractions that do not resolve with bulb suctioning; stridor; central cyanosis; seesaw respirations; absent or markedly diminished breath sounds on one side.
Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationYour baby's breathing may be a little irregular at first, with brief pauses of a few seconds — that's normal. But call us or come in if you notice faster-than-normal breathing, pauses longer than 15 seconds, the chest and belly moving in opposite directions (like a seesaw), flaring nostrils, grunting sounds, or if your baby seems unusually sleepy or hard to wake.
Other Caregiver EducationKnow what normal newborn breathing looks like (irregular, with brief pauses) versus true trouble breathing (fast, labored, grunting, flaring, or bluish color). If you see the concerning signs, contact the parents and seek care right away rather than waiting to see if it resolves.

APGAR Scoring
▸
Purpose & what it does NOT tell you

The Apgar score summarizes the newborn's transition to extrauterine life at fixed time points, based on five physiologic/behavioral signs. It does not predict long-term neurologic outcome — it is a snapshot of immediate status and response to resuscitation, not a prognosis tool. This is a frequently tested distinction.

Timing
  • Scored at 1 minute and 5 minutes after birth
  • If the 5-minute score is <7, repeat scoring every 5 minutes for up to 20 minutes total
  • If resuscitation is needed, it is initiated before the 1-minute score is even calculated — scoring never delays intervention

Each of the five categories is scored 0, 1, or 2, for a maximum total of 10.

New Ballard Score — Gestational Age Assessment
▸
Purpose & overview

The New Ballard Score is the most commonly used tool for determining a newborn's gestational age by physical examination — useful both to confirm dating from the last menstrual period/ultrasound and as the primary method when gestational age is otherwise unknown. It can assess gestational ages as young as 20 weeks. The exam yields two subscores — neuromuscular maturity and physical maturity — each covering six characteristics, for 12 total scored items.

Timing
  • Best performed within the first 2 hours of life per Perry Ch. 23 nursing guidance; more broadly, experts recommend the initial exam within the first 48 hours of life for accuracy
  • For infants ≤26 weeks gestation: exam should occur at <12 hours of postnatal age
  • For infants ≥26 weeks gestation: exam can be performed up to 96 hours after birth
  • The scoring system is most accurate when performed on term infants between 10 and 36 hours of life
  • Extremely immature neonates may need a follow-up exam since neuromuscular tone adjusts after birth
How scoring works

Each of the 12 characteristics is scored individually (most range from -1 or -2 up through 4 or 5, depending on the characteristic). The 12 scores are summed into a single total, which is then plotted on a maturity rating scale to yield the estimated gestational age in weeks. Half-scores are recorded when a characteristic falls between two options. The order in which items are assessed does not matter.

Key conceptual pointMaturity does not have to progress uniformly across categories — an infant can score full maturity (e.g., a 4) on one item without every other item also scoring a 4. The infant's total maturity score does not directly equal gestational age in weeks; it must be extrapolated using the maturity rating scale.

How the nurse uses this in practice
  1. Perform the assessment within the recommended timing window for the infant's apparent gestational age — earlier for suspected extreme prematurity.
  2. Score all 12 items (6 neuromuscular + 6 physical), using half-scores where a finding falls between two options.
  3. Sum the total score and plot it on the maturity rating scale to obtain the estimated gestational age in weeks.
  4. Compare this exam-derived gestational age against the dating from LMP/ultrasound — a significant discrepancy should be reported, since it affects risk stratification for conditions covered throughout this guide (RDS, hypoglycemia, hyperbilirubinemia, etc. in the High-Risk Unit 10 guide).
Exam pearl: Ballard = Neuromuscular (posture, square window, arm recoil, popliteal angle, scarf sign, heel-to-ear) + Physical (skin, lanugo, plantar surface, breast, eye/ear, genitals). Twelve items, two categories, one combined score extrapolated to weeks.
Growth Classification: AGA, SGA, LGA
▸
How classification works

Separate from the Ballard gestational-age exam, the newborn's weight is plotted against gestational age on a standardized intrauterine growth chart to classify growth status. This is a second, independent axis of risk classification (see Unit 10 for the full discussion of why both gestational age AND growth status matter together).

  • AGA (Appropriate for Gestational Age): weight falls between the 10th and 90th percentile for gestational age
  • SGA (Small for Gestational Age): weight falls below the 10th percentile for gestational age
  • LGA (Large for Gestational Age): weight falls above the 90th percentile for gestational age
Example reference range (40 weeks gestation)

The 10th-90th percentile range at 40 weeks is approximately 2700-3850 g (6-8.5 lb) for females and 2800-4000 g (6.2-8.8 lb) for males — illustrating that percentile cutoffs are gestational-age- and sex-specific, not a single fixed number.

Other methods to estimate/confirm gestational age

Fundal height measurement before birth, ultrasonography, eye lens vascularity, and calculation from the mother's last normal menstrual period all supplement or cross-check the Ballard exam findings.

Blood pressure — full technique and trend (not routinely measured in a healthy term infant)

BP is not routinely assessed in a normal, low-risk term newborn with no signs for concern — it becomes a priority measurement specifically for high-risk infants (where it's an indicator of fluid status, cardiovascular perfusion, endocrine function, and overall disease severity, e.g., sepsis) or when a murmur is auscultated.

  • Cuff selection: width should be approximately 0.5 (50%) of the extremity's circumference, or a width-to-limb ratio of 0.45-0.70; many neonatal cuffs print the correct circumference range directly on the cuff. A cuff that's too narrow gives a falsely HIGH reading; too wide gives a falsely LOW reading.
  • Site: right arm is standard unless contraindicated; the calf may be used in the first few days of life. Never place the cuff over an IV site, broken skin, or a joint.
  • Technique: take the reading while the infant is at rest, ideally asleep or calmed with a pacifier — activity and crying can raise readings by 10-20 mmHg. An oscillometric monitor calibrated specifically for neonatal pressures is preferred over manual auscultation.
  • Normal expected trend over time: BP is usually highest immediately after birth, decreases over the next 3 hours (a drop of roughly 15 mmHg systolic in the first hour is common), then rises steadily until reaching a plateau around 4-6 days after birth.

A wide pulse pressure can indicate a patent ductus arteriosus, AV malformation, or truncus arteriosus; a narrowed pulse pressure can indicate peripheral vasoconstriction or cardiac failure. As a rule of thumb, mean arterial pressure (MAP) should roughly equal the infant's gestational age in weeks (e.g., a 40-week infant should have a MAP of at least 40).
Safety alertRectal temperatures should NOT be routinely performed on a newborn due to perforation risk and vagal stimulation. Temporal and tympanic thermometers are not effective for newborns — axillary is the site of choice.
Additional expected findings
  • Sleep: 16-18 hrs/day; approximately 50% REM sleep
  • Heart sounds: S1 louder/duller than S2 (sharp); S3/S4 not audible in newborns. A soft murmur in the first few days is common and usually resolves by 6 months — but any murmur WITH poor feeding, apnea, cyanosis, or pallor is abnormal and requires investigation
  • Peripheral pulses: should be equal and strong bilaterally (brachial, femoral); weak/absent or unequal pulses suggest decreased cardiac output, thrombus, or coarctation of the aorta

HEENT Assessment (Head/Eyes/Ears/Nose/Throat)
▸
Facial symmetry

Face should appear rounded and symmetric at rest AND during crying. Assess movement specifically while crying, since this is when subtle asymmetry becomes visible. Birth-related damage to the 7th cranial (facial) nerve can cause unilateral drooping of the mouth/tongue, unequal cheek muscle movement, or abnormal eyelid movement on the affected side — asymmetry that shows up specifically with crying/movement, not at rest.

Ear placement — the technique that's actually graded

Draw an imaginary line from the inner canthus to the outer canthus of the eye and extend it toward the ear. The top of the ear pinna (where it meets the scalp) should be at or above this line. Low-set ears (falling below this line) are a red flag for chromosomal abnormalities (trisomy 21, trisomy 18), renal anomalies, or intellectual disability. Ears should also be soft/pliable with easy recoil when folded and released; firm, well-formed cartilage suggests more advanced gestational maturity.

Cardiovascular & Peripheral Vascular Assessment
▸
Skin color/perfusion

At rest, skin should be pink, progressing to redder with crying/activity. Assess perfusion most reliably at the lips, gums, and buccal mucosa. Cyanosis anywhere other than the hands/feet (i.e., central cyanosis), with or without increased work of breathing, suggests a respiratory and/or cardiac problem and should be reported.

Capillary refill — technique

Gently pinch the end of a finger or toe, then count the seconds until color returns. Average/expected refill time is ≤3 seconds. A refill time greater than 3 seconds suggests blood is being shunted from the periphery toward the trunk (poor perfusion).

Peripheral pulses — technique

Palpate all peripheral pulses (brachial, femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial) for bilateral symmetry, strength, and rate. Specifically compare the femoral pulses to the brachial pulses side-by-side.

Newborn Resuscitation Sequence
▸
The sequence: Stimulate → Warm → Dry → Position → Suction → Oxygen → Bag-mask ventilation → Intubation → Chest compressions → Medications
Initial rapid assessment (within seconds of birth)

Three questions asked simultaneously: Term gestation? Breathing or crying? Good tone? If yes to all three → routine care (warmth, clear airway if needed, dry, ongoing evaluation) and the infant stays with the mother.

If any answer is "no"
  • Warm, clear airway if necessary, dry, stimulate (rubbing the back, flicking the feet)
  • Reassess: HR below 100, gasping, or apnea? → begin positive pressure ventilation (PPV) with SpO2 monitoring
  • Labored breathing or persistent cyanosis? → clear airway, SpO2 monitoring, consider CPAP
Escalation if HR remains below 100 or drops below 60
  • Take ventilation corrective steps; intubate if no chest rise is seen with PPV
  • HR below 60 despite adequate PPV → begin chest compressions, coordinated with PPV; consider intubation; consider hypovolemia or pneumothorax
  • If HR remains below 60 → IV epinephrine
Targeted preductal SpO2 after birth (do NOT rush to 100%)
  • 1 min: 60-65%
  • 2 min: 65-70%
  • 3 min: 70-75%
  • 4 min: 75-80%
  • 5 min: 80-85%
  • 10 min: 85-95%
Critical pointResuscitation begins with room air (21% O2), not 100% oxygen — current guidelines specifically discourage starting with 100% O2 due to oxidative stress risk. Oxygen is titrated up only if the infant fails to improve.
Priority sequencing ruleNewborn resuscitation ALWAYS takes priority over obtaining the Apgar score — you treat what you see in real time; the Apgar is a retrospective documentation tool, not a trigger to wait before acting.
Airway Maintenance & Bulb Syringe Technique
▸
Normal airway clearance

A healthy term infant born vaginally usually clears the airway on their own — gravity and the cough reflex move most secretions to the oropharynx to be drained, swallowed, or wiped away.

Bulb syringe technique
  • Mouth before nose — always suction the mouth first, then the nose. Suctioning the nose first can stimulate a gasp reflex that draws oral secretions into the airway.
  • Compress the bulb BEFORE inserting it into the mouth/nose, then release slowly to draw secretions in
  • Avoid vigorous or deep suctioning — it can cause trauma to fragile tissue and can trigger vagally-mediated bradycardia
  • Routine chest percussion and suctioning of healthy term/late-preterm infants should be avoided; gentle naso/oropharyngeal suctioning is only for an actually obstructed airway
Auscultation findings

Fine crackles may be heard for several hours after birth, especially in infants born by cesarean (retained lung fluid) — this is expected and should clear as fluid is reabsorbed, typically within a few hours, occasionally up to 24 hours.

Notify provider ifBulb syringe suctioning does not clear mucus interfering with respiratory effort (deeper mechanical suction may be needed, but only after weighing bradycardia risk); inspiratory stridor is present; crackles persist beyond 24 hours or are accompanied by other distress signs.

▸
Ranked by what would kill/harm the infant fastest if missed

  6. Parent-infant interaction, breastfeeding initiation — once physiologic stability across the above is confirmed.

Phytonadione (Vitamin K) — AquaMEPHYTON
▸
Classification

Therapeutic: vitamin/antidote. Pharmacologic: fat-soluble vitamin.

Action / Indication

Required for hepatic synthesis of clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X. Prevents and treats vitamin K deficiency bleeding (hemorrhagic disease of the newborn). Newborns are deficient because vitamin K requires intestinal bacteria to synthesize, and the newborn gut is sterile at birth — the gut isn't colonized enough to produce adequate vitamin K until around day 8.

Dosage / Route
  • ≤1500 g: 0.5 mg (0.25 mL) IM
  • >1500 g: 1 mg (0.5 mL) IM
  • Given soon after birth, within 1-2 hours; may be delayed until after the first breastfeeding to support bonding
  • Site: vastus lateralis (preferred) — middle third, lateral aspect. The anterior thigh is avoided due to neurovascular structures
  • Needle: 25-gauge, 5/8 to 7/8 inch, inserted at a 90-degree angle
Expected/common effects

Injection-site discomfort, local erythema, edema.

Adverse effects & critical safety pointRarely: hemolysis, jaundice, hyperbilirubinemia (especially in preterm infants). IV administration is reserved for emergencies only — rapid IV push can cause severe reactions including cardiac arrest. If IV is required (e.g., preterm infant with minimal muscle mass), the dose is diluted and given slowly over 10-15 minutes with continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring.
Contraindications

Hypersensitivity to phytonadione or components (including benzyl alcohol in the injectable formulation). Use cautiously with impaired liver function.

Nursing considerations
  1. Protect the medication from light.
  2. Administer IM using proper neonatal injection technique; use comfort measures (skin-to-skin, sucrose, swaddling) before/during/after.
  3. Monitor the injection site for bruising and monitor the umbilical cord for bleeding.
  4. Report unusual bleeding/bruising (bleeding gums, nosebleed, black tarry stools, hematuria, bleeding from the cord base) — may indicate the dose was ineffective and more vitamin K is needed.
Notify provider ifAny unexplained bleeding or bruising after administration; a drop in hemoglobin/hematocrit; jaundice appearing in the first 24 hours (may signal hemolysis from the medication in rare cases, though far more likely another cause — still must be reported).
Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationThis shot is not a vaccine — it's a vitamin. Newborns are naturally low on vitamin K because their gut doesn't yet have the bacteria needed to make it. Other Caregiver EducationIf you notice any unusual bruising, bleeding from the injection site, or blood in the stool/vomit in the days after birth, tell the parents and pediatrician promptly.

Fostering Early Infant Attachment
▸
Bonding vs. attachment — key distinction

Bonding refers to the parent's early process of becoming acquainted with, identifying, and claiming the infant as a family member; it occurs through mutually satisfying experiences (e.g., a mother feeling her infant's grasp reflex). Attachment is the broader, ongoing tie that develops between parent and child over time through repeated positive interactions. The terms are often used interchangeably in practice, but bonding is the earlier/faster process and attachment is the durable relationship it builds toward.

Key concepts from attachment theory
  • There is no strict "critical period" that must occur in the first minutes/hours — human parents are adaptable, and attachment can still form successfully after separation (e.g., NICU admission), though early contact is beneficial when possible
  • For secure attachment, the parent must provide a "secure base" for exploration and a "safe haven" during distress — meaning consistent proximity and consistent response to the infant's cues
  • The "fantasy/dream child" imagined during pregnancy is compared, consciously or not, to the "real" infant at birth; how closely they match affects the bonding process
The en face position

Parent's face and infant's face are held approximately 8 inches apart, on the same plane, making direct eye contact. Parents instinctively move the infant into this position. This is one of the clearest observable bonding behaviors the nurse can assess for.

Engrossment (paternal bonding)

New fathers/partners often show intense absorption and preoccupation with the newborn — prolonged gazing, described as almost trance-like. This is a recognized, healthy bonding behavior distinct from but parallel to maternal bonding.

Progression of parental exploration behavior

Parents typically begin by examining fingertips, then explore the extremities, then view/stroke the trunk — progressing from fingertip touch to full palmar contact and holding. Failure to progress past fingertip touch is a facilitating-vs-inhibiting behavior the nurse should note.

Nursing activities to foster attachment (USLO 5)
  1. Place the newborn skin-to-skin with the parent immediately after birth (delay unnecessary procedures like weighing/measuring to allow this).
  2. Provide privacy and uninterrupted time for the family in the first hours after birth.
  3. Encourage early breastfeeding initiation and eye contact within the first 30 minutes, when both mother and infant are typically most alert.
  4. Support rooming-in and liberal visiting for partner/siblings/grandparents to maximize observation and bonding opportunities.
  5. Teach parents to recognize infant behavioral cues (rooting, hand-to-mouth, crying types) and how to respond — this builds parental confidence and responsiveness, which strengthens attachment.
  6. Discuss culture-based expressions of attachment with the family rather than assuming a single "normal" pattern — cultural variation is expected and should not be misread as poor bonding.
Assessment cautionAttachment assessment relies on skillful observation and interviewing, not a checklist with concrete pass/fail criteria like a physical exam. Avoid premature judgments about "poor bonding" without considering fatigue, a long/difficult labor, sedation, cultural norms, or temporary separation — all of which can delay positive attachment behaviors without indicating a lasting problem.
Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationBonding takes time and doesn't have to happen instantly — it's a process, not a single moment. Holding your baby skin-to-skin, making eye contact, and responding to their cues (crying, rooting) all build this relationship. It's normal to feel exhausted or unsure at first; this doesn't mean you're bonding "incorrectly."

Newborn Screening & Diagnostics (Expanded)
▸
  • Universal newborn (metabolic/genetic) screening: heel-stick blood spot on filter paper ("Guthrie card"); all circles must be filled completely for accurate lab processing
  • Bilirubin screening: transcutaneous or serum, per hour-of-life nomogram
Newborn hearing screening
  • Evoked otoacoustic emissions (EOAE): soft earpiece makes a clicking sound; a healthy ear "echoes" it back to a microphone in the earpiece
  • Auditory brainstem response (ABR): sensors on forehead and behind each ear measure the acoustic nerve's response to sounds played through an earphone while the infant sleeps
  • Neither test diagnoses hearing loss definitively — both determine whether further audiologic evaluation is needed. If the infant doesn't pass initially, retest before discharge; if still not passing, refer for comprehensive audiologic evaluation by 3 months of age
Critical Congenital Heart Disease (CCHD) screening
  • Performed via pulse oximetry at 24-48 hours of age — detects hypoxemia that may be the only sign of a critical heart defect before other symptoms appear
  • Oxygen saturation measured in the right hand and one foot
  • Passing result: SpO2 >95% in both extremities AND <3% absolute difference between them
  • Failing/concerning result: SpO2 <90% in either extremity (needs immediate evaluation), OR SpO2 90-95%, OR a ≥3% difference between hand and foot — repeat up to 2 more times within the hour; if still concerning, obtain echocardiogram and cardiology evaluation

Discharge Preparation & Teaching
▸
Why this matters

Discharge teaching should begin as early as possible, ideally starting prenatally, not crammed into the final hour before leaving. Parents are often tired, overwhelmed, and reluctant to ask questions after labor and birth — create a nonjudgmental space and use teach-back to confirm understanding rather than just asking "any questions?"

Car Seat Safety
  • All infants/toddlers ride rear-facing until age 2 or until they reach the seat manufacturer's maximum height/weight, whichever comes first — never in the front seat (airbag suffocation/injury risk)
  • Use a five-point harness over both shoulders and hips, buckling at the crotch; shoulder straps at or below shoulder level, retainer clip at armpit level (not abdomen or neck)
  • Position the infant at a 45-degree angle to prevent slumping/airway obstruction; a tightly rolled blanket can be placed on either side if needed for support
  • Dress the infant in a single thin layer so the harness fits snugly against the body — avoid bulky clothing, sack sleepers, or heavy bundling, which can loosen the harness fit; blankets can be added on TOP of the harness for warmth
  • Never use a secondhand seat of unknown history, one involved in a moderate/severe crash, missing parts/instructions, without a model/manufacture label, or subject to a recall
  • Infant car seat challenge: recommended by the AAP for infants born <37 weeks — the infant is monitored in the car seat for 90-120 minutes (or the length of the ride home) for apnea, bradycardia, and desaturation before discharge is approved in a standard car seat
Umbilical Cord Care
  • Clean the stump with plain water only (gauze if needed to remove debris) — avoid soaps, lotions, alcohol, or antiseptic preparations; evidence does not support routine antimicrobial cord care in most settings
  • Fold the diaper below the cord stump to keep it dry and exposed to air; keep the area free of urine/stool contamination
  • Sponge bathe only (no full immersion bath) until the cord has completely fallen off
  • Expected timeline: cord begins drying within 1-2 hours of birth; plastic clamp is removed once dried, typically 24-48 hours; cord darkens/shrivels by day 2-3; average separation is 10-14 days, up to 3 weeks is still normal; some dried blood at the separation site is expected
Notify provider ifBleeding from the stump, redness spreading beyond the base, purulent (pus) drainage, foul odor, moist/wet appearance that doesn't dry, or the infant develops lethargy or fever alongside any cord finding. A cord that is simply moist with some discharge (without redness, odor, or systemic symptoms) can often be monitored for another 24-48 hours per provider guidance rather than requiring an immediate visit — but any combination with fever, lethargy, or spreading redness needs prompt evaluation.
Bathing
  • Sponge bathe only until the cord falls off; after that, immersion bathing (or swaddled immersion, where the infant is wrapped and one body part is unwrapped/washed at a time) may begin
  • Water temperature: warm, approximately 37.8-38.8°C (100-102°F); water depth should cover the shoulders in an immersion bath but not the head/neck
  • Frequency: a daily bath is NOT necessary and can actually disrupt the skin's protective acid mantle; bathing 2-3 times a week is adequate. Face, neck folds, and diaper area should be cleaned daily/as needed regardless
  • Use a mild, unscented, neutral-pH cleanser; avoid antimicrobial soaps
  • Wash head/hair with mild shampoo to remove maternal fluids and help prevent cradle cap; proceed head-to-toe
  • Check the infant's temperature before and after bathing to monitor for cold stress — a bath is safe as long as temperature stays within 97.7-99.3°F (36.5-37.4°C)
  • Dry and dress the infant immediately after to prevent evaporative heat loss
  • Bath time doubles as a bonding opportunity and a chance for the nurse/parent to observe the infant's tone, alertness, and overall condition
Nonnutritive Sucking (Pacifier Safety)
  • Offer a pacifier for naps/bedtime once breastfeeding is well established — there is compelling evidence pacifier use helps reduce SIDS risk
  • Use only when placing the infant supine for sleep; do NOT reinsert once the infant has fallen asleep; never force a pacifier
  • Safe pacifier design: one-piece construction with a shield/flange too large to enter the mouth and a graspable handle
  • Never hang a pacifier around the infant's neck (strangulation risk) or attach it to clothing; never coat it with a sweet solution; clean and replace regularly
  • Thumb/finger sucking generally does not need to be restricted unless it persists past age 4 or after permanent teeth erupt — discuss with the pediatric provider/dentist if parents are concerned
Infant Follow-Up Care
  • Standard follow-up: 3-5 days after birth, or 48-72 hours after hospital discharge, with an additional visit around 2 weeks of age
  • If discharged at <48 hours of age, the follow-up visit may be scheduled for the very next day
  • Early follow-up is especially important for breastfed infants to assess feeding adequacy, weight trends, and hydration status
  • If a follow-up appointment wasn't scheduled before discharge, parents should call the pediatric office/clinic promptly after arriving home rather than waiting
  • NICU graduates have additional discharge criteria: respiratory stability, consistent weight gain, and successful oral feeding are the standard benchmarks before discharge is considered; ongoing nursing follow-up calls after discharge reduce parental anxiety and improve follow-up compliance
Infant CPR & Choking Response Education

All parents should receive instruction in infant CPR and relieving airway obstruction before discharge — this is especially critical for infants with a history of prematurity, or cardiac/respiratory problems. All caregivers (grandparents, babysitters, anyone who will care for the infant) should learn these basics, not just the primary parents.

  • Pulse check location in an infant: brachial artery (not carotid, as used in adults)
  • Mild airway obstruction (good air exchange, forceful cough, wheezing between coughs): do not interfere with the infant's own attempts to clear it — stay close and continue monitoring; escalate only if it persists or worsens
  • Severe airway obstruction (poor/no air exchange, weak or absent cough, high-pitched sound or no sound on inhalation, cyanosis, inability to cry): must be relieved immediately — activate emergency response if the obstruction does not clear
  • Formal classes are often available through the hospital, birthing center, or community organizations, ideally completed prenatally or very soon after birth
Diaper Changes & Perineal Care
  • Frequency: check the diaper often and change it as soon as the infant voids or stools — not on a fixed schedule. By day 4, expect roughly 6 wet diapers/24 hrs, which parents can use as a rough gauge that feeding intake is adequate
  • Cleaning technique: use plain warm water with a mild soap if needed, or unscented/alcohol-free baby wipes; always let the skin dry completely before putting on the next diaper
  • Female infants: clean front to back (pubic area toward the anus) to avoid introducing stool organisms into the vagina/urethra
  • Uncircumcised male infants: wash and rinse the penis with soap and warm water; do NOT retract the foreskin — it is normally non-retractable in infancy and may not fully retract until age 3 or later; forcing it can cause tearing and scarring
  • Circumcised male infants: clean by gently squeezing warm water over the penis with each diaper change; a whitish-yellow film over the glans in the days after circumcision is normal healing tissue, not infection — do not scrub it off; once healed, no special care beyond routine cleaning is needed (see full Circumcision card, Pain & Circumcision tab)
  • Diaper fit: keep it loose enough to avoid friction/pressure on a circumcision site or the umbilical cord, but snug enough to contain stool/urine; fold diapers below the cord stump until it separates
  • Letting the infant go diaper-free for short periods (tummy time on a protected surface) allows air-drying of the skin and helps prevent rash
Diaper rash (diaper dermatitis)
  • Common (irritant) diaper rash: redness, scaling, or small bumps from prolonged wet/soiled contact, infrequent changes, diarrhea, or plastic pants that trap moisture. Prevention: frequent changes, gentle cleaning, thorough drying, and diaper-free air time. Treatment: a barrier ointment such as zinc oxide or petrolatum at each change to protect skin from urine/stool contact; most cases resolve within a few days
  • Candidal (yeast) diaper rash: intensely red rash with sharply demarcated, scalloped borders and characteristic satellite lesions extending beyond the main rash, often in skin folds — thrives in the warm, moist diaper environment. Requires an antifungal ointment (e.g., clotrimazole, miconazole) with each diaper change rather than a simple barrier cream; distinguishing this pattern from ordinary irritant rash is a common exam point
Notify provider ifDiaper rash persists despite home treatment, shows the scalloped/satellite pattern suggestive of a yeast infection, or is accompanied by fever, blistering, or skin breakdown.
Additional standard discharge topics
  • Newborn hearing screen and universal metabolic screen results/follow-up (see Feeding & Medications tab)
  • Hepatitis B vaccination status (first dose typically given before discharge)
  • Safe sleep/SIDS prevention (see full card, Unit 10)
  • Infant abduction prevention: verify ID band matching, know facility security protocols, never hand the infant to unidentified staff
  • Diaper type preferences: disposable diapers may cause skin reactions from added fragrance in sensitive infants (switching brands often resolves this); cloth diapers should be laundered separately using a small amount of detergent, with presoaking often needed for stains; exclusively breastfed stool is typically odorless and does not stain as much as formula stool
  • Nail care: newborn nails are rarely trimmed in the first days (injury/infection risk); filing or use of infant mittens is preferred initially, with careful trimming once nails separate from the skin after about a week, often while the infant sleeps
Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationBefore you leave, make sure you feel confident with: installing the car seat correctly (rear-facing, snug harness), cleaning the cord stump with water only and folding the diaper below it, giving a sponge bath 2-3 times a week, safely offering a pacifier once breastfeeding is going well, and knowing your follow-up appointment date (usually within 3-5 days). We strongly recommend completing an infant CPR class before or soon after you go home.
Other Caregiver EducationIf you'll be caring for this baby, you should also know infant CPR and how to respond to choking, since emergencies don't wait for the parents to be present. Ask the parents to walk you through their car seat, cord care, and bathing routine so you're following the same approach consistently.

Neonatal Pain Response
▸
Why newborns feel pain — the neurodevelopmental basis

Older clinical thinking assumed that an immature nervous system prevented or blunted pain sensation in newborns and that they couldn't remember painful experiences. This has been disproven by research. The neurodevelopment timeline: the CNS is well developed by 24 weeks gestation; the peripheral and spinal structures that transmit pain signals are present and functional between the first and second trimesters; and the pituitary-adrenal axis is also well developed by this point, producing a genuine fight-or-flight (catecholamine) response to painful/stressful stimuli. In short — even a preterm infant has the anatomic wiring needed to feel and respond to pain.

What influences the pain response

Pain response varies with the characteristics of the stimulus (source, location, timing — acute vs. prolonged/recurrent pain produce different responses), gestational age, genetic differences in available neurotransmitters/receptors, and the infant's behavioral state at the time (a more awake infant typically shows a more robust pain response than one in a sleep state).

The physiologic stress cascade

Pain triggers release of epinephrine, norepinephrine, glucagon, cortisol, corticosterone, and other stress hormones, along with rises in lactate, pyruvate, and glucose. This is not a trivial response — the physiologic cost of unmanaged pain can affect the cardiovascular, respiratory, endocrine, metabolic, immunologic, and coagulation systems, both acutely and with lasting effect if pain is repeated/prolonged (as in a NICU stay).

Physiologic signs
  • Tachycardia, tachypnea, elevated (or sometimes decreased) blood pressure
  • Desaturation/cyanosis, pallor, flushing
  • Changes in intracranial pressure and vagal tone
  • Muscle tremors, hypertonia or hypotonia
  • Sleep/wake cycle changes — more wakeful OR more lethargic
  • Feeding difficulties
Behavioral signs
  • Crying (whimper to high-pitched, shrill cry) — the most common behavioral sign of pain
  • Facial expression: grimace, eye squeeze, brow bulge, nasolabial furrow, widespread/open mouth
  • Flexion/adduction of limbs (a withdrawal posture)
  • Fussy, irritable, or listless affect
Critical assessment pointSeverely compromised or preterm neonates may be unable to generate a visible pain response despite genuinely experiencing pain. Absence of overt crying or facial grimacing does NOT mean absence of pain — this is a common wrong-answer trap on exam questions.
Professional standardThe National Association of Neonatal Nurses (NANN) states that every newborn nurse should have education and competency validation in pain assessment. Every neonate should receive an initial pain assessment plus a pain management plan, with pain assessed and documented on a regular basis — not just when a painful procedure is performed. Even healthy, low-risk term newborns should be assessed for discomfort during routine care and especially during/after procedures like heel sticks, injections, and circumcision.
Pain Assessment Tools
▸
What these tools measure

Because newborns cannot verbalize pain, every tool scores a combination of behavioral components (facial expression, cry, state of arousal, body activity/movement) and physiologic components (heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate/pattern, oxygen saturation). The nurse must consider the infant's gestational age when selecting and interpreting a tool — some scales are built for term infants, others specifically for preterm infants, and some focus on a particular context like postoperative or procedural pain.

Nursing use of these tools
  1. Assess and document pain on a regular schedule, not only when pain is suspected — consistent use of a scale is what allows the nurse to detect a rising pain trend and treat early, before pain becomes severe.
  2. Reassess after any intervention (pharmacologic or nonpharmacologic) to confirm effectiveness, using the same tool for a valid before/after comparison.
  3. Match the tool to the population: use a preterm-specific tool (PIPP) for preterm infants rather than a term-oriented tool, since expected physiologic responses differ by gestational age.
Pain Management — Nonpharmacologic Methods
▸
General principle

Despite decades of evidence, policies, and standards, acute infant pain remains undermanaged in practice. Combining two or more nonpharmacologic methods produces more effective pain reduction than any single method alone — this "stacking" approach is a recurring theme across all the strategies below.

Swaddling

Snugly wrapping the infant in a lightweight blanket. Limits the newborn's boundaries, aids self-regulation, and reduces both physiologic and behavioral stress from acute pain. Also commonly used simply to calm a fussy baby and promote sleep.

Safe swaddling technique — DDH safety alertArms extended (or flexed, in the very early newborn period), legs flexed, hips in neutral position without rotation, with slight flexion and abduction allowing free knee movement. Swaddling with legs extended straight increases the risk of hip dislocation (DDH) — this is a frequently tested safety point. Leave room for 2-3 adult fingers between the infant's chest and the swaddle to prevent overheating/respiratory compromise. Infant should be swaddled lying on the back, and swaddling should stop by about 2 months of age once the infant can roll.
Facilitated Tucking / Containment

A hand-swaddling technique where the caregiver holds the neonate in a flexed, side-lying position using their hands (rather than a blanket). Effective for reducing pain and distress specifically in preterm infants. Often used just before an invasive procedure like a heel stick. Related concept: "nesting" — blanket rolls placed under the sheet to help the infant maintain a flexed position when prone or side-lying, which decreases physiologic/behavioral stress during routine care like bathing or weighing.

Skin-to-Skin Care (SSC) / Kangaroo Care

The undressed infant (diaper only) is placed prone, vertically, directly on the parent's bare chest. Safe and effective for a single painful procedure such as a heel stick, reducing pain by both behavioral and physiologic measures. Also benefits thermoregulation, and for preterm/VLBW infants specifically, may support maternal psychological healing after a high-risk pregnancy and help the mother reclaim the mothering role.

Breastfeeding / Breast Milk & Non-Nutritive Sucking (NNS)

Breastfeeding or expressed breast milk reduces pain during minor procedures like heel sticks. NNS on a pacifier is a common comfort measure, frequently combined with oral sucrose during painful procedures for a stronger combined effect.

Sensorial Stimulation

Uses multiple senses at once to diminish minor pain — speaking softly to the infant, gently massaging the face, and providing oral sucrose on the tongue, all simultaneously. Distraction with visual, oral, auditory, or tactile stimulation can also help, particularly in term neonates.

Other supportive measures

Touch, gentle massage, rocking, holding, and environmental modification (low noise and dim lighting) all contribute to reducing neonatal stress and pain perception.

  1. Initiate comfort measures a few minutes before the procedure begins, giving the newborn time to relax and reorganize — don't wait until the procedure has already started.
  2. Combine methods whenever possible (e.g., SSC + breastfeeding, or NNS + oral sucrose) rather than relying on one technique alone.
  3. Teach parents these techniques so they can be active participants in their newborn's procedural care — but clarify that sucrose is not an appropriate long-term home strategy, only a facility-based procedural tool.

Swaddling snugly (with hips and knees able to move, not straight legs) also helps calm your baby. These techniques work well in the hospital, but sugar water isn't something to use regularly at home.
Other Caregiver EducationIf you need to comfort a fussy baby, swaddling with the hips loose (not legs straight), gentle rocking, and a pacifier are safe go-to options. Never swaddle with the legs pulled straight and bound tightly — this can injure the hips.
Pain Management — Pharmacologic Methods
▸
Local / Topical Anesthesia

Routinely used for procedures like circumcision and lumbar puncture, and sometimes for venipuncture/heel sticks.

  • EMLA (eutectic mixture of local anesthetic — prilocaine-lidocaine) or LMX4 (4% lidocaine): topical creams applied before the procedure
  • Dorsal penile nerve block (DPNB) and ring block: injected local anesthetic specifically for circumcision
Nonopioid Analgesia

Oral liquid acetaminophen is effective for mild-to-moderate pain, particularly from inflammatory conditions.

Opioid Analgesia

Morphine and fentanyl are the most widely used opioids for moderate-to-severe neonatal pain. Given as continuous or bolus IV infusion for effective, safe pain control in this context.

Priority safety monitoringAny neonate receiving opioid-level pain control must be closely monitored for respiratory depression — this is the adverse effect that outranks all others in priority, consistent with opioid monitoring principles covered elsewhere in this guide.
Other pharmacologic methods

Epidural infusion, regional/local nerve blocks, and intradermal or topical anesthetics round out the available options for specific procedural contexts.

Circumcision-Specific Pain Management

This is the clearest real-world example of combining pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic methods for maximum effect: DPNB or topical EMLA/LMX4 plus nonnutritive sucking (NNS) and concentrated oral sucrose together produce better pain control than any single method alone.

Key exam principlePharmacologic and nonpharmacologic methods are not either/or — combining them is the evidence-based standard for procedures like circumcision. An NGN question describing only one method being used for a significant procedure (like circumcision with no analgesia at all) should be recognized as a gap in care.
Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationFor procedures like circumcision, your baby will receive numbing medicine (either a cream or a small injection) along with comfort measures like sucrose and a pacifier — using both together works better than either alone. For babies needing stronger pain medicine (like morphine), the team watches breathing very closely since that's the main risk with these medications.
Other Caregiver EducationIf this baby is on a stronger pain medication after a procedure or surgery, expect more sedation than usual and know that breathing is being closely monitored by the care team as a safety precaution.
Oral Sucrose
▸
Classification

Carbohydrate solution; neonatal procedural analgesic adjunct / nonopioid comfort measure.

Action / Indication

Reduces the behavioral pain response during brief, minor procedures — heel sticks, venipuncture, IM injections (including Vitamin K/HepB above), and as an adjunct during circumcision alongside other analgesia.

Route / Timing

Small amount given orally, typically about 2 minutes before the procedure, often followed immediately by a pacifier (nonnutritive sucking) for combined effect. Per facility protocol.

Expected effects

Minimal when used appropriately in a stable infant.

Notify provider ifChoking, coughing, aspiration, or oxygen desaturation/bradycardia occurs with administration — more relevant in a compromised or very preterm infant, where oral coordination may be unsafe.
Combine with (best practice)
  • Nonnutritive sucking / pacifier
  • Swaddling
  • Skin-to-skin contact (kangaroo care)
  • Breastfeeding during the procedure, when feasible
Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationA small amount of sugar water can help comfort your baby during quick, mildly painful procedures like a heel stick or shot. Exam pearl: Newborn procedural pain → sucrose + pacifier + swaddling/skin-to-skin = appropriate nonpharmacologic strategy, not a substitute for pharmacologic pain relief in major procedures.

Hyperbilirubinemia (Jaundice)
▸
Pathophysiology

Bilirubin is a byproduct of RBC breakdown (heme). The newborn liver is immature and cannot conjugate (process for excretion) bilirubin quickly, so unconjugated bilirubin accumulates. It can cross the blood-brain barrier and deposit in brain tissue.

Etiology
Physiologic jaundice (benign)

Appears AFTER 24 hours of age, peaks day 3-5 in term infants, resolves in 1-2 weeks. Caused by normal newborn RBC turnover + immature liver conjugation.

Pathologic jaundice

Appears WITHIN the first 24 hours of life — at any bilirubin level, jaundice in the first 24 hrs is considered pathologic and requires workup. Causes: hemolytic disease (Rh/ABO incompatibility), sepsis, polycythemia, cephalhematoma resorption, G6PD deficiency.

Breastfeeding-associated (early) jaundice

First week of life; caused by insufficient milk intake → fewer stools → bilirubin reabsorbed from gut.

Breast milk jaundice (late)

Onset 5-10 days; substances in breast milk affect bilirubin conjugation. Usually benign; breastfeeding is continued.

Onset timing is the single fastest way to sort jaundice type on an exam question
Signs & symptoms
  • Yellowing that starts at the head and progresses caudally (head → chest → abdomen → extremities — hands/feet last)
  • Enlarged liver, poor muscle tone, lethargy, poor sucking reflex (as levels rise)
  • Dark gold/brown urine can occur with phototherapy dehydration
Visual severity indicator taught in Perry Ch. 23 — assess in natural light
Risk factors
  • Gestational age <35-36 weeks (most common risk factor)
  • Rh/ABO incompatibility, bruising/cephalhematoma, polycythemia
  • Delayed or ineffective feeding, delayed meconium passage
  • East Asian ethnicity, exclusive breastfeeding with poor intake
Complications

Acute bilirubin encephalopathy: lethargy, hypotonia, irritability, seizures, coma (early-weeks presentation). Kernicterus: irreversible long-term bilirubin toxicity — hypotonia, delayed motor skills, hearing loss, cerebral palsy, gaze abnormalities.

  2. Initiate/verify phototherapy per order; maximize skin exposure, eye shields.

  4. Monitor hydration status (urine output, weight) during phototherapy — insensible water loss risk.

  • Lethargy, poor feeding, high-pitched cry, or hypotonia develop in a jaundiced infant (possible encephalopathy)
  • TSB rising rapidly despite phototherapy (may need exchange transfusion)
  • Temperature instability under phototherapy
Treatment

Phototherapy (most common): converts unconjugated bilirubin to an excretable form using blue light spectrum. Exchange transfusion: for rapidly rising levels despite intensive phototherapy or severe hemolytic disease.

Phototherapy safety essentialsNo lotions/ointments on skin (absorb heat, cause burns). Eyes shielded (opaque mask, eyelids closed first) — remove at least once per shift to check for irritation and allow bonding. Cover genitals. Monitor temperature closely. Turn off lights before drawing blood specimens. Watch for bronze baby syndrome as a complication. Expect loose green stools — this is bilirubin excretion, not diarrhea.
Maternal self-care strategies
  • Continue breastfeeding on schedule even during phototherapy — frequent feeding helps clear bilirubin, and breaks in treatment for feeding are appropriate
  • Rest when the infant is under lights and not being held, since bonding time is more limited during treatment
  • Ask staff to help arrange skin-to-skin time during phototherapy breaks to maintain bonding and milk supply

If bili lights are needed, you can still hold and feed your baby during treatment breaks.

Hypoglycemia
▸
Pathophysiology

In utero, fetal glucose is continuously supplied via placental transfer, maintained at 60-70% of maternal levels. After cord clamping, this supply stops abruptly. The newborn must rely on liver glycogen stores and adipose tissue, but gluconeogenesis/ketogenesis are underdeveloped. Glucose is the brain's main fuel; low levels trigger compensatory shunting of blood volume to the brain.

Etiology / at-risk populations
  • SGA, LGA, preterm/late preterm, low birth weight
  • Infant of a diabetic mother (fetal hyperinsulinemia continues after cord clamped)
  • Perinatal stress: asphyxia, cold stress, respiratory distress
Signs & symptoms

Can be asymptomatic — hypoglycemia may be present with no clinical signs. When present: jitteriness/tremor, lethargy, poor feeding, high-pitched or weak cry, cyanosis, apnea, tachypnea, hypotonia, temperature instability, seizures.

Complications

Untreated/severe hypoglycemia → long-term neurological damage.

Hypocalcemia
▸
Pathophysiology

In utero, calcium is actively transported across the placenta, keeping fetal calcium levels higher than maternal levels. After birth, this active transfer stops, and the newborn's parathyroid function is not yet fully mature to compensate.

Etiology / risk factors

Prematurity, infant of a diabetic mother, birth asphyxia, low maternal calcium/vitamin D status.

Signs & symptoms

Tremor, jitteriness (overlaps with hypoglycemia presentation — both should be checked when tremor is seen), neuromuscular irritability, poor feeding.

NCLEX-Style Practice Questions

Modeled on your instructor's NGN format. Tap an answer to see if you're correct, then read the rationale.

Q1. A 2-day-old newborn is noted to have yellowing of the skin extending from the head to the chest. The infant is feeding well and has normal tone. What is the nurse's priority action?
Correct: Obtain a bilirubin level and notify the provider. Jaundice progressing from head toward the chest at day 2 is consistent with physiologic jaundice timing, but any degree of jaundice still requires objective bilirubin measurement to guide management. Feeding should be encouraged, not withheld, since it helps bilirubin excretion.
Q2. A late preterm infant born at 35 weeks has a blood glucose of 38 mg/dL at 2 hours of life and is jittery. What is the nurse's priority action?
Correct: Initiate oral feeding immediately, recheck in 30 min. A symptomatic infant with glucose below the treatment threshold needs prompt action, not delay pending confirmatory labs. IV dextrose is reserved for infants who don't respond to feeding or cannot feed safely.
Q3. In the first 2 hours after birth, which nursing action takes priority over all others?
Correct: Assess and secure the airway. Airway/breathing always precedes every other newborn intervention because hypoxia is the fastest-acting life threat. Vitamin K and eye prophylaxis are time-sensitive but can wait briefly if airway needs attention; breastfeeding initiation comes after physiologic stability is confirmed.
Q4. Two days after a PlastiBell circumcision, the nurse notes the glans is dark red with a yellow coating over the surface. The parent asks if this needs to be cleaned off. What is the nurse's best response?
Correct: "That yellow coating is granulation tissue..." A dark red glans progressing to yellow exudate at 24-48 hours post-circumcision is the expected healing pattern. True infection would present with redness, swelling, discharge with odor, or worsening.
Q5. A breastfeeding newborn has lost 8% of birth weight by day 3. What is the priority nursing action?
Correct: Notify the provider and assess technique. Expected breastfed weight loss is 5-7% in the first week; 8% by day 3 exceeds this threshold and warrants provider notification plus an in-depth feeding assessment (latch, frequency, transfer) before assuming formula is required.
