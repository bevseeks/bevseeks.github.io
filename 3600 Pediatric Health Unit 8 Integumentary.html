    Skin Infections, Inflammations & Infestations

    🔥 Burns — Priority Topic for USLO 3

    ⚠ Why burns are worse in children
    • Thinner skin → lower temp / shorter exposure causes a MORE severe burn than in an adult
    • Higher mortality with severe burns vs. older children/adults with comparable injury
    • Harder to communicate pain accurately
    • ↑ risk of fluid/heat loss, dehydration, metabolic acidosis
    • Higher body-fluid-to-body-mass ratio → greater cardiovascular risk
    • Immature immune system → ↑ infection risk
    • Less muscle mass/body fat → protein-calorie deficiency risk
    • Scarring is MORE severe than in adults; growth delay may follow a significant burn

    Burn Types

    TypeExamples
    Thermal (most common)Flame (fire — inhalation risk), Flash (explosion), Scald (hot liquid/steam), Contact (hot object)
    RadiationSunburn, radiation therapy, radioactive material
    ChemicalCorrosive agents — sulfuric acid, lye, ammonia
    ElectricalChewing electrical cords, power lines, inserting objects into outlets

    Depth of Burn Injury

    DepthKey Features
    Superficial (1st°)Erythema + pain 2–3 days (sunburn). Intact epidermis, no blisters. Peels, heals WITHOUT scarring.
    Partial-thickness (2nd°)Erythema + blisters (may burst/weep). Bleeds easily, very painful. Epidermis + outer dermis. Heals in ~3 wks. Scarring WILL occur.
    Full-thickness (3rd°)Variable color; dry, stiff, leathery; NO blanching; NO pain (nerve destruction). Needs excision + grafting.
    Deep full-thickness (4th°)Extends into muscle/bone. No sensation. Amputation likely if digits affected.
    🎯 High-yield: pain pattern is counterintuitive
    • MORE pain = shallower burn (1st/2nd° — nerves intact) · NO pain = deeper burn (3rd/4th° — nerves destroyed). A burn that doesn't hurt is not a mild burn.

    Extent — Total Body Surface Area (TBSA)

    • Adult "rule of nines" is INACCURATE in children — different body proportions (larger head, smaller legs)
    • Use age-specific charts (Lund and Browder) for pediatric TBSA estimation

    Three Phases of Burn Care

    PhaseFocus
    1. Burn shock / resuscitativeFirst 24–48 hrs; characterized by shock — airway & fluid resuscitation are priorities
    2. Recovery / wound healingClose the wound ASAP; infection prevention, nutrition, pain control
    3. RehabilitativePrevent scar contractures; reintegrate child into social environment

    Burn Shock / Resuscitative Phase — Take Action Priorities

    PRIORITY 1 — AIRWAY (especially flame burns)
    • Inhalation injury red flags: enclosed-space burn, singed nasal hairs/eyebrows, hoarse voice, soot on face, burned facial hair, burns to torso/face, swollen lips, hypoxia
    • SOB/dyspnea worse lying down, wheezing, gasping = acute pulmonary edema — EMERGENCY
    • Action: early intubation & ventilation; 100% oxygen
    PRIORITY 2 — CIRCULATION (fluid resuscitation)
    • Third-spacing from ↑ capillary permeability, evaporative loss (no skin barrier), cell rupture → massive fluid needs
    • Monitor hourly urine output — the key indicator of resuscitation adequacy
    • Adequacy also judged by vital signs (especially HR), capillary refill, and sensorium/LOC — NOT skin turgor or edema level
    WATCH FOR — Compartment Syndrome
    • Highest risk: circumferential 3rd-degree burns (arm/leg/torso)
    • Leathery, non-stretching skin + edema compresses nerves/vessels
    • Fasciotomy or escharotomy may be needed

    Chemical Burns — Special Priority

    🧪 Chemical burn priority action
    • Remove the chemical first — dilute or brush off
    • Do NOT apply another chemical to "neutralize" it
    • Monitor for renal failure and blood pressure changes
    • Otherwise managed like a thermal burn

    Ongoing Priorities (After Resuscitative Phase)

    PriorityNursing Focus
    Pain managementMeds BEFORE dressing changes; diversional activities/visualization; encourage participation; central line often needed
    CirculationOngoing extremity circulation checks; watch for need for surgical decompression
    NutritionNG to suction during burn shock phase; resume feeds after diuresis; ↑ protein/calorie needs; possible TPN
    Sepsis recognitionEarly: ↓LOC/lethargy, fever, restlessness, cap refill >5 sec. Late: ↓BP — don't wait for hypotension

    Grafting & Discharge

    TopicKey Points
    Graft typesSheet graft — face/hands, best cosmetic result. Mesh graft — larger surface areas.
    Pruritus mgmtCool the graft; hydroxyzine (Atarax) or diphenhydramine (Benadryl)
    RehabPositioning/splinting to prevent contractures; active ROM; massage; compression garments
    PsychosocialPrepare child for appearance changes; consider Erikson's stage (body image is huge for adolescents)
    Discharge teachingMUST attend all follow-ups; visiting nurse/PT/OT often needed; grafted skin has NO sweat glands (overheating risk); grafted skin feels pressure, NOT pain (injury risk)

    Development & Burn Risk by Age (USLO 3)

    Age/StageWhy at risk
    Infant↑ gross motor skills → scalds reaching for stove items, chewing cords, flame/inhalation from house fire
    ToddlerExploring/independence → scalds (stove/hot water), lighters/matches, hot appliances, cords in mouth, house fire
    Preschool↑ mobility/independence → running into kitchen while cooking, hot appliances, cords, lighters/matches, house fire
    School-age/AdolescentMore freedom/access → cooking scalds, lighters/matches, chemical experimentation, power-line proximity while climbing
    🚩 Red flag: burns suggestive of abuse
    • Stocking/glove pattern — sharp, uniform immersion line = suspicious for deliberate dipping
    • Splash/splatter pattern with irregular borders = more consistent with accidental burn
