Anemia (General)

Anemia — General Concept

The most common hematologic disorder of childhood

Pathophysiology & Etiology

Anemia is a reduction in circulating red blood cells (RBCs) or hemoglobin, which decreases the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood. It is not a disease itself but an indication of an underlying pathologic process — nutritional deficiency, blood loss, hemolysis, or bone marrow failure. It is the most common hematologic disorder of childhood. Lecture

Risk Factors
  • Nutritional deficiencies (iron, B12, folate)
  • Chronic blood loss
  • Hemolytic processes (e.g., sickle cell disease)
  • Bone marrow suppression (e.g., chemotherapy, aplastic anemia)
  • Chronic disease/inflammation

Signs & Symptoms

Clinical manifestations
  • Fatigue
  • Shortness of breath
  • Lethargy
  • Tachycardia (compensatory — heart works harder to deliver oxygen with fewer RBCs available)
  • Pale skin
  • Irritability
  • Dizziness
Why tachycardia with anemia? Fewer RBCs means less oxygen delivered per heartbeat, so the heart compensates by beating faster to maintain oxygen delivery to tissues. Tachycardia in an anemic child is a compensatory sign, not a primary cardiac problem.

Notify the Provider If

  • Hemoglobin/hematocrit dropping significantly from baseline

Priority Nursing Interventions

  1. Urgent
    Assess for and report signs of hemodynamic compromise (severe tachycardia, hypotension, altered mental status) that suggest the anemia is severe enough to impair oxygen delivery. Why: Severe anemia can progress to hypoxic tissue injury if oxygen delivery fails to meet demand.
  2. Important
    Identify and treat the underlying cause (nutritional, hemolytic, bone marrow-related) — anemia is a sign, not a standalone diagnosis.
  3. Important
    Administer blood transfusions or colony-stimulating factors (e.g., Epoetin Alpha/Epogen) as prescribed; provide supportive care and educate on rest/activity balance. Lecture

Treatment

  • Blood transfusions as needed for symptomatic or severe anemia
  • Colony-stimulating factors: Epoetin Alpha (Epogen) to stimulate RBC production Lecture
  • Diet or vitamin supplementation depending on underlying cause
  • Supportive care

Medication Spotlight: Epoetin Alfa — Epogen / Procrit

Parent / Caregiver Teaching

  • Monitor blood pressure as instructed
  • Understand that the medication stimulates red blood cell production — it takes time to see effect, it is not an immediate fix
  • Report severe headache, chest pain, shortness of breath, weakness, or swelling promptly
Pediatric Patient Teaching

  • School-age: explain why energy may improve gradually as the anemia improves
  • Adolescent: involve the patient directly in understanding lab results and treatment goals

Iron-Deficiency Anemia

Iron-Deficiency Anemia (IDA)

Most prevalent nutritional disorder worldwide

Pathophysiology & Etiology

Iron is essential for hemoglobin synthesis. When iron stores are depleted, hemoglobin production falls, reducing the oxygen-carrying capacity of RBCs. IDA is the most prevalent nutritional disorder worldwide. Lecture

Causes / Risk Factors
  • Decreased iron supply (inadequate dietary intake — classically excessive cow's milk intake displacing iron-rich foods)
  • Increased iron demands (rapid growth periods — infancy, adolescence)
  • Blood loss (acute or chronic)

Signs & Symptoms

Clinical manifestations
  • Irritability
  • Fatigue
  • Delayed motor development (in infants/toddlers — iron is critical for neurodevelopment)
  • Tachycardia
  • Shortness of breath
  • Pale skin / conjunctival pallor
Signs of Exertion From Low Oxygen — Especially in Infants Lecture
Watch for during feeding and activity
  • Difficulty sucking
  • Slow, strained movements
  • Sluggish behavior, limp posture
  • Activity intolerance
  • Palpitations, dyspnea, shortness of breath, tachycardia
  • Sweating
  • Dizziness/lightheadedness

This list matters clinically because an infant with IDA can show these exertional signs specifically during feeding — feeding itself becomes a physical exertion the anemic infant struggles to tolerate, which can further impair adequate intake and worsen the anemia in a vicious cycle.

Key point Delayed motor development is a distinguishing feature of IDA in young children — iron deficiency in infancy is linked to lasting cognitive/neurobehavioral effects if not corrected, which is why screening and prevention in this age group matters so much.

TestPurpose
History & PhysicalAssess dietary history (cow's milk intake, dietary iron sources) Lecture
Lab tests (CBC, ferritin, iron studies)Confirm low iron stores and microcytic, hypochromic anemia pattern Lecture
Knowledge Check — Poor Response to Iron Therapy Lecture

Scenario: A child with anemia does not show an increase in hemoglobin levels after 1 month of iron therapy. What should the nurse assess for?

Assess for: noncompliance with iron administration, an underlying bleeding disorder, improper iron administration technique, and persistent ongoing bleeding.

Note the distractor: "failure to brush teeth after taking the iron supplement" causes cosmetic tooth staining, not treatment failure — it does not affect the hemoglobin response and is not a reason to investigate further. Don't confuse a cosmetic side effect with a clinical treatment-failure cause.

Foods Patients Can and Cannot Eat — Prevention & Treatment Lecture

Encouraged / Iron-Rich
  • Breast milk or iron-fortified formula until 12 months of age
  • Iron-fortified cereal from 6–12 months of age
  • Iron-rich foods generally (red meat, leafy greens, legumes, iron-fortified grains) as age-appropriate solids are introduced
  • Vitamin C-rich foods to pair with iron doses/meals, to enhance absorption: oranges, strawberries, and other vitamin C sources Lecture
Avoid / Limit
  • No cow's milk before 12 months of age — a critical safety point
  • After 12 months, limit cow's milk intake — excessive milk consumption displaces iron-rich foods and can itself cause or worsen IDA
  • Note: broccoli and blueberries do not meaningfully enhance iron absorption the way vitamin C-rich fruits do — a common knowledge-check distractor pairing

Priority Nursing Interventions

  1. Important
    Obtain dietary history, especially cow's milk intake pattern, to identify the most common preventable cause. Why: Excess cow's milk before 12 months (or in excess after) is one of the most common and correctable causes of IDA in toddlers.
  2. Important
    Initiate iron supplementation and/or dietary modification as prescribed; monitor for improvement via follow-up labs.

Treatment

  • Iron-rich foods, ideally paired with a vitamin C source to enhance absorption
  • Iron supplementation (oral, typically)
  • Follow-up lab work to confirm response to treatment
Medication Administration Safety Point Oral iron supplements should be administered through a straw or medicine dropper placed at the back of the mouth because liquid iron stains the teeth. This is a classic NCLEX administration detail — but remember, missing this step affects cosmetic staining only, not treatment efficacy.

Medication Spotlight: Ferrous Sulfate — Oral Iron

Parent / Caregiver Teaching
  • Give exactly as prescribed
  • Use an oral syringe or dropper for liquid preparations
  • Give toward the back of the mouth, or use a straw when age-appropriate, because iron can stain teeth — a specific point Saunders emphasizes
  • Expect dark (greenish-black) stools — this is a normal, expected effect, not a bleeding concern
  • Monitor for constipation/GI upset
  • Keep iron locked away — overdose can be dangerous or fatal in a young child
  • Follow the child's iron-rich dietary plan alongside the supplement, pairing with vitamin C sources to enhance absorption
Pediatric Patient Teaching By developmental stage
  • Infant/toddler: caregiver administers; use a straw/dropper when age-appropriate
  • Preschool: "This medicine helps your body make healthy blood"
  • School-age: teach why iron is needed and have the child report stomach upset or constipation
  • Adolescent: teach adherence, dietary pairing, expected dark stools, and safe medication storage

Source: Saunders 9th ed. (iron administration technique, tooth-staining precaution); Unit 10 lecture (iron-deficiency anemia content).

Patient Education — Split by Audience & Developmental Stage

Parent / Caregiver Education
  • No cow's milk before 12 months; limit cow's milk after 12 months to avoid displacing iron-rich foods Lecture
  • Use breast milk or iron-fortified formula through the first year; introduce iron-fortified cereal starting 6 months Lecture
  • Administer liquid iron via straw or dropper to the back of the mouth to prevent tooth staining
  • Store iron supplements safely away from children — iron overdose is toxic and potentially fatal; keep no more than a month's supply in the home
  • Explain that stool may turn dark/greenish on iron therapy — this is expected, not a bleeding concern
Pediatric / Child-Directed Education Most cases diagnosed in infancy/toddlerhood — education is caregiver-directed
  • As the child grows into preschool/school age, use simple language about "foods that make strong blood" to build positive associations with iron-rich foods
  • Encourage the older child to help choose iron-rich snacks as a way to build autonomy around their own nutrition

Self-Care Strategies

Ongoing family management
  • Track cow's milk intake and keep within recommended limits for age
  • Give iron supplement consistently, ideally with a source of vitamin C to enhance absorption, and away from dairy/calcium (which inhibits absorption)
  • Attend follow-up labs to confirm treatment is working
  • Keep iron supplements locked away/out of reach given overdose toxicity risk

Sickle Cell Disease (SCD)

Sickle Cell Disease

Autosomal recessive hemoglobinopathy — chronic disease with acute life-threatening crises

Pathophysiology & Etiology

SCD is an autosomal recessive hemoglobinopathy in which normal hemoglobin A is partly or completely replaced by abnormal hemoglobin S (HbS). When both parents carry the sickle cell trait (heterozygous, HbAS), there is a 25% chance per pregnancy of a child with sickle cell anemia (homozygous, HbSS). HbS is sensitive to deoxygenation — under low-oxygen conditions, RBCs assume a rigid, sickle (C- or S-shaped) shape rather than their normal flexible biconcave disc shape. These sickled cells clump together and obstruct capillary blood flow (vaso-occlusion), causing local hypoxia, tissue ischemia, and infarction. Repeated sickling eventually causes cells to become permanently sickled. Newborns are largely asymptomatic due to protective fetal hemoglobin (HbF), but as HbF declines over the first year of life, sickle-related complications emerge.

Risk Factors
  • Both parents carrying the HbS gene (autosomal recessive inheritance)
  • African American descent (highest U.S. incidence — sickle trait incidence ~8% in African Americans, up to 40% in West Africa, related to historical malaria resistance)
  • Also seen in Hispanic, Italian, Greek, Iranian, Turkish, Arab, Caribbean, and Asian-Indian populations

Signs & Symptoms (Baseline Disease)

Chronic baseline manifestations
  • Pain (chronic and episodic)
  • Weakness
  • Pallor
  • Fatigue
  • Tissue hypoxia

Complications (Long-Term, Chronic Disease Course)

  • Avascular necrosis (hip, shoulder)
  • Splenomegaly progressing to autosplenectomy (repeated splenic infarction destroys splenic function, raising infection risk long-term)
  • Hepatomegaly, gallstones
  • Priapism
  • Retinopathy, potential blindness
  • Stroke, paralysis
  • Chronic leg ulcers (rare in children, more common in adults)
  • Dactylitis (hand-foot syndrome) — often the first painful manifestation of vaso-occlusive crisis, typically occurring between 6 months and 2 years of age
  • Pulmonary hypertension
  • Heart failure from chronic anemia burden

Sickle Cell Crisis — Precipitating Factors Lecture

Anything that increases the body's need for oxygen or alters oxygen transport can precipitate a crisis:

  • Trauma
  • Infection, fever
  • Physical and emotional stress
  • Dehydration
  • Hypoxia
  • High altitude
  • Fatigue

Types of Sickle Cell Crisis — Know These Cold Lecture

Vaso-Occlusive (Thrombotic) Crisis

The "painful crisis." Ischemia from stasis/clumping of sickled cells in microcirculation → distal ischemia → infarction.

Signs: Fever, pain, tissue engorgement/swelling of hands, feet, joints, or affected area; abdominal pain.

Aplastic Crisis

Diminished production and increased destruction of RBCs — often triggered by viral infection.

Signs: Paleness, lethargy, headache, fever, profound anemia, fainting, recent illness history.

Splenic Sequestration

Intrasplenic pooling of large amounts of blood. Peak age 5 months–2 years. Life-threatening — death can occur within hours.

Signs: Profound anemia, hypovolemia, shock.

Acute Chest Syndrome

Lowers blood oxygen level; resembles pneumonia with new pulmonary infiltrates.

Signs: Chest pain, tachypnea, fever, wheezing, cough, hypoxia.

A fifth type, hyperhemolytic crisis, involves accelerated RBC destruction over a short time, presenting with anemia, jaundice, and reticulocytosis (Saunders 9th ed.).

Acuity Ranking Within Crisis Types Splenic sequestration and acute chest syndrome are the most immediately life-threatening — both can progress to death within hours if not treated urgently. Vaso-occlusive crisis is the most common presentation but is rarely immediately fatal on its own. Recognizing which crisis type is unfolding changes your entire priority list.
Notify the Provider Immediately If
  • Signs of splenic sequestration: sudden pallor, lethargy, abdominal fullness/pain with signs of hypovolemia or shock
  • Signs of acute chest syndrome: chest pain, tachypnea, wheezing, new hypoxia — this can progress rapidly to respiratory failure
  • Any neurologic change (possible stroke — children with HbSS have elevated stroke risk and are monitored with annual transcranial doppler)
  • Fever of 38.5°C (101.3°F) or greater — Perry identifies this as the specific threshold requiring early intervention in a child with SCD, given markedly increased infection risk from progressive loss of splenic function (autosplenectomy)
  • Priapism lasting longer than a brief episode

Priority Nursing Interventions — Acuity Order

Mnemonic from lecture: "HOP to it!" — Hydration, Oxygenation, Pain control Lecture

  1. Will Kill First
    Recognize and immediately escalate suspected splenic sequestration or acute chest syndrome — these can be fatal within hours. Why: These crisis types progress to shock or respiratory failure faster than vaso-occlusive pain crises, and require immediate provider notification and intervention (transfusion, oxygen, respiratory support).
  2. Urgent
    Hydration — administer oral and/or IV fluids; electrolyte replacement as needed. Why: Adequate hydration is essential to reverse sickling (which is reversible with adequate oxygenation/hydration before cells become permanently sickled) and without it, pain will not be controlled no matter what analgesics are given.
  3. Urgent
    Oxygenation — administer oxygen and blood/exchange transfusions as prescribed to increase tissue perfusion and reduce circulating sickle cells. Why: Severe hypoxia can cause massive systemic sickling that can be fatal; oxygen alone often can't reverse sickling once vessels are clogged, but it prevents worsening.
  4. Important
    Pain control — administer analgesics around the clock (not PRN alone); PCA pumps commonly used. Position for comfort with extremities extended to promote venous return; avoid raising the knee gatch or straining painful joints; keep head of bed ≤30 degrees. Why: Pain is the most common crisis presentation, but comes after establishing hydration and oxygenation because pain control is far less effective without those first two measures in place.
  5. Important
    Antibiotic therapy if any infection is present or suspected — a documented crisis trigger and independently life-threatening given functional asplenia. Why: Infection both precipitates and complicates sickle cell crises, and functional asplenia leaves these children at markedly increased risk of overwhelming sepsis, particularly from encapsulated organisms.
Nursing Alert — Oxygen Therapy Nuance Oxygen therapy has little value unless the patient is actually hypoxic — it cannot reverse sickling or reduce pain by itself, because oxygen can't reach already-clogged microvasculature. Prolonged, unnecessary oxygen administration can also depress bone marrow and worsen anemia. This is a common point of confusion: oxygen is not automatically indicated just because the child is in crisis — assess for actual hypoxia first.
Critical myth to correct — opioid addiction Lecture It is a MYTH that children will become addicted to opioids if the medication is used properly to treat pain. Undertreating pain out of fear of addiction is a real and documented problem in sickle cell care — do not let this fear drive under-dosing of a child in a genuine vaso-occlusive crisis.

Treatment

  • Hydration (oral/IV), electrolyte replacement
  • Oxygen (if hypoxic) and blood/exchange transfusions
  • Around-the-clock analgesia; PCA pumps for significant pain Lecture
  • High-calorie, high-protein diet with folic acid supplementation (supports the high RBC turnover demand)
  • Hydroxyurea may be used to reduce crisis frequency — increases fetal hemoglobin (HbF) production, reduces sickle cell adhesion to blood vessel walls, improves cell hydration and size, increases nitric oxide (a vasodilator), and lowers white blood cell and reticulocyte counts; long-term use is associated with at least a 40% reduction in mortality and decreased frequency of vaso-occlusive crisis, acute chest syndrome, hospitalizations, and need for transfusions
  • Penicillin prophylaxis beginning at 2 months of age to decrease the risk of pneumococcal sepsis — an exact, testable age from lecture Lecture
Recommended Vaccines — Functional Asplenia Lecture

Children with sickle cell disease are at increased infection risk due to functional asplenia (the spleen progressively loses function from repeated infarction). Recommended vaccines include:

  • Haemophilus influenzae (Hib)
  • Pneumococcal vaccine
  • Varicella
  • Annual influenza and COVID-19 vaccines
  • Meningococcal vaccine

This full list matters because functional asplenia raises risk from all encapsulated organisms, not just one — a common exam trap is picking only the "obvious" pneumococcal vaccine and missing the rest.

Patient Education — Split by Audience & Developmental Stage

Parent / Caregiver Education
  • This is a chronic illness requiring lifelong management — set expectations accordingly Lecture
  • Teach signs/symptoms of sickle cell crisis and when to seek emergency care Lecture
  • Teach prevention of complications: adequate hydration is the single most actionable daily prevention strategy — encourage fluid intake 1.5 to 2 times the daily requirement Lecture
  • Avoid known precipitating factors where possible: extreme temperatures, high altitude, dehydration, overexertion, unnecessary stress
  • Keep the full recommended vaccine schedule current (Hib, pneumococcal, varicella, annual flu/COVID, meningococcal) and give prophylactic penicillin starting at 2 months of age as prescribed
  • Genetic counseling regarding recurrence risk in future pregnancies (25% risk if both parents carry the trait)
Pediatric / Child-Directed Education Diagnosed via newborn screening — education scales up as the child grows into self-management
  • Preschool/school-age (concrete thinkers): simple, concrete framing — "drinking lots of water helps your blood cells stay round and healthy" — avoid abstract hemoglobin biochemistry
  • School-age: can learn to recognize their own early pain crisis warning signs and know to tell an adult right away
  • Adolescent (formal operational): can understand the genetics and pathophysiology more fully; involve directly in self-management decisions (hydration habits, activity pacing, recognizing when to seek care) — critical since chronic illness self-management independence is a major developmental task of adolescence
  • Address psychosocial impact: chronic pain and repeated hospitalizations can affect school attendance and peer relationships at every age — validate this rather than only focusing on physical management

Self-Care Strategies

Ongoing disease management
  • Maintain consistent, generous fluid intake daily, not just during illness
  • Avoid known crisis triggers: dehydration, extreme cold/heat, high altitude, overexertion, unmanaged infection/fever
  • Take prophylactic penicillin and keep up with recommended vaccines
  • Know personal early-warning signs of a developing crisis and have a plan for when to call the provider vs. go to the ED
  • Keep regular follow-up appointments, including annual transcranial doppler screening for stroke risk in HbSS disease
  • Maintain a high-calorie, high-protein diet with folic acid supplementation as directed

Hemophilia

Hemophilia

X-linked recessive coagulation factor deficiency

Pathophysiology & Etiology

Hemophilia refers to a group of bleeding disorders resulting from a deficiency of specific coagulation proteins, so the coagulation process cannot be completed and bleeding is prolonged (not faster bleeding — longer bleeding). Bleeding tendencies range from mild to severe. Transmitted as an X-linked recessive disorder (can also arise from spontaneous gene mutation). The two most common types are Hemophilia A (Factor VIII deficiency, "classic hemophilia") and Hemophilia B (Factor IX deficiency, "Christmas disease").

Risk Factors
  • X-linked recessive inheritance — carrier females pass the defect to sons; daughters are rarely affected unless their father has hemophilia AND their mother is a carrier
  • Family history of hemophilia
  • Spontaneous gene mutation (no family history required)
Timing point from lecture Symptoms may not appear until around 6 months of age — often once the infant becomes mobile and starts experiencing falls and minor trauma. Before mobility, bleeding may first be noticed after circumcision. Lecture

Signs & Symptoms

Clinical manifestations
  • Abnormal bleeding in response to trauma or surgery (sometimes first detected after circumcision)
  • Epistaxis (nosebleeds)
  • Tendency to bruise easily
  • Mobility leads to injuries from falls and accidents once the child becomes active Lecture
Hemarthrosis — The Most Common Form of Internal Bleeding Lecture
Bleeding into joint spaces (knee, ankle, elbow)
  • Swelling
  • Warmth
  • Redness
  • Pain
  • Loss of movement/limited range of motion

Repeated hemarthrosis without aggressive treatment leads to chronic crippling joint effects over time — this is why prompt factor replacement after any joint bleed is emphasized.

Complications

  • Chronic joint damage/crippling effects from repeated hemarthrosis
  • Intracranial hemorrhage — the most dangerous complication, can occur in neonates and at any age with head trauma
  • Excessive bleeding with any surgery or invasive procedure if not properly prepared with factor replacement first

Labs & Diagnostics

TestPurpose
PT/PTTPTT is prolonged in hemophilia (intrinsic pathway factor deficiency); PT is typically normal Lecture

Notify the Provider Immediately If
  • Any head injury, even seemingly minor — risk of intracranial hemorrhage is the top safety concern
  • Joint swelling, warmth, or pain suggesting an evolving hemarthrosis — early factor replacement limits joint damage
  • Bleeding that does not stop with direct pressure
  • Signs of significant blood loss (pallor, tachycardia, weakness)

Priority Nursing Interventions

  1. Will Kill First
    Assess for and immediately report any signs of intracranial hemorrhage after head trauma (even minor) — altered LOC, severe headache, vomiting, pupil changes. Why: Intracranial bleeding is the most life-threatening complication of hemophilia and can progress rapidly without prompt factor replacement.
  2. Urgent
    Administer factor replacement (Factor VIII for Hemophilia A, Factor IX for Hemophilia B) promptly for any significant bleeding episode, per Saunders — prompt treatment prevents chronic complications. Why: The coagulation defect itself must be corrected before bleeding can be controlled; delay increases the risk of joint damage or more severe hemorrhage.
  3. Important
    Ensure patient safety and a safe environment to prevent trauma; provide close supervision, especially during mobile developmental stages. Lecture
  4. Important
    Bleeding management: distinguish superficial vs. significant bleeding to guide the intensity of the response. Lecture

Treatment

  • Primary treatment: replacement of the missing clotting factor (Factor VIII or IX) — IV administration
  • Parents are taught to administer factor via IV at home once the child is 2–3 years of age — a specific, testable milestone from lecture Lecture
  • Superficial bleeding: apply direct pressure for at least 15 minutes, plus RICE (Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation) Lecture
  • Significant bleeding: transfuse for factor replacement Lecture
  • Prompt treatment of any bleed to prevent chronic joint damage
Medication Orders to Question Lecture
  • Question any IM injection order — intramuscular injections risk causing a deep muscle hematoma in a child with a clotting factor deficiency; use subcutaneous or oral routes when possible (this includes routine immunizations like the MMR, which is normally given IM/SQ — clarify route and consider factor coverage first)
  • Question NSAID orders (e.g., Motrin/ibuprofen) — NSAIDs impair platelet function and increase bleeding risk; acetaminophen (Tylenol) is the safer choice for pain/fever
  • Applying ice to superficial cuts and using acetaminophen for pain are both appropriate, safe orders
NCLEX Application — From Lecture Slides Lecture

Scenario: Parents of a school-age child with hemophilia ask which sports are recommended.

Recommend (low contact/low trauma risk): Swimming, golf, bowling.

Avoid (high contact/collision risk): Soccer, basketball, wrestling, jogging, and tennis (running/pivoting sports still carry meaningful joint-trauma risk) — these carry significant risk of trauma and joint injury that could precipitate bleeding or hemarthrosis.

This reflects the broader teaching point: encourage activity and normal childhood participation, but steer toward non-contact, lower-trauma-risk options.

Medication Spotlight: Factor VIII & Factor IX Replacement

Hemophilia A (Factor VIII deficiency) and Hemophilia B (Factor IX deficiency) are both treated with replacement of the missing clotting factor.

Factor VIII
Parent / Caregiver Teaching

  • Give factor replacement exactly as prescribed
  • Learn bleeding-control procedures (direct pressure, RICE — see above)
  • Prevent injury and recognize signs of intracranial bleeding, the most dangerous complication
  • Maintain a safe home environment
  • Keep emergency and provider contact information easily accessible
Pediatric Patient Teaching By developmental stage
  • Toddler/preschooler: simple "bleeding medicine" explanation; emphasize safe play
  • School-age: teach safe sports choices and what to do immediately after an injury
  • Adolescent: encourage responsibility for recognizing bleeds and obtaining factor promptly, while maintaining appropriate adult supervision and support

Parent / Caregiver Teaching
  • Understand Hemophilia B = Factor IX deficiency
  • Teach bleeding precautions, injury prevention, and prompt treatment initiation
  • Watch especially for joint swelling, pain, warmth, or decreased movement (hemarthrosis)

  • School-age: teach recognition of early joint bleeding signs
  • Adolescent: promote increasing independence with treatment planning and emergency preparation

Patient Education — Split by Audience & Developmental Stage

Parent / Caregiver Education
  • Prevent bleeding: childproof the home, especially once the child is mobile; pad sharp furniture corners Lecture
  • Monitor for signs/symptoms of a cerebral bleed after any head trauma, even minor Lecture
  • Close supervision and a safe environment, balanced with allowing normal activity Lecture
  • Recommend non-contact sports (swimming, golf, bowling) over contact/collision sports (soccer, basketball, wrestling)
  • Teach factor replacement administration via IV — most families begin home administration once the child reaches 2–3 years of age Lecture
  • Teach the RICE protocol plus 15 minutes of direct pressure for any superficial bleeding Lecture
  • Always inform any new provider, dentist, or school nurse of the diagnosis before procedures, injections, or medication administration
  • Genetic counseling regarding inheritance pattern for future pregnancies and extended family

  • Toddler/preschool: caregiver-directed; begin simple safety language ("we're careful so we don't get owies that don't stop")
  • School-age (concrete thinkers): teach to recognize joint swelling/warmth as a sign to tell an adult immediately; involve in choosing safe activities they enjoy
  • Adolescent: can understand the genetics and manage more independence around activity choices and recognizing early bleed signs; address the emotional weight of being excluded from popular contact sports and help problem-solve alternatives that still allow social participation

Self-Care Strategies

Ongoing management
  • Know how to recognize early hemarthrosis signs (joint warmth, tingling, stiffness before overt swelling) and seek treatment immediately
  • Maintain a safe home/activity environment without becoming overly restrictive
  • Carry medical identification (bracelet) noting the specific factor deficiency
  • Keep factor replacement product accessible/know the emergency treatment plan, especially for older children/teens with more independence
  • Choose lower-contact recreational activities while still supporting normal social and physical development

Neutropenia

Neutropenia

Low absolute neutrophil count — infection risk defined by a number

Pathophysiology & Etiology

Neutropenia is defined by the absolute neutrophil count (ANC): Lecture

When the count drops below these thresholds, the child loses a major line of immune defense — common causes in this unit's context include chemotherapy-induced bone marrow suppression, but neutropenia can also result from infection, autoimmune destruction, or bone marrow disorders.

Signs & Symptoms

Clinical manifestations
  • Fever
  • Lymphadenopathy
  • Cold symptoms
  • Fatigue
  • Mouth sores
  • Rashes
  • Generalized feeling of illness
The single most important safety concept in this entire condition Fever is a medical emergency in a neutropenic child. Because the neutrophils that would normally produce a robust local inflammatory response are absent, fever may be the only sign of a serious, potentially life-threatening infection. A neutropenic child with fever requires blood cultures and prompt antibiotics — this cannot wait. Lecture

Knowledge Check — Neutropenic Precautions Lecture

Correct neutropenic precautions: no fresh flowers or live plants in the room; practice gentle oral care with a soft toothbrush; no visitors who are currently experiencing an illness.

Incorrect/distractors to catch: a neutropenic child should NOT share a room with another child on neutropenic precautions — cohorting two immunocompromised children together does not protect either one and can actually increase mutual exposure risk. A "salami sandwich with a fresh apple" is also incorrect — deli meat and unwashed fresh fruit both carry bacterial/mold risk during neutropenia and should follow institutional neutropenic diet protocol instead (cooked meats, thoroughly washed or cooked produce).

Knowledge Check — Common Transfusion Reactions Lecture

All of the following are recognized transfusion reactions and should be on your radar:

  • TRALI (Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury) — a serious, potentially life-threatening reaction causing acute respiratory distress, typically within 6 hours of transfusion
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Fever and chills
  • Itching with a rash or hives

Reactions span a wide severity range from mild (itching) to life-threatening (TRALI, hemolytic reaction, anaphylaxis) — the nursing response (stop, monitor vitals, call the provider) is the same first sequence regardless of type, but the following provider-directed workup and treatment differs by severity and reaction type.

Priority Nursing Interventions

  1. Will Kill First
    At any sign of a transfusion reaction (fever, chills, hives, back pain, hypotension, dyspnea), stop the transfusion immediately, keep the line open with normal saline, monitor vitals, and call the provider. Why: Hemolytic transfusion reactions and anaphylaxis can progress to shock and death within minutes if the infusion continues.
  2. Urgent
    Ensure blood hangs within 30 minutes of receipt and completes within 4 hours; monitor continuously during the first 15 minutes. Why: Blood products degrade and become an infection risk outside these windows, and most severe reactions present early.
  3. Important
    Complete post-transfusion documentation and retain the bag for the required observation period.

Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL)

Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia

Most common cancer in children — overproduction of immature WBCs

Pathophysiology & Etiology

Leukemia is a malignant increase in leukocytes, usually at an immature stage (blast cells), that infiltrate and depress the bone marrow. This bone marrow suppression causes the classic triad of consequences: anemia (decreased erythrocyte production), infection risk (neutropenia), and bleeding (thrombocytopenia from decreased platelet production). ALL is the most common cancer in children, affects mostly immature, undifferentiated cells, and is an overproduction of WBCs. Peak age of onset is 2–3 years old. The exact cause is unknown, involving genetic damage transforming normal cells to a malignant state.

Risk Factors
  • Genetic, viral, immunological, and environmental factors
  • Exposure to radiation, certain chemicals, and medications
  • Down syndrome (increased risk)
  • More common in boys; more common in Hispanic children than non-Hispanic white children; more common in white than African American children

Signs & Symptoms

Classic presentation
  • Vague, flu-like feelings
  • Fever (61% of patients)
  • Pallor, fatigue, anorexia
  • Bone and joint pain (23% of patients; from bone marrow infiltration); pathological fractures possible
  • Hepatosplenomegaly (68%), splenomegaly (63%), lymphadenopathy (50%)
  • Easy bruising, petechiae, or purpura (48%)
  • Abnormal blood counts — approximately half of patients have an elevated WBC count (>10,000/mm³) at presentation
More severe/advanced findings
  • "Blueberry muffin" lesions (extramedullary hematopoiesis in skin) Lecture
  • Signs and labs consistent with DIC Lecture
  • CNS involvement (rare at diagnosis, usually asymptomatic if present)
Link the pathophysiology to the symptom: Anemia → decreased RBCs → pale, tired. Infection → decreased WBCs (neutropenia) → fever. Bleeding → decreased platelets → bruising, petechiae, nosebleeds. Bone pain → leukemic cell infiltration increasing pressure within bone → unable to bear weight. This symptom-to-mechanism mapping is exactly how Saunders NGN case studies test this content.

Complications

  • Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC): can accompany leukemia, especially with sepsis in the immunocompromised child — excessive microthrombi formation throughout the vasculature (hyperactivated clotting cascade, impaired fibrinolysis) paradoxically leaves the child susceptible to severe hemorrhage as platelets and clotting factors are consumed. Platelet count < 20,000/mm³ signals high bleeding risk.
  • Tumor lysis syndrome: a metabolic emergency from rapid lysis of malignant cells (especially with a high WBC count at diagnosis or early in chemotherapy) releasing intracellular contents (potassium, phosphate, uric acid) faster than the body can clear them — risking cardiac and renal complications.
  • Hyperleukocytosis (WBC > 100,000/mm³): can cause capillary obstruction, microinfarction, organ dysfunction, respiratory distress, and neurologic changes.
  • Long-term: infertility, secondary malignancies, organ system late effects (see Cancer Overview above).

    Monitor for and prevent bleeding: use soft toothbrushes, avoid IM injections/rectal temps/suppositories, apply prolonged pressure to puncture sites, monitor platelet counts. Why: Thrombocytopenia places the child at ongoing bleeding risk that requires proactive prevention, not just reaction.
  3. Important
    Support chemotherapy administration across its three phases and manage expected side effects (nausea, mucositis) while distinguishing them from true toxicity requiring medical evaluation.

Neuroblastoma

Neuroblastoma

Tumor of sympathetic nervous system origin — average age 2 years

Pathophysiology & Etiology

The most common extracranial solid tumor of childhood, arising from embryonic neural crest cells (neuroblasts) that normally give rise to the adrenal medulla and sympathetic nervous system. A disease of infancy and early childhood — median age at diagnosis is approximately 19 months. Most commonly develops in the abdomen (often originating in the adrenal medulla), but can also arise in the neck, chest, or pelvis wherever sympathetic nervous tissue is present. Lecture

Neuroblastoma is a "silent" tumor In more than 70% of cases, diagnosis is not made until after metastasis has already occurred — the first signs are often caused by involvement at a distant site (lymph nodes, bone marrow, skeletal system, liver) rather than the primary tumor itself. This "silent" behavior is a major reason neuroblastoma can carry a poor prognosis despite aggressive treatment, and explains why a seemingly unrelated presenting complaint (bone pain, anemia, a skin nodule) can be the first clue.

Signs & Symptoms

Clinical manifestations — vary by location
  • Palpable abdominal mass — a key distinguishing feature: this mass is firm, nontender, irregular, and crosses the midline (unlike Wilms tumor, below)
  • Abdominal pain/discomfort, vomiting, anorexia; urinary frequency or retention if the kidney/ureter/bladder is compressed
  • Thoracic/cervical tumors: dyspnea, Horner syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis), neck mass, stridor, dysphagia
  • Spinal/brain involvement: neurologic deficits, bladder/bowel dysfunction, paraparesis, paraplegia, seizures
  • Bone marrow infiltration: anemia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia
  • Orbital/optic nerve involvement: exophthalmos, periorbital ecchymosis, impaired vision
  • "Blueberry muffin" skin lesions — bluish subcutaneous nodules from skin metastases, seen almost exclusively in infants
  • Bone pain or limping if skeletal metastasis is present
  • Edema of lower extremities if the tumor is severe/compressing venous or lymphatic return
Neuroblastoma vs. Wilms tumor — the defining exam distinction: Neuroblastoma mass crosses the midline. Wilms tumor mass does NOT cross the midline (it originates in one kidney). This single detail is a classic NCLEX discriminator between the two most common pediatric abdominal tumors.

Wilms Tumor

Wilms Tumor (Nephroblastoma)

Kidney-origin tumor — favorable prognosis

Pathophysiology & Etiology

Originates in one or both kidneys. Average age at diagnosis is between 2 and 5 years. Both sexes are equally affected. 5-year survival rate is approximately 90% — one of the more favorable pediatric solid tumor prognoses. Lecture

Signs & Symptoms

Clinical manifestations
  • Palpable abdominal mass that does NOT cross the midline (contrast with neuroblastoma above) — firm, nontender, confined to one side, deep within the flank; often first discovered incidentally during routine bathing or dressing
  • Painless in most cases — abdominal pain is present in only about 40% of patients
  • Hematuria — occurs in less than 25% of children
  • Hypertension — occurs in about 25% of children, likely from excess renin secretion by the tumor
  • Anemia (from hemorrhage within the tumor) causing pallor, anorexia, lethargy
  • Fever in about 10% of cases
  • Respiratory symptoms (dyspnea, cough, chest pain) if pulmonary metastasis has occurred
Associated congenital anomalies to ask about When Wilms tumor is suspected, history and physical exam should specifically screen for aniridia (absence of the iris), hemihypertrophy, developmental delay, hypospadias, and cryptorchidism, along with any family history of cancer — several genetic syndromes carry increased Wilms tumor risk.
The single most important safety instruction for this diagnosis "No abdominal palpation" — post a sign above the bed and communicate this to all staff and family. Palpating a Wilms tumor risks rupturing the tumor capsule, which can seed cancer cells throughout the abdominal cavity and change the child's staging and prognosis dramatically for the worse. This is one of the highest-yield safety facts in all of pediatric oncology. Lecture

  2. Urgent
    Monitor blood pressure closely (tumor may secrete excess renin); measure abdominal girth at least once daily preoperatively to track any change in mass size. Why: Both hypertension and a rapidly enlarging mass can signal disease progression or an impending complication that needs prompt provider notification.
  3. Urgent
    Postoperatively: monitor temperature and blood pressure closely; watch for signs of hemorrhage and infection; monitor strict intake and urine output. Why: Tracks renal function and any post-surgical changes in the remaining kidney tissue/function, and catches early signs of surgical complications.
  4. Important
    Monitor for abdominal distention, bowel sounds, and other signs of GI activity given the risk of postoperative intestinal obstruction; support chemotherapy, post-radiation care, and surgical recovery.

Treatment

  • Surgical removal of the mass (often nephrectomy)
  • Chemotherapy and post-radiation care as indicated Lecture

  • Do not palpate the child's abdomen at home and inform any other caregivers (grandparents, daycare staff) of this restriction as well

Grief & Loss — Palliative Care vs. Hospice

Palliative Care vs. Hospice

Two distinct but related philosophies of care

Principles of Palliative Care Lecture

  • Multidisciplinary team involved
  • Goal: best quality of life for the client and their care providers/family
  • Focus on symptom control
  • May begin at the onset of diagnosis — palliative care is not exclusively end-of-life care, and can run alongside curative treatment

How Hospice Differs Lecture

  • Death is treated as a normal part of life
  • Focus on pain control and holistic needs
  • Death is expected within 6 months, with physician certification required
Key distinction: Palliative care = symptom management that can start at diagnosis and run parallel to curative treatment. Hospice = specifically for an expected 6-month prognosis, physician-certified, with a holistic comfort-focused approach once curative treatment has stopped. All hospice care is palliative, but not all palliative care is hospice.

Ethical Principles Involved in Death Lecture

  • Caregivers may struggle significantly with the decision to withhold or withdraw medical care
  • Not offering a treatment that could cause pain but only extend life is ethically acceptable — an important principle for supporting families through these decisions without judgment
Definitions to Know — Do Not Confuse These Lecture
TermDefinition
Assisted SuicideProviding the means for a client to end their own life — the client performs the final act themselves
EuthanasiaAn act carried out by another person to end someone else's life

The key distinction is who performs the final act — the patient (assisted suicide) versus another person (euthanasia). This is distinct from withholding/withdrawing treatment, which is a separate, generally ethically accepted practice under the principle above.

Grief & Loss — The Dying Child

The Dying Child — Honesty & Communication

Children are perceptive — they know something is different

Core Principle Lecture

  • Children need honest and accurate information about their illness, treatment, and prognosis
  • Information must be given in clear, simple language at the child's level of understanding
  • Many parents request that the child not be told they are dying — but children are highly perceptive and will sense that something is different, even without being told directly
  • Nurses help parents understand that honesty and shared decision-making with the child support the emotional health of both the child and the parents
  • Toddlers and preschoolers may show signs of regression (e.g., returning to earlier behaviors like thumb-sucking or bedwetting) as a stress response
Why honesty matters clinically, not just ethically When children sense something is wrong but are not given honest information, they often construct their own explanation — which is frequently worse or more frightening than the truth, and can include the child blaming themselves. Age-appropriate honesty actually reduces anxiety compared to secrecy.

Grief & Loss — Perceptions of Death by Stage

Perceptions of Death by Developmental Stage & Interventions

The single most testable content block in this unit

Infant Lecture

  • Minimal impact of death concept before 6 months of age
  • Will feel profound loss of a parent if trust has already been established (attachment-driven, not concept-driven)
  • Interventions: Ensure needs are met; caregiver support/consistency

Toddler Lecture

  • Senses something is wrong through parental reactions/emotional cues
  • Knows death means separation from parents but cannot comprehend permanence or absence fully
  • May act as though the deceased person is still alive
  • Interventions: Mostly affected by change in routine, so try to keep routines consistent; consistent caregivers are important

Preschool Lecture

  • Sees death as reversible (magical thinking — a defining preoperational cognitive feature)
  • May think death is a punishment for bad behavior
  • Greatest fear is parental separation
  • May be in denial; may show inappropriate emotions
  • Interventions: Keep routines and a consistent caregiver; provide parental support and explain the child's behaviors to the parents (so parents understand regression/denial is developmentally normal, not a sign of not caring)

School Age Lecture

  • Understands death is final (a major cognitive shift from preschool)
  • May still feel the death is somehow their fault
  • May personify death
  • Wants details of death; interested in funeral services
  • Greatest fear is the unknown
  • May become obsessed with death
  • Interventions: Allow choices so the child feels in control; encourage expression of feelings; assist parents in answering the child's questions about death honestly

Adolescent Lecture

Perceptions of Death
  • Thinks they are invincible
  • Has a mature idea of death but believes it will not happen to them personally
  • Least likely age group to accept death, especially their own
  • Will feel alone and isolated
  • May fear physical changes more than the prognosis itself
  • May criticize funeral services
Interventions
  • Allow time to say goodbye to friends; journaling
  • Talk about the loss of goals and dreams
  • Allow for maximum control and independence
  • Respect privacy and allow expression of emotions
The developmental arc in one line: Infant (attachment only) → Toddler (senses wrongness, no permanence) → Preschool (reversible, magical thinking, self-blame) → School-age (final, wants details, fears the unknown) → Adolescent (understands fully but resists accepting it, fears loss of independence/future more than death itself). Matching the right intervention to the right stage is exactly what NCLEX tests here.

Grief & Loss — Signs of Impending Death

Signs of Impending Death

Physical changes in the actively dying child

Signs Lecture

Circulatory/Skin

Loss of sensation and movement in the lower extremities, progressing toward the upper body; skin feels cool, but the child may report a sensation of heat; cyanosis possible; weak, slow pulse; decreased blood pressure

Elimination/Intake

Loss of bowel and bladder control; decreased appetite and thirst; difficulty swallowing

Respiratory

Cheyne-Stokes respirations (waxing/waning depth of breathing with regular apnea periods); "death rattle" — noisy chest sounds from accumulated pulmonary/pharyngeal secretions

Neurologic/Sensory

Confusion, loss of consciousness, slurred speech, muscle weakness; senses fail in order — tactile sensation and light sensitivity decrease first, with hearing being the last sense to fail; changes in pain perception

Clinical takeaway — why hearing matters most here Because hearing is believed to be the last sense to fail, continue talking to the child and encourage the family to do the same, even once the child appears unresponsive to touch or sight. This directly supports the "encourage talking with the child" nursing principle in the next section.

Priority Nursing Interventions

  1. Important
    Recognize these signs as expected physiologic changes at end of life, not events requiring aggressive resuscitative intervention (unless the family's goals of care direct otherwise). Why: Understanding these as the normal dying process — rather than treatable emergencies — allows the nursing focus to shift fully to comfort and family support, matching the family's established goals of care.
  2. Important
    Continue comfort measures and appropriate pain management even as perception and vital signs change, since pain perception itself is altered but comfort needs remain a priority.

Grief & Loss — Nursing Care of the Dying Child

Nursing Care of the Dying Child & Family

Before, during, and after death

General Nursing Care Principles Lecture

  • Allow parents to hold and visit as long as possible
  • Provide a peaceful environment and privacy
  • Encourage talking with the child, even if the child appears unresponsive — hearing is often preserved late
  • Use a team approach; stay consistent with words and actions across all staff
  • Ask questions and validate the family's care decisions rather than second-guessing them
  • Bathe the child, brush hair, dress them — dignity-preserving comfort measures
  • Be present
  • Provide appropriate pain management
  • Support the child's right to die with dignity
  • Stop any unnecessary treatments once goals of care shift to comfort
  • Do not share personal loss stories with the family — this centers the nurse's experience rather than the family's, which is not therapeutic

Interventions Before Death Lecture

  • Comfort measures: clean bedding, soft touch, keep the child clean/dry/warm, provide a favorite toy
  • Pain management via any appropriate route: PO, IV, IM, subcutaneous, or through a G-tube — the route should match what the child can tolerate and what provides the most comfort, not a one-size-fits-all approach Lecture
  • Communication: create a peaceful environment, let the family be alone with the child, turn down lights, decrease noise, keep the family informed

Interventions After Death Lecture

  • Contact the bereavement team
  • Provide community resources for the family
  • Create a ledger/keepsake of important times in the child's life
  • If death was unexpected: describe the child's appearance, any injuries, the position of the child, and what occurred at the moment of death before the family enters the room, to reduce shock Lecture
  • Give the family the choice of seeing the child alone or with a healthcare provider present
  • Let the parents stay with the child as long as they need
  • Treat the child's belongings with respect — never place them in a plastic bag — use a proper bag/box, since a plastic garbage-style bag communicates carelessness at the worst possible moment for a grieving family Lecture
  • Support the family in saying goodbye
  • Support the family through the grieving and coping process going forward, including awareness of the Kubler-Ross stages

Priority Nursing Interventions — Sequenced

  1. Important
    Before death: prioritize comfort measures and a peaceful, private environment for the family. Why: This is the family's last time together with their child; environment and comfort directly shape how this experience is remembered.
  2. Important
    At the time of death: give the family control over how they experience the moment (alone vs. with HCP present) and prepare them gently if the death was unexpected. Why: Preserving family autonomy and reducing shock supports healthier grief processing afterward.
  3. Important
    After death: connect the family with the bereavement team and community resources promptly, since ongoing support needs continue well beyond the moment of death.

Grief & Loss — Communication Strategies

Communication Strategies

What to say and what never to say

Saying Goodbye Lecture

  • Allow time
  • Call the child by their given name — a small but meaningful act of respect and personhood
  • Allow time for the family to be alone
Don't Say
  • "You can have another child"
  • "It could have been worse" or "I have seen worse"
  • "You don't want him to live like that"
  • "You should be feeling better by now"
  • "I know how you feel"
  • "Your child isn't suffering anymore" (offering rationalizations for the death)

These minimize the loss, offer artificial consolation, or impose the speaker's judgment onto the family's grief.

Do Say
  • "I'm sorry"
  • "Is there someone I can call for you?"
  • "Do you want me to stay with you?"
  • "You're still feeling all the pain of losing a child" (focus on feelings by naming the feeling directly)
  • "We will miss him so much" / "We tried everything; we feel so sorry we couldn't save her" (expressing genuine personal feelings of loss is appropriate and human)

These validate the loss and offer concrete, actionable support without minimizing. Perry emphasizes that the single most valuable thing a nurse can do is listen — families understand no words will relieve their pain; they want acceptance, understanding, and respect for their grief.

Perry's Nursing Care Guidelines — By Timing

At the Time of Death
  • Reassure the family that everything possible is being done, if they want life-saving interventions
  • Do everything possible to ensure the child's comfort, especially relieving pain
  • Provide the opportunity to review special memories
  • Provide honest information the family requests
  • Respect the emotional needs of siblings, who may need brief respite from the dying child
  • Arrange for family, especially parents, to be present at the moment of death if desired
  • Allow the family to stay with the child as long as they wish, and to rock, hold, or bathe the child
  • Arrange spiritual support based on the family's beliefs
After Death (Post-Death Follow-Up)
  • Attend the funeral or visitation if there was a special closeness with the family
  • Initiate and maintain contact afterward — cards, calls, inviting them back to the unit, a home visit
  • Refer to the child by name; discuss shared memories
  • Discourage using drugs/alcohol to escape grief
  • Encourage every family member to communicate their feelings rather than staying silent to protect others
  • Emphasize that grieving is a painful process that often takes years, not weeks, to resolve
Complicated grief — know the definition Grief reactions persisting more than 1 year after the loss — including intense intrusive thoughts, severe emotional pangs, distressing yearning, excessive loneliness/emptiness, unusual sleep disturbance, and maladaptive loss of interest in personal activities — are considered complicated grief and warrant referral to a grief/bereavement counseling specialist.

Source: Unit 10 Grief and Loss lecture slide 11; Perry Ch. 36 (Nursing Care Guidelines: Supporting Grieving Families; complicated grief definition).

What Each Stage Actually Looks Like

StageWhat You'll See
Denial & IsolationShock, disbelief; may include physician shopping, attributing symptoms to a minor condition, refusing to believe test results, delaying consent for treatment, acting optimistic despite the diagnosis, or asking no questions at all about diagnosis/treatment/prognosis. This is a necessary, adaptive cushion against being overwhelmed — not automatically a problem to "fix." It only becomes maladaptive when it prevents necessary treatment or rehabilitation decisions.
AngerMay be directed at staff (complaints about nursing care, physicians "not spending enough time," perceived lack of skill during procedures) or at other family members. Affected children and well siblings can also show anger — siblings especially may resent the loss of routine/parental attention.
BargainingAttempts to negotiate more time or a better outcome, often through promises (to God, to oneself) in exchange for a different result.
DepressionSadness, withdrawal, hopelessness as the reality of the loss sets in.
AcceptanceOpen acknowledgment that the condition/loss exists; for families of a chronically ill child, this often looks like gradual acceptance — placing realistic restrictions on the child, encouraging self-care, and promoting reasonable physical/social participation, rather than a single dramatic moment of "being okay."
Important nuance for exam application These stages are not strictly linear — people (both dying patients and grieving family members) can move back and forth between stages, skip stages, or experience more than one simultaneously. Nursing care should meet the person wherever they currently are, rather than expecting or pushing progression through the stages in order. Note also that guilt and self-accusation are described separately by Perry as nearly universal accompanying reactions, especially when a parent feels any traceable responsibility (e.g., a genetic condition or an accidental injury) — even when no realistic basis for that responsibility exists.

Source: Unit 10 Grief and Loss lecture slide 13 (Kubler-Ross, 1983); Perry Ch. 36 (Shock and Denial, Adjustment sections).

Patient Education — Split by Audience & Developmental Stage

Parent / Caregiver Education
  • Support honesty with the dying child rather than protective secrecy — explain why this benefits both the child and the family's own emotional health
  • Prepare parents for developmentally normal reactions in siblings/the dying child (e.g., preschool regression, adolescent anger or withdrawal) so these aren't misread as behavioral problems
  • Teach what to expect physically as death approaches (signs of impending death) so the family isn't caught off guard
  • Connect families with palliative care, hospice, bereavement, and community resources as early as appropriate — not only in the final days
  • Offer grief education: normalize the non-linear nature of the Kubler-Ross stages, so family members don't feel they are "grieving wrong" if they don't move neatly through each stage
Pediatric / Child-Directed Education Tailor communication about illness, death, and loss precisely to developmental stage — see full breakdown above
  • Infant: no verbal education; meet needs and maintain caregiver consistency
  • Toddler: simple words, maintain routines, consistent caregivers
  • Preschool: concrete, simple explanations; correct magical thinking gently (e.g., that the death is not the child's fault) without overwhelming detail
  • School-age: honest, detailed answers to their specific questions; allow choices to restore a sense of control; they may want to know funeral details — this is normal, not morbid
  • Adolescent: honest, direct communication respecting their formal-operational understanding; allow maximum independence and privacy; support their need to say goodbye to friends and process loss of future plans/goals

Self-Care Strategies for the Family

Supporting the family's own coping
  • Accept support from the bereavement team and community resources rather than trying to manage grief in isolation
  • Allow each family member (including siblings) to grieve in their own way and pace, without judgment
  • Keep tangible memory-keeping items (the "ledger" of important times, mementos, photos) as a healthy part of the grieving process
  • Recognize that grief is not linear and revisiting stages like anger or sadness later is normal, not a setback
  • Seek professional grief counseling support if coping becomes overwhelming or prolonged in a way that impairs daily functioning
