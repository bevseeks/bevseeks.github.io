Classification of High-Risk Newborns

Defining & Classifying High-Risk

Defining "High-Risk"
▸

High-risk neonate: regardless of gestational age or birth weight, has a greater chance of morbidity/mortality. Period of viability = 20 weeks gestation through 28 days after birth.

Major categories: prematurity, birth trauma, infection, maternal substance abuse, congenital anomalies.

Classifying High-Risk Newborns via Screening Tools (USLO 1)
▸

Every high-risk newborn is classified along TWO independent axes: gestational age AND growth/weight for that gestational age. Both must be assessed together — an infant can be preterm AND large for gestational age, for example (as with many IDM infants).

Care of the High-Risk Newborn & Family

NICU Levels & Developmental Care

NICU Levels of Care
▸
Classification
  • Level I: well-newborn nursery; can stabilize a high-risk newborn for transport but does not manage ongoing high-risk care
  • Level II: everything Level I provides, plus care for premature infants, oxygen via hood, and IV therapy initiation
  • Level III (NICU): the most sophisticated level — provides mechanical ventilation and comprehensive intensive care
Why this matters clinically

Recognizing which level of care an infant needs (and whether the current facility can provide it) drives transport decisions. An infant requiring ventilation at a Level I or II facility needs transfer to a Level III NICU.

Developmental & Environmental Care
▸
Why this matters

The NICU environment is inherently overstimulating compared to the intrauterine environment the infant left. Excessive noise, uncontrolled lighting, and frequent handling are linked to real physiologic harm — including a correlation between noise/overstimulation and intracranial hemorrhage risk in ELBW/VLBW infants. Developmental care aims to make the NICU environment as close to a smooth continuation of intrauterine conditions as possible.

Protected sleep
  • Newborns should not be awakened for routine care — care is timed around natural wake periods whenever possible
  • Sleep periods should remain undisturbed for at least 50 minutes to allow a complete sleep cycle
  • Sleep is essential for brain development and recovery/growth in a fragile, immature newborn
Cluster care

All nursing care tasks for a given time period (vitals, diaper change, feeding, assessment) are grouped together and performed at once, rather than disturbing the infant repeatedly throughout the shift. This minimizes the number of stimulation/wake episodes and reduces the cumulative stress burden on an infant who has limited ability to self-regulate.

Noise & lighting control
  • Reduce noise-generating activity: close incubator portholes gently, avoid loud talking/radios near the bedside, handle equipment quietly
  • Establish a day-night cycle: dim/darken the room at night, cover incubators with blankets, use eye shields as appropriate to protect from bright procedure lighting
  • Recommended ambient lighting is generally 10-600 lux, with brief exceptions up to 2,000 lux for specific procedures
  • The parent's face is considered one of the best visual stimuli for a developing infant — other visual stimulation should be minimized, especially before 33 weeks gestation
Gestational-age-specific toleranceThe more immature the infant, the less able they are to tolerate even a single routine procedure (like repositioning) without becoming overstimulated. Before ~33 weeks gestation, external stimulation can produce disorganized, uncoordinated responses: jerky limb extension, hyperflexion, and irregular vital signs — this is a sign the nervous system is overwhelmed, not a normal response to be pushed through.
Reading behavioral cues

Nurses and parents learn to read infant cues in two categories:

  • Approach/engagement behaviors (support and encourage these): tongue extension, hand clasping, hand-to-mouth movements, sucking, looking, cooing
  • Avoidance/stress behaviors (signal need for a "time-out" from stimulation): gaze aversion, splayed fingers, grimacing, color changes, apnea/bradycardia — see the full stability-vs-distress cue table in the Transition tab, Unit 9, for the complete list
How to introduce developmental interventions
  1. Only begin structured developmental stimulation once the infant is medically stable, off significant support, and able to self-regulate.
  2. Introduce one type of stimulation at a time (e.g., 5 minutes of quiet music OR gentle voice, not both at once) to clearly observe tolerance.
  3. Hearing and vestibular (movement/positioning) stimulation are generally introduced before visual stimulation.
  4. Keep intervention periods brief (2-5 minutes) and stop immediately if stress cues appear.
  5. Softly say the infant's name and place a hand gently on the body before beginning any care — this signals the start of handling and reduces the startle of abrupt contact.

Skin, Nutrition & Pain Management

Skin Care
▸
Why preterm skin is uniquely vulnerable

Preterm skin lacks rete pegs — the structures that normally anchor the epidermis to the dermis — so there is less cohesion between skin layers than in a term infant. This makes the skin thinner, more permeable, and easily excoriated or denuded. Skin barrier properties typically don't resemble a term infant's until 2-4 weeks postnatal age, regardless of gestational age at birth — meaning even a very preterm infant's skin eventually "catches up," but is acutely fragile in the interim.

Where skin breakdown happens & why it matters

Common sites: where tubes/leads contact skin (nose, behind the ears), and the occipital region from extended supine positioning — this can progress to actual pressure ulcers. Any skin breakdown increases nosocomial (hospital-acquired) infection risk, which is why skin assessment is a genuine infection-prevention intervention, not just comfort care.

Bathing

Routine bathing is not done daily in high-risk newborns — typically every third day — to avoid drying the fragile skin further. Plain water is used instead of soap for very preterm infants; alkaline-based soaps are avoided since the skin's acid mantle is protective against infection.

Adhesives & topical products
  • Use minimal adhesive tape; back tape with a skin barrier or hydrocolloid dressing, and delay removal until adherence has naturally reduced
  • Remove tape/adhesive with warm water only — avoid solvents/isopropyl alcohol, which dry and chemically burn fragile skin
  • Pectin barriers and hydrocolloid adhesives mold well to skin contours and adhere better in moist conditions — preferred over standard tape
  • All skin products (alcohol, chlorhexidine, povidone-iodine) must be used cautiously and rinsed off with water afterward in VLBW/ELBW infants, since increased skin permeability facilitates absorption of whatever is applied
  • Emollients (e.g., Eucerin, Aquaphor) reduce transepidermal water loss and prevent cracking/peeling, but some studies link them to increased coagulase-negative infection risk in preterm infants — use per unit protocol, not routinely
Minimizing transepidermal water loss (infants <30 weeks)
  • Apply an occlusive polyethylene body bag immediately at birth, removed once the infant is stabilized in the NICU
  • Consider a humidified incubator at 80% humidity for the first 7 days, gradually decreasing thereafter
  • Use supplemental conductive heat and reduce radiant heat exposure
  • Semipermeable transparent dressings can be applied to vulnerable skin surfaces
Preventing & treating skin breakdown
  • Prevention: decrease external pressure with water/air/gel mattresses or cotton bedding; ensure adequate protein, fat, and zinc intake; use barrier cream in the diaper area
  • Treating a wound: irrigate with warm diluted or normal saline; transparent adhesive dressing for uninfected wounds; hydrogel for infected wounds; hydrocolloid for deep uninfected wounds or as an ostomy barrier; avoid antiseptic solutions on open wounds (intact skin only)
  • Diaper dermatitis: keep skin clean/dry with frequent changes; mild irritation → petrolatum or zinc oxide barrier; developing dermatitis → generous zinc-oxide barrier, remove only soiled matter and leave the barrier in place; Candida → antifungal ointment; avoid powders and antibiotic ointments
Watch for zinc deficiency & notify provider ifSubtle skin breakdown around the mouth, buttocks, fingers/toes (or in preterm/VLBW infants, the neck creases, wrists, ankles, and around wounds) can indicate zinc deficiency — more likely with inadequate zinc intake, an ileostomy, short-bowel syndrome, or chronic diarrhea. Report suspicious lesions so zinc supplementation can be considered. Also notify for: any worsening pressure ulcer classification (warrants prompt physician consultation), or skin injury from phototherapy blanket use.
Exam pearl: Preterm skin lacks rete pegs → think "less glue holding layers together" → tape removal is a genuine injury risk, not just an annoyance. Water over soap, patience over force.
Nutrition & Metabolic Support
▸
Why nutrition is uniquely difficult in this population

The high-risk newborn has increased metabolic needs for growth, but immature ingestion/digestion mechanisms make meeting those needs harder — the more immature the infant, the greater the challenge. Critically, the suck-swallow-breathe coordination needed for safe oral feeding doesn't develop until ~32-34 weeks and isn't fully synchronized until 36-37 weeks; before that, sucking isn't accompanied by coordinated swallowing, and infants are at real aspiration risk.

Supporting breastfeeding for a preterm/high-risk infant

Mothers are taught to pump at regular intervals to maintain supply before the infant can feed directly at the breast; pumped milk can be given by bottle or syringe in the meantime. If the infant can't yet latch, hand-expression and spoon- or syringe-feeding are alternatives. Nonnutritive sucking (NNS) on a pacifier is promoted for both physiologic and psychological benefit, and may itself help promote later breastfeeding success.

Formula considerations

Preterm formula has increased protein, calcium, phosphorus, and medium-chain triglycerides compared to standard term formula. When preparing formula for a high-risk newborn, use a clean surface, prepare only one feeding at a time, and label properly — this is a stricter standard than routine home formula prep given the population's vulnerability.

Calculating intake & output

High-risk newborns are maintained on strict intake and output (I&O) until growing adequately. Actual intake is calculated on total daily calories (kcal/kg/day) and fluid requirements (mL/kg/day).

Lab monitoring

Mineral levels and hemoglobin are checked frequently; supplements may be added to prevent mineral/nutrient deficiencies.

Priority monitoring (ranked)
  1. Frequent temperature and blood glucose checks — both directly affect the infant's physiologic stress load, and cold stress specifically depletes glucose reserves, so keeping both in normal range reduces overall metabolic demand.
  2. Daily weight monitoring to confirm adequate growth trend, since this is the primary marker that nutritional support is actually working.
  3. Advance feeding method (parenteral → minimal enteral → full enteral → oral) as tolerated, watching for feeding intolerance (bilious residuals, abdominal distention) at each step.
  4. Monitor for TPN complications (glucose/electrolyte instability, signs of catheter infection) in infants requiring prolonged parenteral support.
Notify provider ifOliguria (<0.5-1 mL/kg/hr); bilious gastric residuals or increasing abdominal distention (possible feeding intolerance/NEC — see full NEC card, Prematurity chapter); inadequate weight gain trend; signs of TPN-related complications (hyperglycemia, signs of catheter site infection).
Exam pearl: Suck-swallow-breathe coordination isn't reliable until 32-34 weeks (fully synchronized by 36-37) — this single fact explains why route progresses parenteral → enteral → oral, and why "minimal enteral feeding" starting in tiny volumes is now standard even in infants who can't yet safely feed by mouth at all.
Pain & Stress Management
▸
Why this is a priority, not an extra

High-risk newborns undergo many potentially painful life-saving interventions, and are unable to tolerate high amounts of physiologic stress. Pain triggers real, measurable harm: changes in heart rate, blood pressure, intracranial pressure, vagal tone, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation, plus release of epinephrine, norepinephrine, glucagon, cortisol, and other stress hormones (see full mechanism, Neonatal Pain Response card, Unit 9). Despite decades of evidence and policy, acute infant pain remains undermanaged in practice — this is a real, persistent care gap, not a solved problem.

Nonpharmacologic management

Combining two or more methods produces more effective pain reduction than any single method alone.

  • Swaddling: limits the infant's boundaries, aids self-regulation, reduces physiologic/behavioral stress — must be done correctly (see full technique, Unit 9 Pain Management card)
  • Skin-to-skin/kangaroo care: strong analgesic effect during procedures like heel lance; safe and effective for a single painful procedure
  • Nonnutritive sucking (NNS): often combined with oral sucrose for a stronger combined effect
  • Breastfeeding/breast milk: reduces pain during minor procedures such as heel stick
  • Oral sucrose: administered ~2 minutes before, during, and sometimes 1-2 minutes after a minor procedure (heel stick, venipuncture, subcutaneous injection); effective for single painful procedures especially paired with sucking, but found ineffective for peripheral IV insertion pain specifically — don't assume it works for every procedure type
  • Sensorial stimulation: speaking softly + gentle face massage + oral sucrose together
Evidence caveatNonpharmacologic methods (music, massage, breast milk, skin-to-skin) are somewhat effective but NOT as effective as pharmacologic methods for genuine pain. For moderate/severe pain, nonpharmacologic strategies alone are not sufficient — escalate to pharmacologic management rather than relying on comfort measures indefinitely.
Pharmacologic management
  • Local/topical anesthesia: routinely used for circumcision; topical anesthesia also used for lumbar puncture and sometimes venipuncture/heel sticks
  • Nonopioid analgesia: oral liquid acetaminophen, effective for mild-to-moderate pain, particularly from inflammatory conditions
  • Opioids (morphine, fentanyl): the most widely used agents for moderate-to-severe neonatal pain; continuous or bolus IV infusion provides effective, safe control — infants receiving this degree of pain relief require close monitoring for respiratory depression
  • Other methods: epidural infusion, local/regional nerve blocks, intradermal or topical anesthetics
Priority nursing actions
  1. Use a validated pain scale consistently and treat pain before it becomes intense — proactive planning allows pain to be anticipated and prevented rather than just reacted to.
  2. Select the tool appropriate to the infant's gestational age and clinical context (see full Pain Assessment Tools card, Unit 9 for NIPS/PIPP/NPASS/CRIES comparison).
  3. Initiate comfort measures a few minutes before a procedure begins, giving the infant time to relax and reorganize rather than starting cold.
  4. Combine methods (pharmacologic + nonpharmacologic, or 2+ nonpharmacologic methods together) rather than relying on any single approach.
  5. Reassess with the same tool after intervention to confirm effectiveness.
Notify provider ifPIPP score remains elevated (>6-7, or 12 = severe) despite intervention; signs of respiratory depression in an infant receiving opioid analgesia; pain manifestations persisting despite combined pharmacologic/nonpharmacologic management.
Exam pearl: Every NANN-trained nurse who cares for newborns must have pain-assessment competency — and severely compromised or very preterm infants may be UNABLE to generate a visible pain response despite genuinely experiencing pain. Absence of crying/grimacing never means absence of pain in this population.

Kangaroo Care & Family Partnership

Kangaroo Care
▸
Technique

Skin-to-skin holding of the newborn, dressed only in a diaper, held upright against the parent's bare chest — ideally positioned so the infant's ear rests over the parent's heart to hear the familiar heartbeat. Appropriate once the infant is stable enough to tolerate time out of the incubator.

Documented benefits
  • Effective for maintaining newborn temperature
  • Strong analgesic effect during painful procedures such as heel lance
  • Improved oxygen saturation, feeding vigor, and growth
  • Infants of breastfeeding mothers who received skin-to-skin contact maintained higher O2 saturation and had fewer desaturations below 90%
  • Supports continued breastfeeding both during hospitalization and for at least 1 month after discharge
  • Reduces stress, improves quality of movement and self-regulation, and enhances maternal-infant bonding
Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationKangaroo care means holding your baby skin-to-skin against your bare chest, wearing only a diaper. This is one of the most powerful things you can do for your baby's temperature stability, breathing, and growth — and it helps you bond even while your baby is still in the NICU. Ask your nurse when your baby is ready to try it.
Other Caregiver EducationIf invited to do skin-to-skin care by the parents and care team, follow the same positioning guidance (baby upright, ear over your heart) and don't attempt it without nursing supervision the first few times.
Family Partnership & Support
▸
The parent's NICU journey

Parents typically move through recognizable phases: initially the NICU environment feels overwhelming, but most parents are able to shift focus onto their infant and push environmental distractions into the background; they then begin actively building a relationship with the infant and taking ownership of care. Family-centered care philosophy treats parents as genuine partners — not visitors — and positions them as "the voice of the newborn."

Nursing measures that facilitate parent partnership
  1. Orient parents thoroughly to the NICU environment, policies, and relevant privacy regulations.
  2. Assign one primary care nurse as a consistent main contact point for the family.
  3. Explain the rationale behind policies/procedures rather than just stating rules.
  4. Actively listen to parents' expectations for their own role in care, and incorporate their preferences into the care routine where possible.
  5. Use a bedside whiteboard for ongoing communication between shifts/family.
  6. Encourage parents to call the unit 24/7 for updates, and encourage them to bring in the infant's own clothing when allowed.
  7. Create space for parents to voice dissatisfaction with care without it damaging the relationship — this is a sign of engagement, not a problem to manage away.
  8. Encourage touch from the very first visit onward, and offer kangaroo care as soon as the infant is stable enough.
  9. Include fathers/partners explicitly in both education and hands-on care, not just the mother.
Maternal/parental self-care strategies
  • Accept that the NICU environment feels overwhelming at first — this is a normal phase, not a sign you aren't coping well
  • Use the primary nurse relationship to avoid having to re-explain your questions/preferences to a new person every shift
  • Participate in developmental care and kangaroo care as soon as you're able — this benefits your own psychological adjustment as much as the infant's physiology
  • Bring your own emotional needs to social work, chaplaincy, or a NICU parent support group rather than only focusing outward on the infant
Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationYou are a partner in your baby's care, not just a visitor — tell us your preferences and don't hesitate to ask questions or voice concerns, even repeatedly. You'll have one main nurse to help coordinate your baby's care and answer questions. Touching, holding, and kangaroo care are encouraged as soon as your baby is ready.
Other Caregiver EducationIf you're supporting the family of a NICU infant, understand that the environment can feel intense at first for everyone involved. Follow the parents' lead on how they want to be supported, and respect the care team's guidance on when/how you can interact with the infant.

Respiratory Distress Syndrome

Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS)
▸
Pathophysiology

Surfactant deficiency (hyaline membrane disease). Deficient surfactant → unequal alveolar inflation + collapse on expiration → widespread atelectasis → infant works harder each breath → exhaustion. Inadequate perfusion/ventilation → hypoxemia + hypercapnia → pulmonary vasoconstriction (worsening cycle). Prolonged hypoxemia → anaerobic glycolysis → metabolic acidosis, plus respiratory acidosis from CO2 retention.

Why surfactant is disease-modifying, not just supportive: it breaks the cycle at its origin
Etiology

Almost exclusively preterm infants; 2x more common in males. Responsible for more infant deaths than any other neonatal disease.

Signs & symptoms

Cardinal signs: nasal flaring, grunting, tachypnea, central cyanosis, retractions. Apnea, especially during feedings.

Risk factors

Prematurity (primary), male sex, maternal diabetes (delays surfactant production), cesarean without labor, second-born twin.

Complications

Pneumothorax, BPD (chronic lung disease), PDA, intraventricular hemorrhage, ROP, air leak syndrome — most stem from the ventilation/oxygen therapy needed to treat RDS itself.

Priority nursing interventions (ranked by what kills fastest)
  1. Airway and oxygenation — this disease kills via progressive respiratory failure; continuous monitoring of respiratory rate, effort, and SaO2 is the top priority, above every other action listed here.
  2. Administer exogenous surfactant per order (endotracheal or LISA technique) — disease-modifying, not just supportive.
  3. Support with CPAP (preferred to avoid intubation) or ventilation as ordered.
  4. Maintain neutral thermal environment and acid-base balance — both cold stress and acidosis worsen pulmonary vasoconstriction.
  5. Minimize handling/stimulation to reduce oxygen consumption.

Treatment

  • CPAP as initial approach; escalate to mechanical ventilation if needed
  • Severe cases: inhaled nitric oxide (iNO), ECMO
Resuscitation noteCurrent guidelines: initiate neonatal resuscitation with room air (21% O2), not 100% O2, to reduce oxidative stress. Add supplemental O2 only if no improvement within 90 seconds.
Maternal self-care strategies
  • Pump and store breast milk even if the infant cannot yet feed orally — this maintains supply for later use and gives you an active role in care
  • Ask about kangaroo (skin-to-skin) care opportunities as soon as the infant is stable enough — this benefits both bonding and infant physiologic stability
  • Accept that NICU stays are stressful; seek support from social work, chaplaincy, or a NICU parent support group if available

We're giving a medicine called surfactant that coats the inside of the lungs to help them stay open, along with breathing support. This is one of the most treatable conditions we see in premature babies.
Other Caregiver EducationIf this baby has a history of RDS/prematurity and you're providing care after discharge, watch closely for breathing changes (fast breathing, flaring, grunting, blue color) and know these require immediate emergency evaluation, not a wait-and-see approach.
Exogenous Pulmonary Surfactant — Beractant (Survanta), Poractant alfa (Curosurf)
▸
Classification

Pulmonary surfactant; respiratory agent.

Action / Indication

Prevention and rescue treatment of Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS) from surfactant deficiency, primarily in preterm infants. Mechanism: ↓surface tension → ↑alveolar stability → ↑lung compliance → improved oxygenation — this directly interrupts the RDS vicious cycle covered in the Prematurity/RDS tab.

Route

Administered directly into the trachea via endotracheal tube, or via LISA (less invasive surfactant administration) technique. Dosing is product- and weight-specific — not a single number to memorize for this exam.

Expected effect

Often rapid improvement in lung compliance and oxygenation shortly after administration.

Post-Term Infant

Post-Term (Postdates) Infant
▸
Definition & key distinction

Post-term: gestation of 42 0/7 weeks or beyond, regardless of birth weight — a dating category alone. This accounts for only about 0.25% of all U.S. births, since most pregnancies now end earlier via elective induction. Not every post-term infant is compromised — some are simply AGA and continued growing normally past 40 weeks.

Postmature infant / dysmaturity syndrome: a subset of post-term infants (occurring in ~10-20% of post-term pregnancies) who show the actual physical signs of progressive placental insufficiency — the placenta has aged past its functional capacity and can no longer adequately support the fetus. This is the clinically important distinction: "post-term" describes timing, "postmature/dysmaturity" describes an infant actually harmed by an aging placenta.

Pathophysiology — why placental aging causes problems

As the placenta ages beyond term, its ability to transport oxygen and nutrients declines. Depending on how the fetus responds, this produces one of two very different outcomes:

  • If the placenta continues functioning adequately: fetal growth continues past 40 weeks, producing a macrosomic infant — at increased risk for birth injury, shoulder dystocia, and operative delivery, similar to an LGA infant
  • If placental function progressively declines (placental insufficiency): the fetus is deprived of adequate nutrients/oxygen despite ongoing gestation, and may actually lose weight in utero — this produces the classic wasted, "old-man" postmature appearance from depleted subcutaneous fat

A post-term infant can therefore be macrosomic, appropriately grown, OR growth-restricted — gestational age alone doesn't predict which.

Complications
  • Significant increase in fetal and neonatal mortality compared to term infants — this is true across post-term infants generally, not just the postmature subset
  • Oligohydramnios (decreased amniotic fluid) — common in postterm pregnancy, creates risk for umbilical cord compression and hypoxemia during labor
  • Meconium-stained amniotic fluid and meconium aspiration syndrome (MAS) — the greatest single respiratory risk, from fetal distress causing in-utero meconium passage
  • Persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN) — right-to-left shunting through the foramen ovale/ductus arteriosus; can be triggered by MAS or other causes of fetal distress
  • Birth injury and neurologic damage — particularly for macrosomic post-term infants, from shoulder dystocia and a difficult delivery
  • Hypoglycemia — from depleted glycogen stores in the growth-restricted/postmature subset
  • Increased maternal risk as well: severe perineal injury, chorioamnionitis, postpartum hemorrhage, and increased cesarean birth rate

Hemolytic Disease

Rh Incompatibility (Isoimmunization)
▸
Pathophysiology

Rh-negative mother carries Rh-positive fetus. Fetal RBCs enter maternal circulation (usually at birth) → mother produces anti-Rh antibodies (sensitization). In a subsequent Rh-positive pregnancy, these antibodies cross the placenta and attack fetal RBCs → hemolysis → fetus compensates with accelerated erythropoiesis, releasing immature RBCs (erythroblasts) — "erythroblastosis fetalis."

RhoGAM given at 28 weeks + within 72 hrs postpartum prevents the mother from ever reaching the "sensitized" step
Etiology / risk factors for sensitization

First pregnancy usually unaffected. Risk increases with spontaneous/therapeutic abortion, CVS, amniocentesis, cordocentesis, placental abruption, maternal abdominal trauma.

Signs & symptoms in severe form

Progressive hemolysis → fetal hypoxia → immune hydrops fetalis: cardiac failure, generalized edema (anasarca), pericardial/pleural/peritoneal effusions. Newborn may be stillborn or show respiratory distress at birth. Hepatosplenomegaly, marked pallor, hypovolemic shock in severe cases.

Complications

Hydrops fetalis, severe anemia, kernicterus if hyperbilirubinemia untreated, hypoglycemia (from pancreatic cell hyperplasia).

The most common incompatibility is a type O mother with a type A, B, or AB infant. The mother's naturally-occurring anti-A and/or anti-B antibodies cross the placenta and attack fetal RBCs, causing hemolysis.

Infant of Diabetic Mother (IDM)
▸
Pathophysiology

Chronic maternal hyperglycemia crosses the placenta; fetal pancreas responds with hyperinsulinemia (insulin acts as a growth hormone in the fetus). After birth, maternal glucose supply stops abruptly but fetal insulin production continues at a high rate → rapid, significant hypoglycemia.

Etiology

Poorly controlled maternal diabetes (gestational or pre-existing) during pregnancy.

Signs & symptoms
  • Macrosomia — characteristic large-for-gestational-age appearance
  • LGA infant may paradoxically show premature-like organ immaturity (lungs, especially) despite size/gestational age
  • Respiratory distress, hypoglycemia, hypocalcemia, polycythemia
  • Neurological deficits: seizures, jitteriness, lethargy
Risk factors

Elevated maternal A1c, poor glycemic control especially in 3rd trimester.

Complications

Birth injuries (shoulder dystocia, clavicle fracture, brachial plexus injury) from macrosomia; congenital anomalies (cardiac, neural tube) if maternal hyperglycemia was present during early organogenesis; severe/prolonged hypoglycemia → neurologic injury if untreated.

Metabolic & Genetic Screening

Universal Newborn Screening (Guthrie Test)
▸
Purpose & scope

Universal newborn screening is a state-mandated public health program aimed at early detection of genetic, metabolic, and endocrine diseases that cause severe, often irreversible health problems if not caught and treated early — before symptoms appear. Most disorders included in the panel are not symptomatic at birth, which is exactly why screening (rather than waiting for clinical signs) is essential. Roughly 4 million babies are screened annually in the U.S.

The three categories of disorders screened
  • Metabolic disorders — e.g., PAH deficiency (formerly PKU)
  • Genetic/hematologic disorders — e.g., sickle cell disease, other hemoglobinopathies
  • Endocrinopathies — e.g., congenital hypothyroidism

The federal Health Resources and Services Administration recommends screening for 35 core disorders plus 26 secondary disorders; individual states can add more. All 50 states screen for PKU and congenital hypothyroidism; about 46 states screen for galactosemia and 45 for hemoglobinopathies. Nurses should know their specific state's panel.

The Guthrie test — technique
  • A heel stick (heel puncture) obtains a capillary blood sample from the newborn
  • Apply a heel warmer before the puncture — a cool extremity has diminished blood flow, making the sample harder to obtain and potentially causing a falsely low result
  • Blood is collected directly onto a special absorbent filter paper ("Guthrie card"), filling each designated circle completely
  • The card is air-dried, then mailed to a designated state laboratory
  • Analysis uses tandem mass spectrometry, which can screen for dozens of disorders from this single dried blood spot
Critical timing ruleThe heel stick must be performed after the newborn is 24 hours old — this allows enough time for the infant to have ingested formula or breast milk, which is necessary for an accurate PKU result specifically (phenylalanine only accumulates to detectable levels after protein intake begins). Samples are typically collected after 24 hours of age and before hospital discharge. If the infant was NPO for any reason, or discharged before 24 hours, the test must be repeated at a follow-up visit, usually by 2 weeks of age at the latest. Some conditions are only caught on this second screening.
Nursing responsibility — why this is a genuine priority, not paperwork
  1. Ensure screening is actually performed before discharge — this is the single most important nursing objective for this topic. Infants discharged early, born preterm, or born outside the hospital are at highest risk of being missed.
  2. If a repeat/follow-up screen is needed (early discharge, NPO status, or a borderline initial result), ensure a specific follow-up appointment is arranged before the family leaves — don't just tell them to "get it done eventually."
  3. Document the exact time of the heel stick relative to birth and feeding status, since this affects result interpretation.
Notify provider / escalate ifScreening was not completed before discharge for any reason; the infant was NPO at the time of the intended screen; any abnormal/out-of-range result returns from the lab (requires prompt confirmatory testing and specialist referral, not a "wait and see" approach given how time-sensitive conditions like PKU and congenital hypothyroidism are).
Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationBefore you go home, your baby will have a simple heel-stick blood test that checks for several rare but serious conditions that don't show any symptoms at birth. This test only works accurately after your baby has fed for at least 24 hours, so if you're discharged early, we'll schedule a follow-up test. Catching these conditions early — often before any symptoms appear — makes a huge difference in your baby's long-term health.
Other Caregiver EducationIf you're told this baby needs a repeat newborn screening test, help make sure that follow-up appointment actually happens — it's easy to overlook once everyone is home and adjusting, but these results matter for lifelong health.
Congenital Hypothyroidism
▸
Pathophysiology

An endocrine disorder producing a deficiency of thyroid hormone (T3/T4). Thyroid hormone is essential for normal brain development and growth — without adequate levels, the infant develops permanent intellectual disability and growth delay if treatment is delayed. Congenital hypothyroidism is one of the most common preventable causes of cognitive impairment, which is exactly why universal screening exists for it.

Etiology & epidemiology
  • May be primary (thyroid gland itself) or secondary (pituitary-based)
  • Affects approximately 1 in 2,000-4,000 newborns (some sources cite 6 per 10,000); more than twice as common in females
  • More prevalent among Hispanic and Native American/Alaskan Native populations; less prevalent among Black Americans
  • Infants with Down syndrome have a much higher rate of both permanent and transient forms
  • Preterm infants, especially born before 28 weeks, may have transient hypothyroidism from hypothalamic/pituitary immaturity and may need temporary thyroid hormone replacement
Signs & symptoms — why this is easy to miss

Clinical manifestations are often NOT obvious at birth, likely because maternal thyroid hormone crosses the placenta and temporarily masks the deficiency. This is precisely why screening (rather than physical exam alone) is essential — by the time symptoms are obvious, treatment is already delayed.

PAH Deficiency (formerly PKU) — Dietary Management
▸
Pathophysiology

Genetic deficiency of phenylalanine hydroxylase enzyme → inability to metabolize phenylalanine (an amino acid) → toxic accumulation → brain damage if untreated.

Diagnosis

Detected via universal newborn screening (Guthrie test/heel stick).

Treatment

Lifelong dietary phenylalanine restriction, started as early as possible after diagnosis.

Foods to avoid (high phenylalanine)
  • Meat, poultry, fish, eggs (regular amounts)
  • Dairy: milk, cheese, regular yogurt
  • Nuts and seeds, legumes/beans
  • Regular wheat products (bread, pasta) in large amounts
  • Aspartame-containing products (artificial sweetener contains phenylalanine)
Foods generally allowed
  • Special low-protein/phenylalanine-free medical formula (primary nutrition source in infancy)
  • Most fruits and vegetables (in measured amounts per dietitian plan)
  • Low-protein specialty breads/pastas
  • Fats and sugars (do not contain phenylalanine)
Critical family teaching pointThis is a lifelong dietary condition managed by a metabolic dietitian/specialist — the nurse's role is to reinforce adherence and refer, not to independently prescribe diet specifics. Genetic counseling is also indicated.

Galactosemia — Dietary Management
▸
Pathophysiology

Genetic deficiency in an enzyme needed to metabolize galactose (a sugar from lactose breakdown) → toxic accumulation → liver damage, cataracts, developmental delay if untreated.

Treatment

Immediate and lifelong elimination of galactose/lactose from the diet.

Foods/items to avoid
  • Breast milk (contains lactose) — must be avoided even though normally recommended
  • Regular cow's-milk-based infant formula
  • All dairy products: milk, cheese, butter, yogurt
  • Foods with hidden lactose (some processed foods, certain medications use lactose as filler)
Foods/items allowed
  • Soy-based or lactose-free specialty infant formula
  • Most fruits, vegetables, meats (as diet advances with age)
  • Lactose-free dairy alternatives
Critical pointUnlike most newborn conditions where breastfeeding is encouraged, galactosemia is a rare case where breastfeeding is contraindicated because breast milk contains lactose.

Birth Trauma

Birth Trauma
▸
Pathophysiology/Etiology

Many birth injuries are avoidable (ultrasound can diagnose macrosomia, malpresentation, hydrocephalus antenatally, allowing planned cesarean). Some are unavoidable: difficult/prolonged labor, precipitous ("explosive") labor, shoulder dystocia, forceps/vacuum-assisted delivery. Birth injuries are broadly categorized as bone fractures or neurological injuries (peripheral nerve/plexus injuries, cranial nerve injuries, or intracranial/spinal injuries), and diagnosis relies on thorough nursing assessment plus x-ray or other imaging as indicated.

Overview of injury sites (for orientation)

Common birth trauma spans scalp (caput/cephalhematoma/subgaleal hemorrhage — see Head-to-Toe Exam tab, Unit 9), skull fractures, intracranial hemorrhage, spinal cord injury, brachial/lumbosacral plexus injuries, and cranial/peripheral nerve palsies (facial, phrenic, radial, laryngeal). This card focuses on the most common and most testable: clavicle fracture, brachial plexus injuries (Erb's and Klumpke's palsy), facial nerve palsy, and phrenic nerve palsy.

Fractured Clavicle — Etiology

The clavicle is the bone most commonly fractured during birth, typically at the middle third of the bone. Shoulder dystocia is the classic predisposing factor; can also occur with forceps use.

Fractured Clavicle — Key assessment cues
  • Palpable crepitus at the fracture site
  • Limited arm motion on the affected side
  • Absent Moro reflex on the affected side — the same hallmark finding seen in brachial plexus injury, so both should be considered together on assessment
Nursing interventions & care
  1. Gentle handling of the affected arm; avoid pulling or lifting the infant from under the axillae on that side.
  2. Containment of the limb against the chest (e.g., pinning the sleeve to the shirt, or a figure-8 splint per provider order) may be used for comfort, though often no device is needed at all.
  3. No specific treatment is typically required — newborn fractures heal rapidly and the prognosis is excellent.
  4. Support parents, who are often afraid of hurting the infant; encourage and supervise their practice with handling, changing, and feeding to build confidence and support attachment.
Brachial Plexus Injuries — Pathophysiology

Results from forces that stretch or alter the normal position/relationship of the arm, shoulder, and neck during birth — classically shoulder dystocia or a difficult vertex/breech delivery. Nerve roots C5-T1 make up the brachial plexus; which roots are affected determines which type of palsy results.

Brachial Plexus Injuries — Diagnostics

Complete neurologic assessment to determine the type/degree/extent of nerve impairment; assess Moro, biceps, and radial reflexes specifically. Imaging (myelography, CT myelography, MRI) can confirm and characterize the injury when needed.

Brachial Plexus Injuries — Complications

Approximately 93-95% of infants recover with conservative management alone. Complete recovery from a stretched-nerve injury typically takes 3-6 months. However, avulsion (complete disconnection of the nerve roots/ganglia from the spinal cord) results in permanent damage. Injuries not improving by 3 months should be evaluated for possible surgical intervention (nerve decompression or grafting).

Nursing interventions & care — brachial plexus injury
  1. Initially, rest the arm to allow any swelling to decrease before starting range-of-motion work.
  2. Positioning: the affected arm should be positioned abducted 90° with external shoulder rotation, forearm supination, and wrist extension with the palm facing the infant's face — this protects correct placement of the humeral head in the glenoid fossa and helps prevent contractures.
  3. Passive range-of-motion exercises of the shoulder, wrist, elbow, and fingers, typically initiated around 5-10 days of age (per Perry) or "the latter part of the first week," performed multiple times daily in consultation with physical therapy.
  4. A wrist splint with padding in the fist may be used to prevent wrist flexion contractures.
  5. Dressing technique: when dressing, start with the affected arm first; when undressing, start with the unaffected arm first — this minimizes unnecessary manipulation and stress on the paralyzed muscles.
  6. Handling/positioning teaching for parents: use the football hold when carrying the infant; avoid lifting or pulling the infant up from under the axillae or by the arms, which can worsen the injury.
Notify provider ifAsymmetric Moro reflex is noted (requires evaluation to grade the degree of nerve injury and plan follow-up); no improvement in movement/tone by 3 months (surgical evaluation candidate); new swelling, color change, or signs of pain in the affected limb.
Facial Nerve Palsy (7th Cranial Nerve) — Pathophysiology

Pressure on the facial nerve during birth, often from forceps application, causing loss of movement on the affected side.

Facial Nerve Palsy — Key assessment cues
  • Inability to completely close the eye on the affected side
  • Drooping of the corner of the mouth
  • Absence of forehead wrinkling and nasolabial fold on the affected side
  • Most noticeable when the infant cries: mouth is drawn toward the UNAFFECTED side, wrinkles are deeper on the normal side, and the eye on the involved side stays open — this asymmetry-with-crying pattern is the same principle used for general facial symmetry assessment (see HEENT card, Unit 9)
Facial Nerve Palsy — Complications

Often temporary, resolving within hours to days. Permanent paralysis is rare and occurs only if the nerve fibers were actually torn, in which case surgical intervention may be necessary.

Nursing interventions & care — facial nerve palsy
  1. Assist with feeding technique — the infant's ability to suck effectively can be impaired and drooling is common; gavage feeding may be required to prevent aspiration if sucking is significantly affected.
  2. Breastfeeding is not contraindicated, but the mother may need additional lactation consultant support to help the infant achieve an effective latch/areolar compression.
  3. Eye protection: if the eyelid on the affected side doesn't close completely, instill artificial tears to prevent drying/injury to the conjunctiva, sclera, and cornea; the eyelid may be taped shut for protection. Teach parents how to administer eyedrops if eye care is still needed at discharge.
Phrenic Nerve Paralysis — Pathophysiology

Usually unilateral injury resulting in diaphragmatic paralysis; the affected side's diaphragm cannot contract normally, so that lung fails to expand fully. Sometimes occurs together with brachial plexus injury (given their anatomic proximity).

Phrenic Nerve Paralysis — Key assessment cues
  • Respiratory distress is the most common and most important sign — this is fundamentally a respiratory injury, not primarily a motor one
  • Paradoxical/asymmetric chest movement; breathing is primarily thoracic rather than the normal newborn abdominal pattern
  • Cyanosis, tachypnea, or in severe cases complete respiratory failure
  • Confirmed via ultrasonography showing paradoxical chest movement and an elevated diaphragm on the affected side (chest x-ray may be falsely reassuring if the infant is on positive-pressure ventilation, since PPV can mask the elevated diaphragm)
Phrenic Nerve Paralysis — Complications

Pneumonia and atelectasis on the affected side can develop given the persistently under-expanded lung.

Nursing interventions & care — phrenic nerve paralysis
  1. Manage exactly as any infant in respiratory distress (see full Respiratory Assessment card, Unit 9) — this outranks any other consideration since ineffective respiratory effort is the acute threat.
  2. Position the infant on the affected side to allow maximum expansion of the uninvolved (healthy) lung.
  3. Anticipate an extended recovery course; ongoing follow-up is essential.
Notify provider ifAny signs of respiratory distress in an infant with known/suspected brachial plexus injury (phrenic nerve involvement should be suspected); asymmetric chest movement; cyanosis or worsening tachypnea.
Maternal self-care noteIf your birth was complicated by shoulder dystocia or a difficult delivery, it's normal to have questions or feelings about it — ask your provider for a clear explanation of what happened at your postpartum visit, and don't hesitate to seek support if you're processing a difficult birth experience.
Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationYour baby has some weakness from pressure on nerves during birth. Many babies improve substantially on their own over the first few months, and we'll show you exactly how to position, dress, and hold your baby to protect the area while it heals. You'll follow up with a specialist to track progress and catch early if any additional treatment is needed.
Other Caregiver EducationHandle the affected arm or side gently during dressing/diapering; avoid pulling on it, and follow the specific dressing order (affected side first when dressing, unaffected side first when undressing) the parents show you. Know which side is affected and report any new swelling, color change, breathing difficulty, or the baby appearing to be in pain.

Infection: Sepsis & TORCH

Neonatal Sepsis
▸
Pathophysiology

Overwhelming infection with inability to clear toxins. Newborn immune system is immature (low IgM, delayed neutrophil function) and cannot localize infection — systemic spread is common and rapid.

Etiology / patterns
  • Early onset: congenital/prenatal exposure, often group B strep or gram-negative organisms acquired during labor/delivery
  • Late onset: nosocomial (hospital-acquired)
Signs & symptoms by system
  • Respiratory: apnea, distress, ↓SaO2
  • Cardiovascular: ↓perfusion, HR changes, hypotension
  • CNS: lethargy OR irritability (either direction is concerning), hypotonia, temperature instability
  • GI: poor feeding, vomiting, diarrhea
  • Integument: jaundice, pallor, petechiae, mottling
Risk factors

Prematurity (greatest risk), PROM, chorioamnionitis, maternal fever, antenatal/intrapartal asphyxia, invasive procedures.

Complications

Septic shock, meningitis, DIC, death — sepsis can progress to hemodynamic collapse faster in neonates than in older children/adults.

  3. Administer IV antibiotics as ordered — timing significantly affects neonatal sepsis outcomes; don't let culture collection meaningfully delay this.

Treatment

Empiric broad-spectrum IV antibiotics pending culture results, then narrowed per organism sensitivity. Supportive respiratory/cardiovascular care as needed.

TORCH Complex Infections
▸
TORCH: Toxoplasmosis, Other (syphilis, Hepatitis B, HIV, Parvovirus B19, West Nile, Zika, Group B Strep, Chlamydia, Gonorrhea, Listeria), Rubella, Cytomegalovirus, Herpes Simplex Virus
Why these are grouped together

All are organisms capable of crossing the placenta (transplacental transmission) and/or infecting the newborn during birth. They tend to produce overlapping, nonspecific findings in the newborn (rash, hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice, growth restriction, CNS involvement) — which is exactly why they're tested as a panel/"TORCH workup" rather than one at a time when congenital infection is suspected. Note: common bacterial infections (like typical neonatal sepsis organisms) are usually diagnosed by clinical picture and routine cultures instead, not the TORCH panel.

Common maternal presentation across TORCH organisms

Generally produce mild, influenza-like symptoms in the pregnant woman (or she may be entirely asymptomatic) — but fetal/neonatal effects can be severe. This mismatch (mild maternal illness, severe fetal consequences) is a key teaching point and a common NCLEX trap.

Priority nursing interventions (ranked by acuity)
  1. For any suspected HSV exposure: initiate contact precautions and notify provider immediately — disseminated HSV has the highest mortality of the TORCH organisms and can progress within days; do not wait for visible lesions.

  3. Implement standard + organism-specific isolation precautions to prevent nosocomial spread to other infants.
  4. Monitor for the specific organ systems each organism targets (eyes, hearing, CNS, liver) since many TORCH infections are initially asymptomatic and complications emerge over the following weeks to years.

  • Failed newborn hearing screen in an infant with any TORCH risk factor — CMV-related hearing loss can be progressive and may not be present at the initial screen
  • New-onset jaundice, hepatosplenomegaly, or abnormal growth pattern in an infant with known maternal TORCH exposure during pregnancy
Maternal self-care / prevention strategies
  • Toxoplasmosis: avoid changing cat litter (or wear gloves + wash hands immediately if unavoidable), cook meat thoroughly, wash produce well
  • CMV: practice careful hand hygiene, especially after contact with young children's saliva/urine (daycare workers and mothers of toddlers are at higher exposure risk); avoid sharing food/utensils with young children
  • Rubella: confirm immunity status pre-conception when possible; if non-immune and postpartum, get vaccinated before leaving the hospital and use reliable contraception for the recommended waiting period before attempting another pregnancy
  • HSV: disclose any history of genital herpes to your obstetric provider early in pregnancy so a delivery plan (including possible cesarean birth for active lesions at labor onset) can be made in advance

"Substance-exposed infant" is not one uniform presentation — different drug classes cross the placenta by different mechanisms, cause harm through different pathways (withdrawal vs. direct teratogenic/vascular injury vs. both), and produce withdrawal on very different timelines. Polysubstance use complicates the picture

Many women who use one substance use more than one. The nurse is often the first to observe withdrawal signs and plays a key role in this differential process, not just in scoring severity.

Neonatal Abstinence Syndrome (NAS) / Substance-Exposed Infants
▸
Pathophysiology

Chronic in-utero exposure to opioids/other substances leads to physiologic dependence. After birth, the substance supply stops abruptly → withdrawal syndrome affecting CNS, GI, and autonomic nervous system.

Etiology

Maternal use of narcotics (heroin, methadone), other illicit substances, smoking, alcohol during pregnancy. See the full Types of Substance Exposure card above for how these differ by class.

Signs & symptoms
  • CNS: irritability, high-pitched cry, tremors, hypertonicity, poor sleep, seizures (severe)
  • GI: poor feeding, vomiting, diarrhea, poor weight gain
  • Autonomic: sweating, temperature instability, nasal stuffiness, sneezing, yawning
Clinical picture cautionA drug-exposed newborn is typically irritable and overloaded by stimulation — hyperextends/postures rather than cuddles when held. This is the opposite presentation of a lethargic/sleepy infant; don't confuse the two on an NGN item.
Assessment tools
Finnegan ToolNNNS
Complications

Poor weight gain/failure to thrive if feeding is severely disrupted; seizures in severe untreated withdrawal; long-term neurodevelopmental risk correlates with exposure severity.

Priority nursing interventions (ranked)
  1. Score withdrawal severity on a validated tool (Finnegan) at regular intervals — this drives the decision for pharmacologic treatment before seizures or severe dehydration develop, so it outranks comfort measures alone.
  2. Minimize environmental stimulation: low light, quiet, limit unnecessary handling.
  3. Swaddle, offer non-nutritive sucking, and support small/frequent feedings to protect nutritional status.
  4. Administer pharmacologic treatment (methadone, phenobarbital, morphine) as ordered if scores indicate need.
Notify provider if
  • Finnegan score reaches the facility's treatment threshold
  • Seizure activity
  • Feeding intolerance leading to weight loss beyond expected parameters
  • Signs of dehydration
Fetal Alcohol Syndrome (FAS) — distinct condition
  • Microcephaly
  • Small eyes, thin upper lip, flat mid-face
  • Growth restriction, neurodevelopmental deficits (fine motor, IQ, speech)
Maternal self-care strategies
  • Connect with substance use treatment/recovery resources and postpartum follow-up — this is a health condition, not a moral failing, and ongoing support improves outcomes for both mother and baby
  • Ask about rooming-in and skin-to-skin opportunities, which can help both maternal bonding and infant withdrawal symptoms
  • Seek nonjudgmental care coordination (social work, case management) for housing, custody, or legal questions rather than navigating alone
Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationYour baby may be extra fussy, have trouble sleeping, or need extra help feeding for a while because of substance exposure before birth. We use a scoring tool to track how your baby is doing and decide if any medicine is needed. Swaddling, a quiet room, and skin-to-skin time can really help calm your baby.
Other Caregiver EducationThis baby may be more sensitive to noise, light, and handling than a typical newborn. Keep the environment calm and quiet, swaddle snugly, and avoid unnecessary stimulation. Report increasing fussiness, feeding refusal, or any shaking/seizure-like movement immediately.
Morphine Sulfate & Methadone (NAS Treatment)
▸
Classification

Opioid agonists.

Action / Indication

Pharmacologic management of significant Neonatal Abstinence Syndrome (NAS) when nonpharmacologic measures (swaddling, low stimulation, frequent small feeds) are insufficient, per Finnegan scoring. Morphine and methadone are the two opioids specifically used for this indication; both replace the withdrawal substance temporarily and are then gradually tapered.

Route

Typically PO for NAS treatment; weight-based, titrated/weaned per institutional protocol based on withdrawal scoring — no single dose to memorize for this exam.

Expected/common effects

Sedation, constipation, nausea, decreased GI motility.

Priority adverse effect — ANY opioidRespiratory depression is the adverse effect that outranks all others in priority for monitoring. Also watch for bradycardia, hypotension, and profound CNS depression. Methadone specifically carries QT-prolongation/dysrhythmia risk.
Contraindications/precautions

Significant respiratory depression; hypersensitivity; caution with other CNS depressants or cardiac conduction abnormalities (methadone).

Nursing considerations (ranked)
  1. Monitor respiratory rate, apnea, HR, and O2 saturation continuously — respiratory depression is the life-threatening risk with any opioid, so this outranks comfort/GI monitoring.
  2. Continue using the validated withdrawal scoring tool (Finnegan) throughout treatment to guide dose titration.
  3. Assess sedation level/LOC, feeding tolerance, and weight gain trends.
  4. Taper gradually as withdrawal is controlled — never discontinue abruptly after sustained treatment.
Notify provider ifApnea, significant bradycardia, excessive sedation/unresponsiveness, or Finnegan score rising despite current dosing.
Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationYour baby is receiving a small, carefully controlled dose of medication to ease withdrawal symptoms from substance exposure before birth. This isn't the same as ongoing opioid use — the goal is to control symptoms now and slowly taper the dose down as your baby's body adjusts.
Other Caregiver EducationThis baby may seem sedated or sleepy while on this medication — that's expected. Report any pauses in breathing, unusual limpness, or difficulty waking the baby immediately.
Exam pearl: NAS nursing care isn't just medication: low stimuli + swaddle + frequent small feeds + scoring tool + medication only if severe, then taper.

▸
Classification

Barbiturate; anticonvulsant/sedative-hypnotic.

Action / Indication

Adjunctive pharmacologic therapy for NAS when withdrawal remains significant or involves polysubstance exposure, especially alongside an opioid. Also used as an anticonvulsant for neonatal seizures when relevant.

Route

  2. Monitor HR/BP and level of sedation.

Nursing Care for Substance-Exposed Infants

The Finnegan Neonatal Abstinence Scoring System
▸
Purpose

Developed by Dr. Loretta Finnegan (1985) to monitor withdrawal objectively and evaluate the infant's response to both clinical care and pharmacologic intervention. This is the standard, most widely used scoring tool for opioid-exposed infants specifically.

The tool scores signs across three physiologic categories — CNS disturbances, metabolic/vasomotor/respiratory disturbances, and GI disturbances — with each sign assigned point values reflecting severity. Scoring is typically done every 3-4 hours, and daily weight is tracked alongside the score. The total score at each assessment drives the treatment decision: nonpharmacologic care alone, or escalation to pharmacologic therapy once a facility-defined threshold is reached.

NNNS (NICU Network Neurobehavioral Scale)
▸
What it is & how it differs from Finnegan

The NNNS is a comprehensive neurologic and behavioral assessment — broader in scope than the Finnegan tool, which focuses specifically on withdrawal signs. The NNNS measures stress/abstinence signs, behavioral state, neurologic status, and muscle tone, all interpreted in the context of the infant's overall medical condition at the time of exam.

Eligibility

Used for medically stable newborns who are at least 34 weeks gestation, up to 48 weeks corrected/conceptional age. It is not a bedside tool used in the first hours of an unstable infant — it requires the infant to be stable enough to tolerate a full neurobehavioral exam.

When it's used

Identifies newborns at risk as a result of intrauterine drug exposure — complementary to Finnegan rather than a replacement for it. Where Finnegan tracks acute withdrawal severity moment to moment, NNNS gives a fuller picture of the infant's neurobehavioral functioning, useful for broader risk identification and follow-up planning.

Exam pearl: Finnegan = withdrawal severity, scored frequently, drives medication decisions. NNNS = broader neurobehavioral picture, requires stability + minimum gestational age, used for risk identification. Don't confuse the two if a question asks which tool to use in a specific scenario.
Nonpharmacologic Nursing Care
▸
Core environmental/comfort measures
  • Low light, quiet environment, minimal handling — long-accepted interventions, though not all are equally evidence-based; still form the foundation of care
  • Swaddling — limits self-stimulation and provides containment
  • Pacifier/nonnutritive sucking — helps with the frantic, uncoordinated sucking pattern common in NAS
  • Skin-to-skin contact and rooming-in — institutional commitment to keeping mother and infant together is emphasized as especially important for NAS infants, not just a nice-to-have
  • Breastfeeding encouraged unless contraindicated (see Treatment Modalities card for exceptions) — can itself help offset withdrawal symptoms
Emerging evidence-based additions
  • Vibrotactile stimulation shows promise; acupuncture evidence is mixed/inconclusive — not all "alternative" nonpharmacologic methods are equally supported
  • Multidisciplinary, integrated family therapy for substance-affected mothers is associated with better neonatal outcomes: greater gestational age at birth, higher birth weight, shorter length of stay, lower NAS scores, and higher rates of discharge home with the mother
The Eat, Sleep, Console (ESC) model

A newer, family-centered care approach that shifts the focus away from a pure numeric Finnegan score toward three functional questions: Is the infant eating normally? Can the infant sleep? Can the infant be consoled within 10 minutes when crying? This model maximizes nonpharmacologic therapies, increases family involvement, and uses medication (typically morphine) on an as-needed basis rather than a fixed schedule. ESC has been shown to decrease length of stay, decrease pharmacologic exposure, and decrease overall cost of care compared to Finnegan-driven management alone.

Positioning caveatProne positioning is sometimes used briefly to help settle a severely irritable NAS infant, but this directly contradicts standard SIDS-prevention teaching (back to sleep). If used, it must be explained to the family as a temporary measure only, used until symptoms improve, with pulse and oxygen levels monitored — and the family should be clearly taught to return to supine sleep positioning once symptoms resolve.
Nursing priorities in nonpharmacologic care
  1. Score consistently and accurately — this is the foundation every other decision builds on.
  2. Begin family-centered education prenatally when possible, covering expected newborn symptoms and coping/comfort strategies, not just after birth.
  3. Advocate for rooming-in and breastfeeding support as an interdisciplinary institutional priority for this population.
  4. Know your state's specific laws regarding maternal substance use disorder reporting — the threat of incarceration is documented as ineffective and counterproductive to getting mothers the treatment/support they need.
Pharmacologic Treatment Modalities
▸
When pharmacologic treatment is indicated

Reserved for severe withdrawal signs and to prevent serious complications such as seizures — not a first-line response to any positive Finnegan score. Nonpharmacologic measures are used with all infants experiencing NAS, whether or not medication is also needed.

Standard regimen for opioid exposure

An opioid (typically morphine, sometimes methadone), followed by phenobarbital or clonidine if withdrawal remains inadequately controlled — this is the typical combination/escalation pattern for in-utero opioid exposure specifically. See full individual medication cards (Morphine & Methadone, Phenobarbital) elsewhere in this section for dosing philosophy, monitoring, and tapering detail.

Critical safety alert — naloxone contraindicationNaloxone (Narcan) is contraindicated in infants born to opioid-using mothers. Reversing opioid effects abruptly in an opioid-dependent newborn can precipitate severe, acute withdrawal symptoms and even seizures. If an opioid-exposed infant needs respiratory support at delivery, use positive pressure ventilation, not naloxone.
Breastfeeding & substance use — when it's safe vs. contraindicated
  • Generally safe: mothers in a structured methadone treatment program who test negative for HIV and are not using illicit drugs can safely breastfeed
  • Contraindicated/discouraged: illicit street drug use (e.g., PCP) — do not breastfeed or feed expressed milk; cocaine use/smoking is contraindicated, and infants should not be near anyone smoking cocaine; marijuana use during breastfeeding should be discouraged given insufficient safety data, and infants should not be exposed to cannabis smoke (possible SIDS association)
Priority nursing actions during pharmacologic treatment
  1. Continue Finnegan scoring throughout treatment to guide dose titration and eventual tapering — medication decisions are ongoing, not a one-time order.
  2. Monitor closely for respiratory depression and excessive sedation with any opioid or phenobarbital therapy.
  3. Never use naloxone in a known or suspected opioid-exposed infant.
  4. Taper medication gradually as withdrawal comes under control — never discontinue abruptly.
Notify provider ifFinnegan score rises despite current pharmacologic dosing; any sign of respiratory depression or excessive sedation; seizure activity at any point in the treatment course.
Exam pearl: "No Narcan for NAS" — naloxone in an opioid-exposed infant can trigger severe withdrawal/seizures instead of helping. This is one of the most consistently tested safety points in this topic.
Additional Conditions at Risk

Congenital Anomalies

Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip (DDH) & Clubfoot
▸
DDH — Pathophysiology

Abnormal development of the hip joint ranging from mild instability to full dislocation of the femoral head from the acetabulum. The acetabulum is flat rather than its normal round, cup-like shape. The affected hip is usually NOT dislocated at birth — it is easily dislocatable, and postnatal factors determine whether it progresses to true dislocation, subluxation, or resolves/remains stable.

DDH — Etiology / risk factors
  • Breech presentation (especially frank breech with extended legs, which restricts hip movement during the period of active bone growth)
  • First-born infants
  • Female sex
  • Family history of DDH
DDH — Expected findings

Symmetric gluteal and thigh skin folds; equal leg length; equal knee height when hips/knees are flexed; hips move easily and smoothly through range of motion.

DDH — Unexpected/key assessment findings
  • Asymmetric gluteal/thigh skin folds and unequal leg length
  • Uneven knee height when hips/knees are flexed (positive Galeazzi/Allis sign)
  • Positive Barlow test: hip flexed to 90°, adducted, with gentle downward pressure on the femoral head — a palpable/audible "clunk" as the femoral head dislocates OUT of the acetabulum is a positive result
  • Positive Ortolani maneuver: hip abducted with gentle upward leverage — a "clunk" as a dislocated femoral head returns INTO the acetabulum confirms the hip was dislocated
  • A simple "hip click" without a true clunk can indicate ligamentous laxity alone (sometimes from maternal hormones) rather than true DDH, but should still be documented and monitored
Breech positioning look-alikeInfants who were breech in utero often have hyperextended knee joints that can visually resemble hip dysplasia. On exam, however, the femoral head remains firmly seated in the acetabulum in these infants — true DDH is confirmed by the actual clunk sign, not just an unusual resting leg position. Reassure families that positional leg appearance from breech positioning resolves on its own over time.
DDH — Complications if untreated

Chronic hip instability, abnormal gait, early-onset osteoarthritis, and long-term impairment of the ability to walk and maintain balance — this is why even a subtle positive finding warrants prompt referral rather than a "wait and see" approach.

Priority nursing interventions & considerations — DDH
  1. Only an experienced examiner (physician/NP) should perform the Barlow and Ortolani maneuvers — an unskilled attempt can injure the hip; the bedside nurse's role is careful observation of skin folds/leg length and reporting asymmetry, not performing the provocative maneuvers themselves.
  2. Document and report any asymmetric fold pattern, leg length discrepancy, or suspected clunk promptly rather than waiting for the next scheduled exam.
  3. Once a Pavlik harness is applied, teach correct diapering technique (diapers go on underneath/around the harness straps, not by removing the harness) and skin checks under the straps to prevent breakdown.
  4. Reinforce that the harness must be worn continuously per the treatment plan — inconsistent use undermines the whole treatment course.
Notify provider ifNew asymmetry in skin folds or leg length noted on any exam; a palpable clunk; skin breakdown or redness under harness straps; harness straps that appear loosened or displaced from the correct position.
DDH — Treatment

Pavlik harness for infants diagnosed early — does not rigidly immobilize but holds the hips flexed/abducted, preventing extension/adduction; worn continuously for approximately 3-6 months until a normal cup-shaped joint forms. Diagnosis is confirmed by ultrasound or CT before treatment begins. May require surgical reduction and casting (spica cast) if diagnosed later or if harness treatment fails.

Clubfoot (Talipes) — Pathophysiology

Congenital deformity in which the foot is twisted out of its normal position, most commonly turned inward and fixed in a plantar-flexion position (talipes equinovarus).

Clubfoot — Key assessment distinction

A TRUE clubfoot is fixed and rigid — it will NOT correct with gentle manual repositioning. A positional foot deformity from in-utero crowding looks similar but WILL correct easily with gentle manipulation and typically resolves on its own without treatment. This distinction is a common and testable assessment point.

Clubfoot — Complications if untreated

Permanent gait abnormality, chronic pain, and significant mobility impairment if the deformity is not corrected during the window when bones/ligaments remain most malleable in infancy.

Clubfoot — Diagnostics

Primarily a clinical/visual diagnosis on physical exam; x-ray may be used to assess bone alignment and confirm severity or monitor correction progress during treatment.

Priority nursing interventions & considerations — Clubfoot
  1. Attempt gentle repositioning on initial assessment to help distinguish true clubfoot from positional deformity — document whether the foot corrects easily or resists.
  2. Support early referral to orthopedics, since Ponseti casting works best when started shortly after birth while tissues are most malleable.
  3. Teach parents cast care: keep the cast dry, monitor toes for color/temperature/swelling with each cast change, and report any sign of impaired circulation immediately.
Notify provider ifToes appear dusky, cold, or significantly swollen after cast application (possible circulatory compromise); the cast becomes wet, cracked, or visibly loose; the foot does not appear to be progressively correcting over successive cast changes.
Clubfoot — Treatment

Serial casting (Ponseti method) starting shortly after birth — the foot is gradually repositioned with weekly cast changes, often followed by a bracing phase to maintain correction. Surgery is reserved for cases not responsive to casting.

Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationYour baby's hip exam showed some looseness in the hip joint. We'll place a soft harness called a Pavlik harness that holds the hip in the right position while it develops normally over the next several weeks — we'll show you exactly how to diaper and check skin without removing it. For clubfoot, casting starts early and is changed weekly to gradually correct the foot's position — this is a gradual process, not a single fix, and getting to every cast-change appointment matters for the outcome.
Other Caregiver EducationIf this baby is in a Pavlik harness or cast, do not remove it unless specifically instructed by the parents/provider. Ask the parents to show you diapering and positioning technique before you're left alone with the baby, and report any toe color change, swelling, or the harness/cast looking loose or displaced.
Tracheoesophageal Fistula (TEF) & Esophageal Atresia
▸
Pathophysiology

Incomplete separation of the esophagus and trachea during embryonic development; the esophagus may end in a blind pouch (atresia) and/or an abnormal connection (fistula) may exist between the esophagus and trachea, allowing secretions/feeds to pass into the lungs.

Signs & symptoms
  • Excessive oral secretions/drooling, frothy bubbles at the mouth and nose
  • Choking, coughing, or cyanosis with feeding attempts
  • Inability to pass a feeding tube into the stomach (with esophageal atresia)
  • Abdominal distention (air passing through a fistula into the stomach)
Priority nursing interventions
  1. Stop oral feeding immediately if suspected — aspiration into the lungs via the fistula is the acute life-threatening risk, outranking nutritional concerns in the short term.
  2. Position with head elevated (upright/semi-upright) and suction oral secretions frequently to minimize aspiration risk while awaiting surgical repair.
  3. Prepare for IV fluids/TPN since oral feeding is not safe pre-repair.
Notify provider ifChoking, coughing, or cyanosis with any feeding attempt; excessive frothy oral secretions in a newly born infant; inability to pass an orogastric/nasogastric tube.
Treatment

Surgical repair to close the fistula and anastomose the esophagus; timing depends on the infant's stability and specific anatomic variant.

Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationYour baby's esophagus (the tube that carries food to the stomach) didn't form completely separate from the airway, so feeding by mouth isn't safe right now. Your baby will get nutrition through an IV until surgery can repair the connection.
Other Caregiver EducationThis baby cannot be fed by mouth under any circumstances until the care team says it's safe — never attempt to feed even a small amount, as this can cause choking or send fluid into the lungs.
Congenital Diaphragmatic Hernia (CDH) & Choanal Atresia
▸
CDH — Pathophysiology

The diaphragm fails to form completely, allowing abdominal organs to herniate into the thoracic cavity, compressing the developing lung(s) and impairing normal lung development. Occurs in about 1 in 2,200 births; survival rate ~50%.

CDH — Signs & symptoms
  • Respiratory distress at birth with diminished breath sounds and difficulty ventilating
  • Bowel sounds auscultated in the thoracic cavity
  • Cyanosis, bradycardia
  • Scaphoid (sunken) abdomen combined with a barrel chest — classic paired finding
CDH — Diagnostics

Chest x-ray shows bowel loops in the thoracic cavity and a displaced heart.

Priority nursing interventions
  1. Maintain airway and oxygenation — this outranks everything else, since lung compression from herniated organs directly threatens gas exchange.
  2. Ensure a nasogastric/orogastric tube is in place to decompress the bowel and reduce further lung compression.
  3. Prepare for respiratory support, possibly ECMO, to stabilize before surgery.
CDH — Treatment

No prevention exists; early prenatal detection allows delivery planning at a Level III NICU capable of ECMO. Surgical closure of the diaphragm is performed once the infant is stabilized and the bowel is returned to the abdomen.

Choanal Atresia — Pathophysiology

Bony or membranous blockage of the posterior nasal passage(s); newborns are obligate nose breathers, so this can cause significant respiratory distress, especially during feeding.

Choanal Atresia — Signs & symptoms

Cyanosis that worsens with feeding and improves with crying (crying opens the mouth, bypassing the obstruction) — a distinctive pattern. Difficulty passing a catheter through the nares.

Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationYour baby's diaphragm (the muscle that helps with breathing) didn't fully close before birth, which let some organs move into the chest space. This needs surgery to fix, and your baby will need breathing support until then. The team is closely monitoring oxygen levels and breathing.
Other Caregiver EducationIf this baby had CDH repair, follow any specific positioning or feeding precautions from the care team, and report any new breathing difficulty right away.
Abdominal Wall Defects: Omphalocele & Gastroschisis
▸
Pathophysiology
  • Gastroschisis: the abdominal wall fails to close, usually to the right of the umbilicus, and the intestines are exposed directly to amniotic fluid/air with NO covering membrane
  • Omphalocele: intestines protrude into the umbilical cord region but remain covered by a thin membrane (peritoneum). Defect >4 cm is an omphalocele; <4 cm is usually just an umbilical hernia and typically doesn't need repair
Key distinguishing feature

Omphalocele is often associated with other anomalies (trisomy 13, trisomy 18, urinary tract anomalies) since it reflects a broader embryologic problem. Gastroschisis is typically an isolated defect.

Complications

Hypothermia and massive insensible fluid loss from exposed bowel (gastroschisis especially); infection; bowel ischemia if mesenteric vessels kink; intestinal atresia if surgery is delayed.

Priority nursing interventions
  1. Cover exposed bowel with sterile, moist gauze and wrap in plastic immediately — prevents both infection and catastrophic fluid/heat loss, the most urgent short-term threats.
  2. Position the newborn supine with the bowel supported above the level of the defect to prevent kinking of mesenteric vessels and preserve blood supply.
  3. Insert NG/OG tube to decompress the bowel and reduce distention.
  4. Initiate IV fluids at increased maintenance rate (1.5x normal) to replace ongoing insensible losses from exposed bowel.
Notify provider ifSigns of bowel ischemia (color change of exposed bowel, e.g., dusky/dark), temperature instability, or signs of hypovolemia (tachycardia, poor perfusion) — fluid losses can be dramatic and rapid.
Treatment

Surgical repair within 2-4 hours of birth if one-stage closure is possible; larger defects use a "silo" device to gradually reduce bowel into the abdomen over 7-10 days before final closure. Postoperative focus: fluid/electrolyte balance, TPN via central line, infection prevention, pain management.

Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationYour baby was born with the bowel outside the belly wall. We're keeping it covered and moist to protect it, and your baby will need surgery — sometimes done in one step, sometimes gradually over a week or so. We'll explain each stage before it happens.
Other Caregiver EducationIf caring for this baby post-repair, follow the specific incision care and feeding instructions from the surgical team closely, since recovery needs are different from a typical newborn.

Hypospadias, Epispadias & Bladder Exstrophy
▸
Hypospadias / Epispadias
  • Hypospadias: urethral opening located behind the glans or anywhere along the ventral (underside) surface of the penile shaft
  • Epispadias: meatal opening located on the dorsal (top) surface of the penis; often associated with bladder exstrophy
  • Management: surgical repair required
Bladder Exstrophy — Pathophysiology

Failure of the abdominal wall and anterior bladder wall to close during development, leaving the bladder exposed/everted on the lower abdomen.

Bladder Exstrophy — Priority interventions

Cover the exposed bladder mucosa with sterile, non-adherent, moist dressing (e.g., plastic wrap) to keep tissue protected and moist prior to surgical closure. Protect from infection and trauma.

Circumcision cautionInfants with hypospadias/epispadias should NOT be circumcised before surgical repair — the foreskin may be needed as tissue for the reconstructive procedure.
Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationYour baby's urinary opening isn't in the typical location, which will need surgical correction, usually done in the first year. Please hold off on circumcision until the urologist confirms it's safe — the extra tissue may be needed for the repair.
Other Caregiver EducationDo not agree to or arrange a circumcision for this baby without explicit confirmation from the parents and urologist, since timing matters for the surgical repair.

The Meaning of Infant Loss for Parents
▸
Why this loss is unique

The loss of an infant represents three overlapping losses: loss of a part of themselves (especially for mothers), loss of the potential for immortality that offspring represent, and loss of the "dream child" fantasized about throughout pregnancy. Parents may experience a profound sense of emptiness and failure that differs from grief for an older family member.

A unique grief challenge

When an infant has lived only a short time, parents may have few or no pleasant memories to draw on for the identification/idealization process that is normally part of grief resolution — this is part of why creating tangible mementos (covered below) matters so much in this specific context.

Scope of perinatal loss

Perinatal loss is not limited to neonatal death — it also encompasses miscarriage, stillbirth, and therapeutic abortion. Loss and grief responses can also occur with the birth of a preterm infant, a newborn with birth complications, a newborn with congenital anomalies, or when a birthing parent is placing a child for adoption. The grief-support principles in this section apply across this full range, not only to infant death after birth.

Individual variationGrief responses vary by gender, culture, and religious belief, and grief does not proceed in a predictable, linear sequence. Not every intervention below is appropriate for every family — always assess and ask rather than assuming.
Care Setting & Time With the Infant
▸
Care setting options

Families may choose the NICU/nursery, or take the infant home with hospice services until death occurs. Parents can view, hold, bathe, or dress the infant as they wish; some also want a member of the clergy present for baptism.

Postpartum unit placement

After the birth, the woman should be given the choice of staying on the maternity unit or moving to another unit — she may want distance from the sound of crying babies and other families with healthy infants. However, staff on a general unit are typically less experienced in bereavement care, so this is a genuine tradeoff to discuss with the family, not an automatic transfer.

Time with the infant after death

Some families need only a few minutes; others want hours. The nurse watches for verbal and nonverbal cues that the family is ready to say goodbye before taking the infant from the room.

Nursing interventions to support this time
  1. Communicate therapeutically and actively listen — give parents uninterrupted time to grieve rather than filling silence.
  2. Offer to notify the hospital chaplain or another religious/spiritual support person, as the family wishes.
  3. Discuss options concretely: seeing, holding, bathing, or dressing the infant; visitation by other family or friends; religious/spiritual/cultural rituals; funeral arrangements.
  4. If possible, mark the door per agency procedure (while maintaining confidentiality) so staff entering the room know this family has experienced a loss.
Maternal self-care during this timeIt's okay to ask for what you need — more time, less time, privacy, or company. There is no "right" way to grieve, and staff can help arrange the environment (lighting, visitors, quiet, unit placement) to match what feels right for you.
Tangible Mementos
▸
Bereavement/memory packet may include
  • Photographs — ideally showing the infant being held or touched by an adult, not a morgue-style image; some hospitals take digital photos and give parents the memory card to view whenever they feel ready, since parents may not want to see photos immediately at the time of death
  • Handprints and footprints, a lock of hair
  • Bedside name card, ID bracelet/armbands
  • Certificate of baptism, if aligned with the family's beliefs
  • A special memories box to hold these keepsakes together
Naming the infant

An important grieving step. Some parents may hesitate to use a name chosen during pregnancy for their "special baby" — but having a tangible, named person to grieve for is an important part of the grieving process, and the nurse can gently encourage this rather than avoiding the topic.

Sensitive Discussions & Ongoing Support
▸
Continuity of care

A nurse familiar to the family should be present for discussions of autopsy, organ donation, and disposition of the body. These conversations should be approached with respect, sensitivity to cultural/religious beliefs, tact, and consideration of the family's wishes.

Funeral arrangements

The nurse should talk with parents openly and honestly about funeral arrangements, since few parents have prior experience with this. It's often helpful for the unit to maintain a list of local funeral homes, services offered, and prices — a funeral/memorial ritual is generally preferable to no service at all, since it provides an opportunity for parents to feel the support of friends and family.

Support organizations
  • Compassionate Friends
  • AMEND (Aiding Mothers and Fathers Experiencing Neonatal Death)
  • Share Pregnancy and Infant Loss Support, Inc.
  • Now I Lay Me Down to Sleep (bereavement photography)
Follow-up after discharge

Before leaving the hospital, parents should be given the unit's phone number and invited to call anytime with questions. Many NICUs contact parents several weeks after a neonatal death specifically to assess coping mechanisms, evaluate the grieving process, and provide additional support/referrals as needed.

Additional nursing interventions
  1. Admit the birthing parent to a private room when possible.
  2. Provide information about support groups and refer to other healthcare professionals (counseling, social work) as appropriate.
  3. Recognize and refer for complicated bereavement risk — families experiencing perinatal loss are at increased risk, and early social work involvement can help mobilize support before problems compound.
Maternal self-care strategies
  • Accept help from support organizations and follow-up calls rather than declining out of a sense that you should be "managing fine"
  • Allow physical postpartum recovery needs (rest, nutrition, incision/perineal care) to continue even while grieving — your body still needs care during this time
  • Consider individual or couples counseling, since grief can affect partners differently and create strain if unaddressed
Maternal Death
▸
Overview

Maternal death can result from hemorrhage, infection, pulmonary embolism, hypertension, cardiomyopathy, or trauma — and is often sudden and unexpected. This compounds grief significantly beyond a single loss: the partner, children, and extended family experience loss simultaneously with (or shortly after) any loss of the infant, and if the infant survives, the partner is suddenly faced with parenting a newborn without the mother present.

Family context to consider
  • Young parents may never have experienced significant personal loss or tragedy before — their own parents/grandparents may still be living, leaving them without a frame of reference
  • Cultural beliefs and customs surrounding death strongly influence the family's response
  • Each family member's grief response will vary; there is no single predictable pattern
  • If other children are in the household, the family faces greater practical challenges adjusting to life without the mother
Hospital protocol & nursing role

The hospital chaplain and social worker often provide the initial contact with the family in a private room. As soon as possible, they're joined by the obstetrician, perinatologist, and nurse. The first meeting is typically brief, focused on offering empathy and compassion and assuring the family of updates as information becomes available — not on delivering exhaustive clinical detail immediately. If the infant survived and is in the NICU, the family can be escorted to the bedside.

Nursing interventions
  1. Assist the family with "next steps" for funeral arrangements, alongside the chaplain/social worker.
  2. Refer to social services to help the family mobilize support systems and access counseling — families experiencing maternal death are at risk for complicated bereavement and altered parenting of the surviving infant and other children.
  3. Provide follow-up care as the family progresses through grief and adjusts to life without the mother; this is an ongoing need, not a one-time intervention.
Staff impact — this is explicitly part of professional practice, not incidentalGuilt, anger, fear, sadness, and depression are all common staff responses to a maternal death. Staff may participate in a debriefing session to review the events, their own participation in care, and their emotional response. Attending the memorial or funeral service can benefit both staff and family, and follow-up conferences with a social worker/grief counselor can help staff process their own grief.
The Nurse's Own Grief & Coping
▸
Professional value explicitly named in USLO 5

NICU nurses experience real grief when caring for critically ill or dying infants — they may feel helpless and sorrowful. It is important that this grief be allowed, not suppressed in the name of professionalism.

Common misconception to correct

Fear that showing emotion is unprofessional, or that expressing grief signals "loss of control," is unfounded. Research demonstrates that to continue functioning as effective managers and providers of care, nurses must be allowed to grieve and support each other through the process — suppressing it does not preserve professionalism, it undermines sustainable practice.

Healthy coping practices for the nurse
  1. Attend the funeral or memorial service when possible — this is a legitimate, supported part of working through grief, not an overstep of professional boundaries.
  2. Participate in unit debriefing sessions after a difficult loss.
  3. Lean on peer support among colleagues who understand the specific nature of NICU/perinatal loss.
  4. Seek follow-up support (counseling, chaplaincy, employee assistance) when a loss affects you personally — this is a sign of sustainable professional practice, not weakness.
Exam pearl: A question describing a nurse who cried after a patient's death, or who wants to attend the funeral, is testing whether you recognize this as a healthy, supported professional response — not something requiring correction or a sign of poor boundaries.

Palliative Care Philosophy & Decision-Making
▸
What palliative care means for a neonate

Palliative care is implemented when the decision has been made not to prolong life through aggressive intervention, and the goal of care shifts from cure to comfort. This is distinct from grief support after death has already occurred — palliative care is active care while the infant is still alive, focused on minimizing suffering and maximizing meaningful time for the family.

When this decision arises
  • A condition incompatible with life is identified prenatally or at birth (e.g., anencephaly — see full card, Screening/Anomalies)
  • A high-risk infant's condition is determined to be unsurvivable despite maximal intervention
  • Parents, in partnership with the care team, choose to prioritize comfort over continued aggressive treatment for an infant with a poor prognosis

Professional Values & Ethical Framework (USLO 5)
▸
The nursing process still applies

Even in palliative/end-of-life care, the nurse's professional standards remain the same nursing process used everywhere else: assessment (collecting data on the infant's comfort/symptoms and the family's needs), diagnosis, outcome identification (individualized — comfort and family support, not cure), planning, implementation, and evaluation. Palliative care is not "doing less nursing" — it's applying the same rigorous process toward a different goal.

Professional performance standards relevant here
  • Ethics: use the ANA Code of Ethics for Nurses to guide practice, and involve the patient's family and other providers in care decisions collaboratively rather than unilaterally
  • Advocacy: the nurse advocates for the infant and family's wishes as they navigate a difficult decision, ensuring their values — not just clinical protocol — shape the plan of care
  • Collegiality/collaboration: palliative decisions are made by an interprofessional team (neonatologist, nurse, chaplain, social worker) in partnership with the family, not by any single provider alone
Core nursing behaviors in palliative/end-of-life care
  1. Communicate honestly and compassionately about prognosis — avoid false reassurance, but deliver information with genuine empathy (see the Indicated vs. Contraindicated communication table in the NGN Case Study card).
  2. Prioritize the infant's comfort: pain/symptom management remains an active nursing responsibility throughout, not something that stops once curative treatment stops.
  3. Support the family's presence and involvement — holding, bathing, and spending time with the infant are part of the care plan, not an afterthought.
  4. Respect cultural, spiritual, and religious practices in the plan of care (e.g., baptism, specific rituals) as the family defines them.
  5. Recognize your own emotional response as a legitimate part of professional practice (see The Nurse's Own Grief & Coping card) — sustainable palliative care requires nurses supporting each other, not suppressing their reactions.
Exam pearl: Palliative care = comfort-focused, active care for a living infant; bereavement/grief support = care for the family after death. They're related but not the same thing — a question about "professional values in palliative care" is asking about care before death, even though the surrounding content in this chapter mostly covers after.
Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationChoosing palliative or comfort-focused care for your baby doesn't mean choosing to "do nothing" — it means our whole team continues actively caring for your baby, focused specifically on comfort, dignity, and time together, rather than aggressive treatments unlikely to change the outcome. You can choose to spend this time here with us or, if you prefer, take your baby home with hospice support.
Other Caregiver EducationIf supporting a family who has chosen palliative or hospice care for an infant, follow the family's lead on how involved they want you to be, and understand that this is an active, caring choice the family and care team made together — not a withdrawal of care.

Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS)

Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS)
▸
Etiology

Cause remains unknown; multiple theories exist. Peak age reported 2-3 months, up to 1 year.

Risk factors
  • Modifiable/environmental: maternal smoking, co-sleeping, unsafe sleep environment, prone or side-lying sleep, unsafe bedding, overheating/excessive bedding, secondhand smoke
  • Infant-related: low birth weight or preterm birth (<37 wks), low Apgar scores, recent viral illness, assigned male at birth
  • Non-modifiable: siblings of ≥2 SIDS victims, higher incidence in American Indian/African-American/Inuit families, parents under 20 years
Protective factors
  • Breastfeeding
  • Pacifier use
  • Supine sleep position ("Back to Sleep")
  • Room-sharing (not bed-sharing)
  • Updated immunizations
  • Tummy time (while awake, supervised)
Priority nursing actionPrevention education is the priority intervention for SIDS since there is no treatment once the event occurs — role-model safe sleep in the hospital setting and teach at every discharge/well-baby visit.
Parent / Primary Caregiver EducationAlways place your baby on their back to sleep, every time, even for naps. Use a firm mattress with nothing else in the crib — no pillows, blankets, or stuffed animals. Keep your baby's crib in your room, but not in your bed, for at least the first 6 months. Don't let anyone smoke around your baby.
Other Caregiver EducationEvery caregiver — grandparents, babysitters, daycare — must follow the same back-to-sleep, bare-crib rule every single time, even for a quick nap. Do not use a car seat, swing, or your own arms as a substitute for a firm sleep surface for planned sleep.

NCLEX-Style Practice Questions

Modeled on your instructor's NGN format. Tap an answer to see if you're correct, then read the rationale.

Q1. The nurse is caring for a preterm infant receiving surfactant therapy for RDS. Which finding requires the MOST urgent notification of the provider?
Correct: Desaturating oxygen saturation. A significant, sustained drop in SaO2 despite current respiratory support signals potential respiratory failure requiring immediate escalation of care — this is what could cause hypoxic injury or death fastest.
Q2. A mother is Rh-negative and gives birth to her first Rh-positive infant. The infant's initial assessment is unremarkable. What is the priority nursing action for the mother in the immediate postpartum period?
Correct: Administer RhoGAM within 72 hours. Even though a first pregnancy is usually unaffected, birth exposes the Rh-negative mother to Rh-positive fetal blood, creating sensitization risk for future pregnancies.
Q3. The nurse is providing discharge teaching for a newborn recently diagnosed with galactosemia via newborn screening. Which statement by the parent indicates a need for further teaching?
Correct (needs more teaching): "...we'll pump breast milk and mix it with the special formula." Breast milk contains lactose and is contraindicated in galactosemia, even mixed with specialty formula.
Q4. A newborn exposed to opioids in utero is exhibiting a high-pitched cry, tremors, and difficulty settling. The nurse should prioritize which intervention FIRST?
Correct: Score withdrawal severity using a validated tool. Objective scoring (Finnegan) determines whether pharmacologic treatment is indicated and guides ongoing management. Bright light and increased stimulation are contraindicated.
Q5. The nurse is providing discharge teaching to new parents on SIDS risk reduction. Which statement by the parents indicates a need for further teaching?
Correct (needs further teaching): "We'll let the baby sleep on her side..." Supine positioning should be maintained consistently throughout infancy, not switched at any age.
