Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus

Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus

Absolute insulin deficiency — autoimmune beta-cell destruction

Pathophysiology & Etiology

Autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells → absolute insulin deficiency. Without insulin, glucose can't enter cells → hyperglycemia. Once blood glucose exceeds the renal threshold (~180 mg/dL), glucose spills into urine (glycosuria), dragging water with it osmotically (polyuria) → compensatory polydipsia. Cells are starved despite hyperglycemia, so the body breaks down fat and protein for fuel — weight loss occurs despite polyphagia. Etiology is autoimmune, with genetic predisposition (family history is a recognized risk factor) triggered by an environmental factor (often viral) in genetically susceptible children.

Risk Factors
  • Family history of Type 1 DM (genetic susceptibility)
  • Autoimmune disease history in the child or family
  • Certain viral infections proposed as environmental triggers
  • Peak onset ages: 4–6 years and again at 10–14 years — approximately 45% of children diagnosed will present before their tenth birthday
The 3 P's + 1: Polyuria · Polydipsia · Polyphagia · Weight loss (despite eating more). Add: enuresis in a previously toilet-trained child, fatigue, blurred vision, slow wound healing, frequent infections (esp. Candida vaginitis in adolescent girls — hyperglycemic tissue is a great yeast environment).

Hypoglycemia vs. Hyperglycemia — Symptom Discrimination Lecture

Your instructor's slides isolate this exact contrast as a stand-alone teaching point — expect it tested directly, since the interventions for the two states are opposite (treat hypoglycemia with fast sugar; treat hyperglycemia with insulin/fluids).

Hypoglycemia
  • Irritable, nervous
  • Difficulty concentrating
  • Shaky feeling
  • Hunger
  • Pallor
  • Sweating
  • Headache
  • Tachycardia
  • Shallow respirations
Hyperglycemia
  • Lethargic
  • Confused
  • Double vision
  • Thirst
  • Weakness
  • Flushed, dry, crusty skin
  • Deep, rapid Kussmaul respirations
  • Fruity/acetone breath
  • Paresthesia
Quick discriminator: Hypoglycemia = "cold and clammy, give them candy" (sweating, shaky, pale — treat with fast sugar). Hyperglycemia = "hot and dry, sugar's high" (flushed, dry skin, Kussmaul, fruity breath — needs insulin/fluids, not sugar).

Glycemic Goals & Monitoring — Exact Targets Lecture

  • Blood glucose goal: 80–120 mg/dL
  • Hemoglobin A1C goal: less than 7.5%
  • Ketone monitoring: only needed if there is concern for ketonuria (not a routine daily check for every child)
  • Food restrictions should NOT be used for diabetes control in growing children unless the child is overweight — nutritional needs for normal growth take priority over rigid dietary restriction

Managing a Hypoglycemic Crisis — Exact Treatment Lecture

If blood glucose < 70 mg/dL

Rapidly give one of the following sources of fast-acting carbohydrate (about 10–15 grams each):

  • ½ to ¾ cup of orange or grape juice (a juice box works well away from home)
  • 2 glucose tablets or 2 doses of glucose gel
  • 2–4 pieces of hard candy
  • 5 gumdrops
  • 1–2 tablespoons of honey
  • 1 small box of raisins
  • 6 oz regular (not diet) soda — about half a can
  • 2 tablespoons of cake icing

Exercise & Sick-Day Management Lecture

Exercise
  • Exercise is encouraged, not restricted
  • Risk of hypoglycemia if exercise is prolonged — give an extra snack before/during
  • Exercise often reduces overall insulin requirements
Sick-Day Rules
  • Do not hold or stop insulin when the child is sick — this is the single most important sick-day rule
  • Manage fever and/or nausea/vomiting
  • Provide calories via sweetened oral rehydration solution, Gatorade, or Jell-O if the child can't tolerate regular food
  • Test urine for ketones every 3 hours on sick days, or whenever blood sugar exceeds 240 mg/dL and ketonuria is a concern

Note from lecture: sick-day management is NOT covered in the textbook — this is instructor-added content specific to this exam.

Signs & Symptoms (Full List)

Classic presenting findings
  • Polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia
  • Weight loss despite increased appetite
  • Enuresis/nocturia in a previously continent child
  • Fatigue, irritability, "not himself/herself"
  • Shortened attention span, lowered frustration tolerance
  • Blurred vision, headache
  • Dry/flushed skin, poor wound healing
  • Frequent infections (candidal vaginitis in adolescents)
Escalating — think DKA is developing
  • Kussmaul respirations (deep, rapid)
  • Fruity (acetone) breath
  • Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain
  • Dehydration signs
  • Change in level of consciousness → coma

Complications

Acute
  • Hypoglycemia
  • Hyperglycemia
  • Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)
  • Coma
  • Hypokalemia / hyperkalemia
Long-Term (after years of disease)
  • Microvascular: nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy (onset 10–15 yrs post-puberty)
  • Macrovascular: atherosclerosis, hypertension (after ~25 yrs of disease)
  • Failure to grow at normal rate, delayed maturation
  • Recurrent infections
  • Limited joint mobility (small joints of hand, ~30% of adolescents) from glycosylation changes

Good glycemic control can postpone vascular changes 20+ years; poor control can produce changes in as little as 2.5–3 years.

Glycosuria alone is not diagnostic — other conditions (infection, stress, other sugars in urine) can cause a false-positive picture. Diagnosis requires one of the four criteria above.

Notify the Provider Immediately If
  • Blood glucose > 300 mg/dL with ketones present
  • Any Kussmaul respirations, fruity breath, or vomiting in a known diabetic (possible DKA onset)
  • Blood glucose < 70 mg/dL not resolving with oral glucose (hypoglycemia unresponsive to initial treatment)
  • New change in level of consciousness

Priority Nursing Interventions — Ranked by What Kills First

  1. Will Kill First
    Obtain blood glucose and urine ketones immediately in any child with new hyperglycemic symptoms — do not administer insulin until both are known. Why: Insulin given blindly could cause dangerous hypoglycemia if the true picture is different than assumed, and DKA changes the entire treatment approach.
  2. Urgent
    Assess for signs of DKA (Kussmaul respirations, fruity breath, dehydration, altered LOC) before assuming this is uncomplicated new-onset diabetes. Why: DKA requires emergency stabilization (fluids/insulin drip), not routine outpatient diabetes teaching.
  3. Important
    Initiate multidisciplinary involvement (endocrinologist, diabetes educator, nutritionist) and begin family/child education on insulin administration, glucose monitoring, sick-day rules. Why: Long-term safety and glycemic control depend on this teaching, but it follows physiologic stabilization.

Treatment

  • Insulin replacement — cornerstone of management; 2+ injections/day or continuous SubQ pump infusion
  • Insulin needs fluctuate with emotions, nutrition, activity, illness, puberty
  • Goal: near-normal glucose while avoiding frequent hypoglycemia
  • No special "diabetic food" required — normal healthy nutrition individualized to age/growth, with consistent carbohydrate counting matched to insulin dosing
Medication Safety Alert Insulin is a high-alert medication. Independent double-check of dose before administration. Rotate injection sites to prevent lipohypertrophy, which alters absorption.

Medications for Type 1 DM — Insulin, All Types

Teaching is split into Parent/Caregiver Teaching and Pediatric Patient Teaching for each insulin type, with the patient side further broken out by developmental stage.

Insulin Lispro — Humalog

Parent / Caregiver Teaching
  • Check the child's blood glucose as prescribed and understand the child's individualized insulin schedule
  • Make sure the child has access to food when rapid-acting insulin is administered — this is rapid-onset insulin, so a delayed or skipped meal risks hypoglycemia quickly
  • Know the signs of hypoglycemia: sweating, shakiness, hunger, pallor, headache, tachycardia, irritability, difficulty concentrating
  • Keep a rapid source of glucose available at all times
  • Teach sick-day rules, glucose monitoring, ketone monitoring, injection technique, storage, and sharps disposal
  • Rotate injection sites to prevent lipohypertrophy
  • Never change insulin type or dose without following the prescribed plan
  • Keep an updated diabetes care plan available for school/daycare
Pediatric Patient Teaching By developmental stage
  • Infant/toddler: no independent medication teaching; use simple language and comfort while the caregiver administers
  • Preschooler: "This medicine helps your body use sugar." Let the child choose between two safe injection-site options when appropriate
  • School-age: teach the child to recognize "low sugar" symptoms and tell an adult immediately; begin supervised participation in glucose checks and logging
  • Adolescent: teach independent glucose checking, carbohydrate awareness, insulin administration, hypoglycemia treatment, sick-day management, and carrying diabetes supplies. Reinforce not skipping insulin just because they feel well.

Insulin Aspart — NovoLog

Parent / Caregiver Teaching
  • Same core insulin education as lispro, with special attention to timing it 5–10 minutes before meals
Pediatric Patient Teaching Meal-timing concept in concrete terms
  • Preschool: "We give this insulin right before you eat"
  • School-age: recognize symptoms of hypoglycemia and notify an adult
  • Adolescent: independently coordinate insulin, meals, glucose checks, and activities under the prescribed diabetes plan

NPH — Humulin N / Novolin N

Parent / Caregiver Teaching
  • Recognize NPH as cloudy intermediate-acting insulin — a visual identifier distinguishing it from clear insulins
  • Roll gently between the hands; do not vigorously shake
  • Know the prescribed timing and expected glucose coverage window
  • Rotate sites and watch for hypoglycemia during the peak window
Pediatric Patient Teaching
  • School-age: learn the difference between "cloudy" NPH and clear insulins as a basic safety check
  • Adolescent: participate in preparing/administering insulin if developmentally and clinically appropriate and properly trained

Insulin Glargine — Lantus

Parent / Caregiver Teaching
  • Explain that glargine provides long-acting basal insulin — a steady background dose, not a mealtime dose
  • Do not mix in the same syringe with other insulins
  • Give consistently according to the prescribed schedule, same time each day
  • Monitor for hypoglycemia even though it's long-acting — it still lowers glucose steadily throughout the day
Pediatric Patient Teaching
  • School-age: "This insulin works in the background to help keep your sugar steady"
  • Adolescent: understand the distinction between basal insulin and mealtime insulin, and the importance of maintaining the scheduled basal dose even on days when routines change

Source: Davis's Drug Guide for Nurses — insulin technique, injection-site rotation, storage/disposal, glucose/ketone monitoring, and hypo/hyperglycemia recognition.

Patient Education — Split by Audience & Developmental Stage

Parent / Caregiver Education
  • Teach insulin drawing/injection technique and glucose meter use with return demonstration
  • Sick-day management rules — insulin is never stopped during illness, even with poor appetite; check glucose/ketones more frequently
  • Recognize hypoglycemia (shaky, sweaty, confused, irritable) vs. hyperglycemia (thirsty, urinating more, fatigued) — treatments are opposite, so accurate recognition matters
  • Carbohydrate counting and matching to insulin dose
  • Coordinate care with school nurse, teacher, coach — child will need glucose checks and possible treatment during school hours
  • Understand that insulin needs change with growth spurts, puberty, activity level — this is not a static prescription
Pediatric / Child-Directed Education Adapt to developmental stage — Type 1 DM most often diagnosed in school-age children (concrete operational thinkers, ages 7–11) or adolescents (formal operational)
  • School-age (concrete thinkers): use simple, concrete demonstration rather than abstract explanation — "this button gives you the medicine your body needs" rather than a lecture on insulin physiology. Use dolls or diabetic teaching kits for injection practice. Involve the child in simple tasks (choosing injection site, pressing the meter button) to build a sense of industry/mastery appropriate to this stage.
  • Adolescent (formal operational thinkers): can understand abstract cause-and-effect and long-term consequences — appropriate to discuss why tight control matters for future complications. Respect the drive for independence: let the adolescent take a lead role in their own care, but watch for rebellion/non-adherence, which is common at this stage and often tied to body image or fitting in with peers.
  • All ages: normalize needing to test/inject at school — frame it factually, not as something to hide or feel ashamed of.

Self-Care Strategies

What the child/family does day to day
  • Check blood glucose per schedule (before meals, at bedtime, per provider orders)
  • Rotate injection sites; inspect sites for lipohypertrophy
  • Keep fast-acting glucose source (juice, glucose tabs) available at all times for hypoglycemia
  • Wear medical identification (bracelet/necklace)
  • Keep a log of glucose readings, insulin doses, and carb intake for provider visits
  • Know personal sick-day plan in advance, before illness strikes

Sources: Perry Ch. 47 (pp. 1435–1447), Box 47.13; Saunders 9th ed. Ch. 33.

Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)

Diabetic Ketoacidosis

Medical emergency — insulin insufficiency, ketosis, and acidosis

Pathophysiology & Etiology

Without insulin, cells can't use glucose, so the body burns fat for fuel. Fat breakdown produces ketone bodies (beta-hydroxybutyric acid, acetoacetic acid, acetone) — strong acids that lower serum pH → ketoacidosis. The respiratory system compensates with Kussmaul respirations to blow off CO₂. Osmotic diuresis causes severe dehydration and depletes total body potassium — even though serum potassium may look normal or high initially (acidosis shifts K+ out of cells), total body K+ is low. As insulin corrects acidosis, potassium shifts back into cells and serum levels can crash — a major reason for close monitoring during treatment. Untreated, this progresses to dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, acidosis, coma, and death.

Risk Factors / Common Precipitants
  • New-onset undiagnosed Type 1 DM (often the presenting event)
  • Missed or inadequate insulin doses
  • Illness, infection, or physiologic stress increasing insulin needs
  • Emotional stress (catecholamine release promotes fat breakdown → ketonemia)
  • Growth/puberty-related insulin resistance
Prevention Lecture
  • Consistent monitoring and control of blood sugars
  • Infection prevention

Signs & Symptoms

Expected in DKA
  • Kussmaul respirations
  • Fruity/acetone breath odor
  • Hyperglycemia, glycosuria, ketonuria
  • Dehydration (poor turgor, dry mucous membranes)
  • Hypotension (contracted blood volume)
  • Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain
Red flags — escalate immediately
  • Decreasing level of consciousness
  • Cerebral edema signs: headache, bradycardia, altered LOC, widening pulse pressure — feared complication of overly rapid correction
  • Cardiac dysrhythmias (from potassium shifts)

Complications

  • Cerebral edema — the most feared complication; linked to overly rapid fluid/glucose correction and sodium bicarbonate use
  • Hypokalemia (as acidosis corrects and K+ shifts intracellularly) → cardiac arrhythmia, cardiac arrest
  • Hypoglycemia (if glucose falls too fast once insulin drip starts and dextrose isn't added in time)
  • Progression to coma and death if untreated

Labs & Diagnostics

TestExpected (Normal)Unexpected (DKA-Diagnostic)
Blood glucose70–99 mg/dL> 200 mg/dL (Saunders/textbook threshold)
Arterial/venous pH7.35–7.45< 7.30
Serum bicarbonate22–26 mEq/L< 15 mmol/L (Saunders/textbook threshold)
Serum/urine ketonesNegativeStrongly positive

Two threshold sets you may see — know which one your exam question is drawing from

Saunders/the textbook define DKA as blood glucose > 200 mg/dL and bicarbonate < 15 mEq/L (used in the table above). Your instructor's Unit 9 presentation instead uses blood glucose > 250 mg/dL and bicarbonate < 18 mEq/L as the diagnostic cutoffs, alongside ketonuria, Kussmaul respirations, fruity breath, nausea/vomiting, and dehydration. Both number sets are clinically legitimate (different institutions/sources draw the line slightly differently) — if a question references your class slides specifically, use the >250 / <18 numbers; if it's a general NCLEX-style question, either textbook threshold is fair game. The clinical picture and priority actions (fluids → insulin → potassium monitoring) are identical either way.

Notify the Provider Immediately If
  • Any new headache, bradycardia, or change in LOC during DKA treatment — possible cerebral edema, a true emergency
  • Potassium dropping rapidly or falling below normal range during insulin therapy
  • Blood glucose not falling within the expected 50–100 mg/dL/hr range, or falling too fast
  • pH not improving despite treatment
  • New cardiac dysrhythmia

Priority Nursing Interventions — True Acuity Order (What Kills First)

  1. Will Kill First
    IV fluid rehydration with normal saline — the first action, before insulin. Why: The child is in a state of severe volume depletion; hypotension and shock from dehydration is the most immediately life-threatening issue. Correct gradually — rapid correction risks cerebral edema.
  2. Will Kill First
    Continuous IV regular insulin infusion at 0.1 units/kg/hr, started only after the initial fluid bolus. Why: This reverses the ketoacidosis itself (the acid-base derangement that can cause cardiac arrest via hyperkalemia/hypokalemia and cellular dysfunction). Given after fluids because glucose falls rapidly once volume is restored — giving insulin too early/fast on top of fluids risks precipitous glucose drop and contributes to cerebral edema risk.
  3. Urgent
    Monitor and adjust — add dextrose to IV fluids once glucose reaches 250–300 mg/dL while continuing the insulin infusion to keep resolving ketoacidosis; monitor potassium closely and replace as needed. Why: Once volume and insulin therapy are underway, the danger shifts to hypoglycemia and hypokalemia from treatment itself — these can cause cardiac arrhythmia if unmonitored.
  4. Important
    Frequent neuro checks, strict I&O, diabetic flowsheet documentation, monitor for infection (fever should be reported — common DKA precipitant). Why: Necessary ongoing safety monitoring, but sequenced after the immediate physiologic threats are addressed.
Monitoring Frequency Lecture
  • Blood glucose: monitored hourly during active DKA treatment
  • Potassium: monitored every 2–4 hours — reflects how quickly K+ can shift once insulin therapy begins
  • Ongoing respiratory and neurologic assessment throughout treatment
Potassium Replacement — Exact Dosing Lecture
Potassium usually given 20–40 mEq/L (added to IV fluids)

Place the child on a continuous EKG monitor during potassium replacement — this is a specific, testable safety step from lecture, since both hypokalemia and hyperkalemia during correction can cause dangerous cardiac dysrhythmias.

Prevention Lecture
  • Consistent monitoring and control of blood sugars
  • Infection prevention — since infection is a common precipitant of DKA
Critical Safety Points
  • NPH insulin is never given IV — only regular insulin is given IV.
  • Insulin is withheld until blood glucose AND urine ketones are obtained.
  • Sodium bicarbonate used conservatively — only for pH < 7.0, severe hyperkalemia, or cardiac instability — linked to increased cerebral edema risk. Monitor LOC closely if given.
  • Target glucose decline: 50–100 mg/dL/hr — not faster.
Important teaching point — DKA can be the first sign of diabetes Many children go into DKA before they are ever diagnosed with Type 1 diabetes — this is not a failure of care or parenting, and families should be reassured of this directly. Lecture

Treatment Summary

  1. Isotonic IV fluids (normal saline) to correct dehydration
  2. Continuous IV regular insulin infusion (0.1 units/kg/hr) after initial fluid bolus
  3. Add dextrose to IVF when glucose reaches 250–300 mg/dL; maintain glucose 120–240 mg/dL
  4. Cautious sodium bicarbonate only for severe acidosis/hyperkalemia/cardiac instability
  5. Electrolyte replacement — potassium typically 20–40 mEq/L, with continuous EKG monitoring, guided by frequent labs

Medication Spotlight: Regular Insulin — Novolin R

Parent / Caregiver Teaching
  • Know that regular insulin is short-acting and is the only insulin given IV, including in DKA
  • During DKA, expect frequent glucose and potassium monitoring
  • Understand that DKA treatment includes fluids, electrolyte replacement, and insulin together, not insulin alone
  • Never independently stop insulin during illness without following the child's sick-day plan
Pediatric Patient Teaching
  • School-age/adolescent: understand that insulin helps lower blood glucose and is especially important during DKA treatment
  • Adolescents should understand why glucose, ketones, potassium, and hydration are all monitored together during treatment

Patient Education — Split by Audience & Developmental Stage

Parent / Caregiver Education
  • Teach recognition of early DKA warning signs (fruity breath, rapid breathing, vomiting, extreme thirst) so future episodes are caught before hospitalization is needed
  • Sick-day rules: never stop insulin during illness, check glucose and ketones more frequently, know when to call the provider vs. go to ED
  • Explain that this event does not mean they failed at managing the disease — DKA can be the first sign of diabetes
Pediatric / Child-Directed Education Most DKA patients are recovering from an acute illness — during acute treatment, education is minimal/supportive; teaching ramps up once stable
  • Once stable, school-age child: simple, concrete reassurance about what happened ("your body needed help using sugar, and now the medicine and fluids are fixing that") — avoid overwhelming detail during the acute recovery period
  • Adolescent: can process a more complete explanation of what precipitated the episode (missed doses, illness, etc.) and participate in problem-solving to prevent recurrence — but avoid a shaming tone, since non-adherence in teens is often tied to normal developmental drives, not defiance

Self-Care Strategies (Post-DKA, Prevention Focus)

Preventing recurrence
  • Never skip or stop insulin, even when sick or not eating well — insulin needs often increase with illness
  • Check urine/blood ketones during any illness or when glucose is unexpectedly high
  • Have a written sick-day plan from the endocrinology team, reviewed before it's needed
  • Know the specific signs that mean "go to the ED now" vs. "call the provider"

Sources: Perry Ch. 47 (DKA pathophysiology, hospital management); Saunders 9th ed. Ch. 33 (diagnostic criteria, complications).

Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus Lecture

Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus

Insulin resistance, not absolute deficiency — not in your syllabus reading list, but covered live in lecture

Pathophysiology & Etiology

The body's cells become resistant to insulin and fail to use it properly, rather than the pancreas failing to produce it (as in Type 1). This is a relative, not absolute, insulin deficiency. Lecture

Type 1 vs. Type 2 — Quick Comparison

FeatureType 1 DMType 2 DM
Core problemAbsolute insulin deficiency (beta-cell destruction)Insulin resistance / relative deficiency
Typical body habitusOften normal weight or underweight at diagnosisObesity is the number one risk factor
OnsetOften abrupt, with clear symptomsMay have no signs at all — insidious
Classic symptoms3 P's prominentMay or may not have 3 P's; fatigue, frequent infections more common presenting complaints

Signs & Symptoms

  • May have no signs at all — this is the key distinguishing feature from Type 1
  • Obesity
  • Fatigue
  • Frequent infections, including recurrent vaginal/Candida albicans and urinary tract infections Lecture
  • Can also present with the classic 3 P's, but less reliably than Type 1

Diagnosis

Obesity > 85th percentile for age/sex/weight PLUS two additional risk factors:

  • Family history (1st or 2nd degree relative)
  • Race/ethnicity — Native American, African American, Latino, Asian, Pacific Islander
  • Insulin resistance markers: acanthosis nigricans, hypertension, dyslipidemia, PCOS, small for gestational age (SGA) at birth
  • Maternal history of diabetes or gestational diabetes
Lab diagnostic criterion Lecture Two random blood glucose values greater than 200 mg/dL support the diagnosis, in the context of the risk factors above.
Analyze Cues → An obese 13-year-old with acanthosis nigricans and a mother with gestational diabetes history, presenting with fatigue and no classic 3 P's, fits the Type 2 risk profile far better than Type 1 — recognizing this pattern is the clinical judgment skill being tested.

Complications of Type 2 DM Lecture

  • Growth failure
  • Delayed puberty
  • Menstrual disturbances
  • Emotional disturbances
  • Cataracts
  • Impaired cognitive function
  • Hyperlipidemia
  • Breakdown or buildup of subcutaneous tissue at the injection site (lipohypertrophy/lipoatrophy, same concept as Type 1)

Treatment & Patient Education Considerations

  • Lifestyle modification (nutrition, physical activity, healthy weight) is central and is the primary prevention strategy Lecture
  • Blood glucose monitoring and Hemoglobin A1c tracking, same as Type 1 Lecture
  • Insulin (rapid, short, intermediate, or long-acting) may be required depending on severity Lecture
  • Oral hypoglycemic agent: Metformin — a first-line oral medication distinct from Type 1 management, which relies on insulin alone Lecture
  • Carbohydrate counting and nutritional education Lecture
  • Family-centered approach is essential — habits usually need to change household-wide, not just for the affected child
  • Screen for and address comorbidities (hypertension, dyslipidemia, PCOS) as part of the full picture

Medication Spotlight: Metformin — Glucophage

Parent / Caregiver Teaching
  • Give with meals to reduce GI side effects
  • Monitor glucose/HbA1c and renal function per the follow-up schedule
  • Know that severe illness, dehydration, severe diarrhea, surgery, and iodinated contrast dye may require temporarily stopping the medication per the medical plan, due to lactic acidosis risk — this is a specific safety teaching point from Davis's Drug Guide
  • Never double a missed dose
  • Reinforce diet, exercise, and weight-management recommendations as part of the whole treatment picture, not medication alone
Pediatric Patient Teaching Type 2 DM is typically diagnosed in school-age/adolescent patients
  • School-age: explain that it helps the body use its own insulin better; emphasize taking it with meals
  • Adolescent: teach medication adherence, healthy eating, exercise, glucose monitoring, and symptoms requiring medical attention such as persistent vomiting, dehydration, or unusual weakness
  • Encourage the adolescent to understand that the medication controls diabetes — it does not cure it, and stopping it does not mean the diabetes is gone

Source: Davis's Drug Guide for Nurses (Metformin — lactic acidosis precautions, hold-for-contrast teaching).

Precocious Puberty

Precocious Puberty (Central & Peripheral)

Premature activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis

Pathophysiology & Etiology

Puberty before 8 years old in girls or 9 years old in boys is precocious (newer guidelines: before 7 in Caucasian girls, before 6 in African-American girls).

  • Central precocious puberty (CPP): hypothalamic GnRH prematurely activates the pituitary-gonadal axis, following the normal sequence just too early. 90–95% idiopathic; more common in girls. Organic causes: congenital CNS anomalies, infection (encephalitis, meningitis), trauma, or CNS tumors.
  • Peripheral precocious puberty (PPP): sex hormone excess from gonads/adrenal glands without hypothalamic-pituitary activation — e.g., congenital adrenal hyperplasia, gonadal tumors, primary hypothyroidism, exogenous hormone exposure.
Risk Factors
  • Female sex (CPP more common in girls)
  • CNS congenital anomalies, prior CNS infection, trauma, or tumor
  • Untreated primary hypothyroidism (a PPP cause)
  • Congenital adrenal hyperplasia or gonadal/adrenal tumor
  • Exogenous sex hormone exposure

Signs & Symptoms — By Sex Lecture

Boys
  • Facial hair
  • Penile growth
  • Increased masculinity
  • Testicular enlargement
  • Voice changes
Girls
  • Breast development
  • Onset of menarche
  • Ovary enlargement
  • Ovarian cysts
Both sexes
  • Axillary and pubic hair
  • Body odor, acne
  • Emotional lability, mood swings
  • Growth spurts
Expected clinical picture
  • Advanced bone age on x-ray
  • Chronologic age and mental/cognitive age remain congruent — the child thinks and acts their actual age
Findings warranting urgent CNS workup
  • Headache, visual changes (possible CNS tumor)
  • Neurologic signs suggesting hypothalamic lesion or brain injury
  • Very young age of onset (higher likelihood of organic cause)
Etiology emphasis from lecture Instructor slides frame precocious puberty specifically as overactivity of the pituitary gland, caused by CNS abnormalities, lesions/tumors, or brain injury — a slightly narrower framing than the textbook's central-vs-peripheral breakdown above. Know both framings: the textbook distinction (central vs. peripheral) for pathophysiology depth, and this pituitary-overactivity framing for how your instructor is likely to phrase exam questions.

Complications / Adverse Consequences Linked to Early Puberty Lecture

  • Short adult stature from premature epiphyseal closure — the central long-term physical risk, even though the child is tall for age initially
  • Psychosocial distress — child looks older than peers, may face inappropriate social expectations from adults and peers who react to physical appearance rather than actual age
  • Increased risk of mismatched social/emotional expectations, since physical maturity outpaces cognitive/emotional maturity
  • If organic (CNS tumor, CAH): complications tied to the underlying cause itself

Hypothyroidism — Congenital

Congenital Hypothyroidism

Present at birth — time-critical for neurodevelopment

Pathophysiology & Etiology

Low circulating thyroid hormone (T4/T3) with elevated TSH at birth, from thyroid gland dysgenesis, hormone synthesis defects, or (rarely) maternal factors. TH regulates basal metabolic rate and is essential for growth and tissue differentiation — deficiency has a more profound effect on growth than GH deficiency because TH touches more systems. Untreated congenital hypothyroidism causes severe, irreversible neurocognitive dysfunction because brain growth is nearly complete by age 2–3 — this is why newborn screening exists and treatment cannot wait.

Risk Factors
  • Thyroid gland dysgenesis or agenesis (congenital)
  • Inborn errors of thyroid hormone synthesis
  • Maternal antithyroid drug use or iodine deficiency/excess during pregnancy
  • Family history of thyroid disease

Signs & Symptoms

Key point from lecture Clinical manifestations are subtle at birth — this is exactly why universal newborn screening exists rather than relying on physical exam alone. By the time symptoms are obvious, the window for preventing neurologic damage has already started closing.
Newborn screening findings
  • Universal newborn screening detects this before symptoms appear in most cases
  • Low T4, elevated TSH on screen
Infant, if untreated / late-recognized Lecture
  • Prolonged jaundice
  • Poor feeding
  • Constipation
  • Cool, mottled skin
  • Hypotonia
  • Sleepiness, decreased crying
  • Larger fontanels
  • Large, thick tongue
  • Enlarged thyroid (goiter) — can cause severe respiratory distress
Congenital hypothyroidism infant picture: think "the slow, puffy, quiet baby" — everything is sluggish (constipation, sleepiness, poor feeding) and everything looks puffy/large (fontanels, tongue). This contrasts sharply with a hyperthyroid infant, who would be irritable, tachycardic, and hyperactive.
If Missed Beyond Infancy — Child Presentation Lecture

If congenital hypothyroidism is not caught by newborn screening and progresses untreated into childhood, the picture evolves to include:

  • Short stature
  • Delays in developmental milestones
  • Weight gain
  • Hypotonia
  • Puffy facial features
  • Intellectual disability
  • Protruding abdomen
  • Sparse, coarse, dry, or brittle hair

Complications

  • Irreversible intellectual disability if treatment is delayed — the central complication
  • Growth failure
  • Airway obstruction if goiter present at birth

Priority Nursing Interventions — Acuity Order

  1. Will Kill First (if goiter present)
    Assess and protect the airway if a goiter is present at birth — have emergency ventilation equipment ready. Why: A large congenital goiter can cause airway obstruction, an immediate life threat, though this is uncommon.
  2. Urgent
    Ensure newborn screening is completed and results followed up promptly; if diagnosis is confirmed, start thyroid hormone replacement as soon as possible.   3. Important
    Educate family on lifelong daily compliance and need for periodic serum thyroid level monitoring.

Treatment

  • Levothyroxine (thyroid hormone replacement) started as soon as diagnosis confirmed — prompt treatment in infants, unlike the more gradual approach used in older children
  • Lifelong therapy with periodic dose adjustment as the child grows

Patient Education — Split by Audience & Developmental Stage

Parent / Caregiver Education
  • Explain why immediate treatment matters — this is the single most important message: delay risks permanent intellectual disability
  • Teach correct daily administration of levothyroxine (crushed tablet in small amount of liquid/formula — not mixed into a full bottle, since if the infant doesn't finish the bottle, the dose is missed)
  • Teach signs of both under- and over-treatment to watch for as the child grows
  • Explain the need for lifelong therapy and periodic labs — this is not a medication that will be discontinued
Pediatric / Child-Directed Education Diagnosed in infancy — direct child education isn't applicable yet; education is aimed entirely at caregivers until the child is old enough to participate
  • As the child grows into toddler/preschool years, begin very simple language: "this medicine helps your body every day" — building toward eventual self-management education
  • By school age, begin involving the child in simple parts of their care (e.g., helping remember the daily dose) as a foundation for future independence

Self-Care Strategies

Ongoing family management
  • Give levothyroxine at the same time every day, consistently, ideally on an empty stomach for reliable absorption
  • Keep a written log of doses given, especially in infancy when a caregiver may not be the same person every day
  • Attend all scheduled lab draws — dose changes with growth are expected and normal
  • Never stop the medication without provider guidance, even if the child seems "fine"

Hypothyroidism — Juvenile (Acquired)

Juvenile (Acquired) Hypothyroidism

Onset after the newborn period — less neuro-urgent, still needs treatment

Pathophysiology & Etiology

Occurs after infancy from: congenital hypoplastic thyroid becoming inadequate with growth demands, thyroidectomy, radiotherapy (e.g., after Hodgkin lymphoma treatment), infectious/autoimmune processes (Hashimoto/lymphocytic thyroiditis is the most common cause), or iodine deficiency (rare in the U.S. due to iodized salt). Because brain growth is nearly complete by age 2–3, intellectual disability and neurologic sequelae are NOT associated with juvenile-onset hypothyroidism — the key distinction from the congenital form and a favorite NCLEX contrast point.

Risk Factors
  • Autoimmune thyroiditis (Hashimoto disease) — most common cause in children/adolescents, peak incidence in adolescence
  • History of thyroidectomy or head/neck radiotherapy
  • Family history of autoimmune thyroid disease
  • Dietary iodine deficiency (rare in U.S.)

Signs & Symptoms

Clinical picture
  • Decelerated growth/growth cessation in a previously normally-growing child
  • Myxedematous skin changes: dry skin, puffiness around eyes, sparse hair
  • Constipation, sleepiness/lethargy, mental decline (slowed cognition that improves with treatment — not permanent intellectual disability)
  • Possible thyromegaly (goiter) — firm, freely movable, nontender, symmetric enlargement
  • Possible tracheal compression symptoms: sense of fullness, hoarseness, dysphagia

Complications

  • Growth delay if prolonged untreated
  • Progressive myxedema symptoms
  • Rarely: airway compromise from significant goiter

Scenario: The nurse is reviewing the EHR of a 4-year-old whose caregivers have concerns about growth and behavior: more tired than usual, less active in play, constipation for several weeks, weight gain despite decreased appetite, no height increase in 6 months, skin cool and dry on assessment. Phenylketonuria (PKU) / Phenylalanine Hydroxylase Deficiency

PKU (PAH Deficiency)

Autosomal recessive metabolic disorder — dietary management is treatment

Pathophysiology & Etiology

PKU/PAH deficiency is an autosomal recessive disorder — an inherited error of metabolism resulting from absence/deficiency of the enzyme (phenylalanine hydroxylase) that metabolizes the amino acid phenylalanine, an amino acid found in proteins. Without this enzyme, phenylalanine accumulates to toxic levels in the blood, causing central nervous system damage. Classic example of gene-environment interaction: the enzyme defect is genetic, but damage only occurs after the infant ingests phenylalanine-containing substances (breast milk or standard formula) — the "environmental trigger." There is no prevention for the underlying genetic defect itself Lecture — the only intervention is early detection and dietary management to prevent the toxic buildup.

Risk Factors
  • Both parents are carriers of the recessive gene (25% recurrence risk in future pregnancies if both parents are carriers)
  • Family history of PKU
  • Consanguinity (increases odds both parents carry the same recessive allele)
Lab values to know:
Normal phenylalanine: 0–2 mg/dL (0–121 mcmol/L)
PKU diagnostic threshold: > 20 mg/dL (1210 mcmol/L)
A result of 1 mg/dL is a negative screen — don't be tricked by a low-looking number; compare against the actual normal range.
Key point from lecture Lecture No signs or symptoms are present at birth. This is why the disorder is picked up exclusively through the metabolic newborn screen — a newborn with PKU looks completely normal on physical exam. Symptoms only emerge after ongoing phenylalanine exposure causes cumulative toxicity.

Signs & Symptoms

In all children (untreated, over time)
  • Digestive problems, vomiting
  • Seizures
  • Musty odor of urine
  • Developmental delays
  • Intellectual disability if untreated
Older children if undiagnosed/untreated
  • Eczema
  • Hypertonia
  • Hypopigmentation of hair, skin, irises
  • Behavioral abnormalities — hyperactivity, bizarre/schizoid behavior
Musty urine + fair skin/hair/eyes that don't match family coloring + developmental concerns → think PKU. The classic clue combination on NCLEX questions.

Complications

  • Irreversible intellectual disability if phenylalanine restriction is not maintained — the central risk
  • Seizures
  • Behavioral and psychiatric symptoms if diet is not followed
  • Maternal PKU syndrome risk: if a woman with PKU does not follow the diet during her own pregnancy (regardless of the fetus's genotype), her elevated phenylalanine can cause fetal intellectual disability, microcephaly, and congenital heart defects — an important teaching point for adolescent females with PKU

Priority Nursing Interventions — Acuity Order

  1. Urgent
    Ensure universal newborn screening is completed — the infant must have started formula or breast milk feeding before specimen collection (protein intake is required to unmask the defect on the test). Why: Missing or mistiming this screen delays diagnosis, and every week of delayed dietary treatment adds cumulative, irreversible neurologic risk.
  2. Urgent
    If initial screening is positive, arrange repeat testing and further diagnostic evaluation; rescreen by 14 days of age if the first screen was done before 24 hours of life.
  3. Important
    If confirmed, immediately restrict phenylalanine intake and begin monitoring physical, neurological, and intellectual development.

Treatment — Dietary Management

The treatment IS the diet There is no drug cure — dietary phenylalanine restriction is the primary, lifelong treatment. Your instructor's slides summarize this simply as a "phenylalanine-free diet" that eliminates proteins — the detailed food breakdown below from Saunders gives you the specifics behind that summary. Lecture

Foods Patients Can and Cannot Eat Lecture

Phe = phenylalanine, the amino acid that must be restricted.

Can Eat in Moderation
  • Fruits and vegetables naturally low in Phe: apples, bananas, berries, cabbage, carrots, tomatoes
  • Low-protein foods: low-protein bread and pasta, cornstarch, tapioca, arrowroot
  • Fats: butter, margarine, vegetable oil, coconut oil, ghee
  • Sweeteners: sugar, honey, maple syrup, molasses
  • Drinks: coffee, green or black tea, fruit juice
  • Medical foods, shakes, and powders specifically designed for PKU
Must Avoid
  • High-protein foods: meat, fish, poultry, eggs, beans, nuts
  • Dairy (milk, cheese, yogurt)
  • Certain grains: wheat, oats, rye, barley, quinoa

Patient Education — Split by Audience & Developmental Stage

Parent / Caregiver Education
  • Correct diet is the primary treatment — this is lifelong, not a temporary intervention
  • Specifically teach: avoid meats/dairy/eggs (high phenylalanine) and aspartame-containing products; cereals/pasta/rice/fruits/vegetables in appropriate amounts are the foundation
  • Teach careful food label reading — phenylalanine and aspartame can appear in unexpected processed foods
  • Stress consistent follow-up appointments to monitor phenylalanine levels and developmental progress
  • Encourage parents to express feelings about the diagnosis; discuss autosomal recessive inheritance and recurrence risk in future pregnancies — refer for genetic counseling
  • Educate on special low-phenylalanine formula use; connect family with social services for the financial burden of specialized formula and foods, which are often not fully covered by insurance
  • If the child is or will become a female of childbearing age: she will need to understand maternal PKU risk and the importance of resuming/maintaining strict dietary control before and during any future pregnancy
Pediatric / Child-Directed Education Lifelong condition — education evolves from caregiver-only to increasing child self-management with age
  • Toddler/preschool (preoperational, 2–7 yrs): education is caregiver-directed; begin very simple concrete language around "safe foods" vs. "foods that aren't safe for your body" as the child starts to eat independently
  • School-age (concrete operational, 7–11 yrs): can begin learning to read simple food labels and identify safe snacks with supervision; use concrete tools like color-coded food lists or picture charts rather than abstract explanations of phenylalanine metabolism
  • Adolescent (formal operational, 11+ yrs): can understand the metabolic rationale behind the diet and increasingly manage their own choices — this is also the highest-risk period for diet non-adherence due to social pressure (wanting to eat what peers eat); acknowledge this directly rather than only lecturing about compliance, and involve the adolescent in problem-solving around school lunches, parties, and eating out

Self-Care Strategies

Ongoing family/patient management
  • Keep a running food/phenylalanine log if directed by the metabolic dietitian
  • Read every food label before introducing a new product — formulations change
  • Maintain a supply of approved low-protein specialty foods and medical formula
  • Attend all scheduled phenylalanine level checks — frequency is higher in infancy and decreases with age but never stops entirely
  • Plan ahead for school lunches, parties, and travel to avoid accidental high-phenylalanine intake
  • For adolescent/adult females: plan pregnancies in coordination with the metabolic team to ensure strict dietary control before conception
Evaluate Outcomes → Teaching is successful when the family can state: "we avoid high-protein foods and aspartame," "diet is the primary treatment," and "follow-up appointments matter" — these are the exact statements Saunders uses to test understanding in its evaluate-outcomes item.

Medication Math You Must Be Able to Do Cold

DKA Insulin Drip Rate
Regular insulin IV infusion = 0.1 units/kg/hr

Example: 30 kg child → 0.1 × 30 = 3 units/hr continuous IV regular insulin.

Remember: insulin starts after the initial fluid bolus, not simultaneously — glucose falls rapidly once volume is restored.

Expected Glucose Correction Rate in DKA
Target decline: 50–100 mg/dL per hour

Add dextrose to IV fluids once glucose falls to 250–300 mg/dL, while continuing insulin — goal is resolving acidosis, not just normalizing the number. Maintain glucose 120–240 mg/dL during this phase.

Decimal-point safety habit (applies to insulin too)

0.1 mg is 10x the dose of 0.01 mg. Always have insulin doses independently double-checked — a decimal error here is catastrophic given insulin's narrow safety margin.
