Increased Intracranial Pressure (ICP)

Increased Intracranial Pressure

The pressure of CSF in the subarachnoid space — a final common pathway for many neuro conditions

Pathophysiology & Etiology

ICP is the pressure of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in the subarachnoid space between the skull and the brain. Lecture The brain is tightly enclosed in the solid, non-expansile bony cranium. Total intracranial volume — brain (80%), CSF (10%), and blood (10%) — must remain constant. An increase in any one component (blood, CSF, or tissue mass from edema/tumor/bleeding) must be offset by a compensatory decrease in another. Children with open fontanels/unfused sutures can compensate somewhat via skull expansion, but this capacity is limited at any age. Once compensation is exhausted, any further volume increase causes a rapid, dangerous rise in ICP.

Risk Factors / Causes
  • Tumors or other space-occupying lesions
  • Accumulation of fluid within the ventricular system (hydrocephalus)
  • Intracranial bleeding
  • Cerebral edema (from trauma, infection, hypoxia)

Signs & Symptoms

The single most important early sign — any age Change in level of consciousness (LOC) is the #1, earliest sign of increased ICP at any age. LOC provides the earliest indication of neurologic improvement or deterioration — watch this above all other parameters. Lecture
Infants — Early Signs
  • Irritability, poor feeding
  • High-pitched cry
  • Tense, bulging fontanels
  • Separated cranial sutures
  • Setting-sun sign (sclera visible above the iris)
  • Distended scalp veins
  • Macewen (cracked-pot) sign on skull percussion
  • Increased head circumference
Children — Early Signs
  • Headache
  • Vomiting (with or without nausea, often forceful)
  • Seizures
  • Diplopia, blurred vision
  • Irritability, restlessness
  • Drowsiness, memory loss
  • Papilledema
  • Decline in school performance
Late Signs — Ominous, Any Age
  • Significant decrease in LOC
  • Bradycardia (paired with rising BP = late Cushing's triad — rare in children but a grave sign when present)
  • Decreased motor/sensory response to painful stimuli
  • Altered pupil size and reactivity
  • Decorticate posturing (flexion — arms flexed to chest, legs extended) — cerebral cortex dysfunction
  • Decerebrate posturing (rigid extension of arms and legs) — midbrain dysfunction, more ominous than decorticate
  • Cheyne-Stokes respirations
  • Coma
Decorticate vs. decerebrate: Decorticate = arms curl "toward the core" (flexed to chest) — cortex-level dysfunction, less severe. Decerebrate = rigid, straight-line extension — brainstem/midbrain-level dysfunction, more severe. Progression from decorticate to decerebrate posturing signals worsening injury.

Complications

  • Herniation (brain tissue forced through structures within the skull) — can be rapidly fatal
  • Permanent neurologic damage from sustained cerebral hypoperfusion
  • Death if uncontrolled

  • Any decline in LOC or GCS score, however small
  • New bradycardia, especially paired with rising blood pressure
  • New posturing (decorticate or decerebrate) or progression from one to the other
  • Pupillary changes (unequal, sluggish, or fixed/dilated)
  • New seizure activity
  • Cheyne-Stokes respirations or any respiratory pattern change

Priority Nursing Interventions — Acuity Order

  1. Will Kill First
    Monitor airway, breathing, and LOC continuously; position head of bed 15–30 degrees with head/neck midline (avoid jugular vein compression, which itself raises ICP). Why: Airway compromise and herniation from uncontrolled ICP are the most immediate threats to life; midline positioning and HOB elevation optimize venous drainage and reduce ICP directly.
  2. Will Kill First
    Check for intact gag/swallow reflexes before oral intake; use caution with suctioning and avoid overstimulation. Why: Suctioning and vigorous stimulation can transiently spike ICP further and risk aspiration in a child with impaired reflexes.
  3. Urgent
    Avoid hypotonic IV solutions; monitor for fever and use a hypothermic blanket if indicated. Lecture Why: Hypotonic fluids can worsen cerebral edema by promoting fluid shift into brain tissue; fever increases metabolic demand and can worsen ICP.
  4. Urgent
    Treat pain and agitation; maintain environmental control (dim lighting, reduced noise); institute seizure precautions and administer seizure/cerebral edema medications as ordered. Lecture
  5. Important
    Support advanced management if critical: sedation (barbiturates), paralysis, and ICP probe monitoring. Lecture

Treatment

  • Monitor vital signs, LOC, reflexes, pupil reaction continuously Lecture
  • Head of bed 15–30 degrees, head/neck midline Lecture
  • Avoid hypotonic IV solutions Lecture
  • Medications to decrease cerebral edema (e.g., hyperosmolar therapy per order)
  • Sedation (barbiturates) in critical cases; paralysis with ICP probe monitoring if severe Lecture
  • Seizure precautions and antiepileptic medication if indicated Lecture

Patient Education — Split by Audience & Developmental Stage

Parent / Caregiver Education
  • Teach the early signs of worsening ICP specific to their child's age (infant vs. older child) so they can report changes promptly at home or during hospitalization
  • Explain why positioning, minimizing stimulation, and avoiding certain fluids matter — parents often want to comfort/pick up a fussy child, so explain gently why minimizing crying/stimulation is protective right now
  • Prepare families for the possibility of advanced interventions (sedation, ICP monitoring) if the child's condition is critical
Pediatric / Child-Directed Education Adjust to developmental stage; a child with significantly altered LOC may not be able to participate in education at all
  • Infant: no verbal education possible; comfort via minimal handling, dim/quiet environment, and consistent caregiving
  • School-age (if alert enough to participate): simple, concrete explanation of why they need to stay still/quiet and why bright lights or loud noise are being minimized right now
  • Adolescent: more complete explanation of what's being monitored and why, respecting their capacity for abstract understanding if their LOC allows

Self-Care Strategies

For families managing chronic/recurring ICP risk (e.g., shunted hydrocephalus, tumor surveillance)
  • Know the child's specific early-warning signs and have a clear plan for who to call
  • Keep all neuro follow-up appointments and imaging surveillance on schedule
  • Avoid activities that pose head injury risk when the child has a known condition affecting ICP compensation

Seizures

Seizure Disorders

Excessive, unorganized neuronal discharges

Pathophysiology & Etiology

A seizure results from excessive and unorganized neuronal discharges in the brain that activate associated motor and sensory organs. Classified as generalized, partial (focal), or unclassified depending on the brain area involved. New-onset seizures always warrant a full workup, including consideration of an underlying malignant neoplasm. Lecture

Risk Factors
  • Fever (febrile seizures — most common in children 6 months–5 years)
  • Family history of seizures
  • CNS infection, trauma, or structural lesion
  • Cognitive impairment and/or cerebral palsy (highest risk group for developing epilepsy)

Signs & Symptoms

Clinical manifestations
  • Loss of consciousness/awareness
  • Motor signs — stiffening of the body Lecture
  • Automatisms (in complex partial seizures)
  • Aura preceding the event (in some seizure types)
  • Postictal state: confusion, drowsiness after the seizure ends

Complications

  • Status epilepticus — a continuous seizure lasting more than 30 minutes, or a series of seizures without return to baseline LOC between them. "Impending status epilepticus" = 5–30 minutes, with treatment expected to start within 5 minutes. This is a medical emergency requiring immediate intervention to prevent brain injury or death.
  • Aspiration/airway occlusion during a tonic-clonic seizure (lost swallowing reflex, increased salivation)
  • Injury from falls or environmental hazards during the seizure
  • Sudden Unexpected Death in Epilepsy (SUDEP) — rare, but the major risk factor is uncontrolled generalized tonic-clonic seizures, particularly during sleep

  • Seizure occurs in water
  • This is the child's first seizure

Priority Nursing Interventions — During a Seizure

  1. Will Kill First
    Protect the airway: ease the child to the floor if standing/seated (do not remove from a wheelchair), position on the side to facilitate drainage, and suction the oral cavity/posterior oropharynx if needed. Why: Loss of the swallowing reflex and increased salivation during a tonic-clonic seizure create real aspiration and airway occlusion risk — this is the most immediate life threat during an active seizure.
  2. Will Kill First
    Never restrain the child's movements and never place anything in the child's mouth. Why: Restraint can cause fractures/injury without stopping the seizure; objects placed in the mouth can cause airway obstruction, aspiration, or dental/oral injury — the old "padded tongue blade" practice is outdated and dangerous.
  3. Urgent
    Remove harmful objects from the area; do not attempt to stop the seizure — it is not possible and should not be attempted. Why: Injury prevention without interference allows the seizure to run its natural, self-limited course safely.
  4. Important
    Time the seizure and document characteristics observed (aura, motor effects, eye movements, alterations in consciousness, postictal state) — describe only what is observed, don't label the seizure type. Why: Accurate documentation is essential for diagnosis and ongoing management, but comes after the immediate safety actions above.
  5. Important
    Stay with the child and reassure until fully conscious after the seizure ends.

Treatment — Status Epilepticus (Escalating Medication Sequence)

  1. Initial support: CAB of life support (circulation, airway, breathing), check blood glucose, administer oxygen, establish IV access.
  2. First-line (in-hospital): IV diazepam or lorazepam (Ativan). If no IV access: rectal diazepam or intramuscular/intranasal/buccal midazolam — the fastest-available route is more important than which specific drug is chosen.
  3. Second-line: IV phenytoin or fosphenytoin (fosphenytoin preferred for better tolerability), followed by phenobarbital if needed.
  4. Refractory: general anesthesia with continuous infusion of midazolam, propofol, or pentobarbital; intubation and continuous EEG monitoring required at this stage.
Medication Safety Alert The combination of benzodiazepines followed by phenytoin/phenobarbital places the child at high risk for respiratory depression and apnea — respiratory support must be readily available. Diazepam is incompatible with many other drugs — do not mix in the same line.

Foods/Substances — Special Diet Consideration

Ketogenic diet caution Children on a ketogenic diet (used for refractory epilepsy) or with certain metabolic disorders (e.g., pyruvate dehydrogenase deficiency) should receive normal saline rather than dextrose-containing IV fluids — dextrose can trigger seizures and worsen their condition in this specific population.

Patient Education — Split by Audience & Developmental Stage

Parent / Caregiver Education
  • Teach seizure first aid: side-lying position, never put anything in the mouth, do not restrain, time the seizure
  • Teach exactly when to call EMS (see list above) vs. when to simply document and inform the provider at the next visit
  • Stress the importance of giving antiepileptic medication on schedule to prevent recurrence — a pill box can help prevent missed doses
  • For febrile seizures specifically: reassure that these are generally benign, with no difference in long-term intelligence, memory, or academic performance compared to peers; tepid sponge baths are NOT recommended (ineffective, cause shivering which increases metabolic rate, and cause discomfort)
  • Have the child wear medical identification
  • Sending a child with a chronic seizure disorder home from school after a seizure is not automatically necessary unless the parents request it
Pediatric / Child-Directed Education Seizures occur across all pediatric ages; adjust explanation accordingly
  • School-age (concrete thinkers): simple explanation of what a seizure is ("sometimes the brain sends too many signals at once and your body reacts — it's not something you did wrong") and what classmates/teachers will do to help keep them safe
  • Adolescent: more complete explanation of the condition, medication adherence rationale, and safety considerations (e.g., swimming supervision, driving restrictions per state law once old enough) — involve directly in self-management

Self-Care Strategies

Ongoing management
  • Take antiepileptic medication exactly as scheduled; use a pill organizer if helpful
  • Keep a seizure log (time, duration, characteristics) to share with the neurology team
  • Identify and avoid known personal seizure triggers where possible
  • Wear medical identification
  • Know the specific "call EMS now" criteria for this child

Meningitis

Meningitis

Inflammation of CSF & meninges — bacterial vs. viral

Pathophysiology & Etiology

Inflammation of the CSF and meninges due to infection. Lecture Two major etiologic categories:

  • Septic/Bacterial: most commonly Haemophilus influenzae type b, Streptococcus pneumoniae, or Neisseria meningitidis. Meningococcal meningitis occurs in epidemic form and is transmitted by droplets from nasopharyngeal secretions.
  • Aseptic/Viral: most commonly enterovirus; also mumps, herpesvirus, cytomegalovirus, arbovirus. Generally more self-limiting with a shorter course and fewer complications than bacterial meningitis.
Risk Factors
  • Incomplete immunization status (Hib, pneumococcal, meningococcal vaccines all reduce risk)
  • Age under 2 months (highest mortality risk group)
  • Complications of neurosurgery, trauma, or sinus/ear infections
  • Systemic infections

Signs & Symptoms

Infant (up to ~18 months)
  • Tense, bulging fontanelle
  • Increasing head circumference
  • High-pitched cry
  • Poor feeding, vomiting, diarrhea
  • Fever, chills — but may be difficult to detect classic meningeal signs at this age
All Children
  • Fever, headache
  • Stiff neck (nuchal rigidity)
  • Positive Brudzinski sign (neck flexion causes hip/knee flexion)
  • Positive Kernig sign (inability to extend the leg when hip is flexed)
  • Decreased LOC, photophobia, irritability, anorexia, emesis, seizures

Complications

  • Communicating hydrocephalus (especially in infants — a major driver of residual deficits at this age)
  • Hearing loss — common; audiology evaluation needed shortly after discharge
  • Seizures during the first few days of treatment
  • SIADH, subdural effusions, cerebral edema and herniation
  • Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome (with meningococcemia): overwhelming septic shock, DIC, massive bilateral adrenal hemorrhage, purpura — a dramatic and serious complication
  • Long-term neurodevelopmental sequelae: up to half of survivors have some deficit, ranging from mild learning/behavioral issues to profound hearing impairment, intractable epilepsy, or significant intellectual disability

  • Signs of shock or petechial/purpuric rash (possible meningococcemia progressing to Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome)
  • New seizure activity
  • Signs of SIADH: decreased urine output with concurrent hyponatremia

Priority Nursing Interventions — Acuity Order

  1. Will Kill First
    Administer antibiotics as soon as they are ordered — this is the single highest-priority nursing action in suspected bacterial meningitis. Why: Acute bacterial meningitis is a medical emergency; prognosis is directly tied to how quickly antibiotic therapy begins relative to symptom onset. Delay increases mortality and long-term neurologic sequelae risk.
  2. Will Kill First
    Initiate respiratory (droplet) isolation precautions for at least 24 hours after starting antimicrobial therapy. Why: Bacterial meningitis (particularly meningococcal and H. influenzae) is transmissible via respiratory droplets; it takes approximately 24 hours for antibiotics to reach a therapeutic level and render the child noninfectious.
  3. Urgent
    Maintain hydration, ventilation, and manage systemic shock; control seizures and temperature; monitor closely for and treat increased ICP. Why: These supportive measures address the major secondary complications that drive morbidity/mortality once antibiotics are underway.
  4. Important
    Careful I&O monitoring to detect early signs of cerebral edema/SIADH or impending shock; maintain IV access for the full antibiotic course (typically ~10 days).
Isolation Precaution Type — Common Exam Trap Bacterial meningitis requires respiratory/droplet isolation, NOT enteric precautions (transmission is not GI) and NOT neutropenic precautions (that's for low neutrophil counts, unrelated to this transmission route). This exact distractor pattern appears on NCLEX-style questions.

Treatment

  • Empiric IV antibiotics started immediately, adjusted once organism identified (cephalosporins are often first-line; vancomycin added given rising antibiotic-resistant S. pneumoniae)
  • Respiratory isolation for at least 24 hours after starting antibiotics
  • IV fluids, antiepileptic drugs, and blood products as needed
  • ICU-level monitoring in many cases
  • Audiology evaluation after recovery given the high rate of associated hearing loss

Prevention

Vaccination is the foundation of prevention Vaccines exist for pneumococci, meningococci (types A, C, Y, W-135), and Hib. Routine meningococcal conjugate vaccination is recommended at age 11–12 years with a booster at 16, though it can be given earlier (age 2 months–10 years) in high-risk children. Given bacterial meningitis carries roughly a 10% mortality rate, on-schedule immunization is one of the most impactful things a nurse can encourage.

Patient Education — Split by Audience & Developmental Stage

Parent / Caregiver Education
  • Emphasize on-schedule immunization (Hib, pneumococcal, meningococcal) as the primary prevention strategy
  • Explain the sudden nature of bacterial meningitis and reassure parents that they were not negligent — many parents feel guilty for not recognizing severity sooner; this guilt is common and should be addressed directly
  • Prepare families for a possible extended IV antibiotic course (~10 days) — some children go home with an intermittent infusion device and parents may be taught IV administration
  • Arrange audiology follow-up given the high incidence of associated hearing loss
  • Discuss possible long-term neurodevelopmental monitoring needs
Pediatric / Child-Directed Education Acute illness often limits direct child education initially; scale up as the child recovers
  • Infant: caregiver-directed entirely; comfort through minimal handling and consistent caregivers
  • School-age (once alert/recovering): simple explanation that "germs got into the fluid around your brain, and the medicine is fighting them" — reassure about isolation precautions so the child doesn't feel like something is wrong with them personally
  • Adolescent: full explanation of diagnosis, treatment course, and any activity restrictions during recovery

Self-Care Strategies

Ongoing/recovery management
  • Complete the full antibiotic course exactly as prescribed, even after symptoms improve
  • Attend the scheduled audiology evaluation after discharge
  • Keep neurology follow-up appointments to monitor for developmental sequelae
  • Ensure any other household children/contacts are up to date on relevant vaccines and, if indicated, receive prophylactic antibiotics per public health guidance for close contacts

Encephalitis

Encephalitis

Inflammation of brain tissue itself, not just the meninges

Pathophysiology & Etiology

Inflammation of brain tissue, as distinct from meningitis (which inflames the meninges/CSF). Most commonly caused by mosquito-borne viruses and herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1). Lecture

Risk Factors
  • Mosquito exposure in endemic areas/seasons (arboviral encephalitis)
  • HSV-1 infection
  • Immunocompromised status

Signs & Symptoms

Clinical manifestations
  • Confusion
  • Headache
  • Nuchal rigidity
  • High fever
  • Photophobia
  • Lethargy
  • Seizures
  • Coma (in severe cases)
Meningitis vs. encephalitis — the key distinction: Meningitis inflames the meninges/CSF (classic signs: nuchal rigidity, Kernig/Brudzinski). Encephalitis inflames brain tissue itself, so confusion, altered mental status, and coma are more prominent relative to meningeal signs. The two can overlap clinically and even coexist (meningoencephalitis).

Complications

  • Coma
  • Permanent neurologic damage, especially with HSV encephalitis if treatment is delayed
  • Seizure disorder
  • Death in severe, untreated cases
Notify the Provider Immediately If
  • Worsening confusion or declining LOC
  • New seizure activity
  • Signs of increased ICP developing

Priority Nursing Interventions

  1. Will Kill First
    Monitor LOC and neurologic status closely; protect airway if consciousness declines. Why: Progression to coma and airway compromise are the most immediate threats; early recognition of decline allows faster escalation of care.
  2. Urgent
    Institute seizure precautions and fever management; support antiviral therapy (e.g., acyclovir for HSV encephalitis) as ordered — timing matters significantly for HSV-specific treatment. Why: Untreated HSV encephalitis carries a high risk of severe permanent damage or death; prompt antiviral therapy significantly improves outcomes.
  3. Important
    Provide a quiet, low-stimulation environment; manage photophobia and headache.

Treatment

  • Antiviral therapy (e.g., acyclovir) when HSV is suspected or confirmed — early initiation is critical
  • Supportive care: fever control, seizure management, ICP monitoring as needed
  • Quiet, low-stimulation environment for photophobia and headache

Patient Education — Split by Audience & Developmental Stage

Parent / Caregiver Education
  • Explain the difference between encephalitis and meningitis if the family has heard both terms, to reduce confusion
  • Discuss mosquito-bite prevention strategies (repellent, protective clothing, eliminating standing water) if an arboviral cause is identified or suspected in the region
  • Prepare families for a potentially prolonged recovery and the possibility of lasting neurologic effects, especially with HSV encephalitis
Pediatric / Child-Directed Education Acute confusion/altered LOC often limits direct education during the acute phase
  • During acute illness: education is minimal and caregiver-directed given confusion/lethargy; focus on comfort and orientation cues (familiar voices, simple reassurance)
  • During recovery (school-age/adolescent): simple, honest explanation of what happened and what to expect during rehabilitation if any deficits are present

Self-Care Strategies

Ongoing management
  • Complete the full antiviral course as prescribed
  • Attend neurologic and developmental follow-up given the risk of lasting effects
  • Implement mosquito-bite prevention measures if an arboviral cause was identified

Reye's Syndrome

Reye's Syndrome

Post-viral encephalopathy linked to aspirin use — now largely preventable

Pathophysiology & Etiology

An acute encephalopathy that follows a viral illness, causing cerebral edema and fatty changes/accumulation in internal organs (especially the liver), along with fluid/electrolyte imbalance, acid-base imbalance, and coagulopathies. Results in increased ICP. Lecture The exact mechanism is unclear, but it is strongly linked to administration of acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin/ASA) during a viral illness — classically influenza or varicella (chickenpox). Primarily affects children ages 4–14 years. Lecture

Risk Factors
  • Aspirin/ASA use during a viral illness (influenza, varicella) — the single most important modifiable risk factor
  • Age 4–14 years
  • Recent (4–7 days prior) systemic viral illness

Signs & Symptoms

Clinical manifestations
  • History of recent viral illness (4–7 days before symptom onset)
  • Fever
  • Restlessness
  • Vomiting, nausea
  • Drowsiness progressing to lethargy (signs of altered hepatic function)
  • Progressive neurological deterioration
  • Seizures
  • Loss of consciousness

Complications

  • Increased ICP and cerebral edema — can be fatal if unmanaged
  • Liver failure/altered hepatic function
  • Coagulopathy and bleeding tendency
  • Progressive coma

Notify the Provider Immediately If
  • Progressive lethargy or decline in LOC in a child recovering from a viral illness who received ASA
  • New bleeding or bruising (coagulopathy)
  • Rising serum ammonia or liver enzymes on repeat labs
  • Signs of increased ICP (see ICP section)

Priority Nursing Interventions — Acuity Order

  1. Will Kill First
    Assess airway; administer and monitor oxygen. Lecture Why: Progressive neurologic deterioration can compromise airway protection; oxygenation supports cerebral perfusion in the setting of rising ICP.
  2. Will Kill First
    Perform frequent neuro assessments to catch early deterioration; institute seizure precautions. Lecture Why: Reye's syndrome causes increased ICP, and early detection of neurologic decline allows faster intervention before herniation risk escalates.
  3. Urgent
    Provide a quiet, dimly lit environment to decrease cerebral stimulation — this is the historically emphasized priority nursing intervention for Reye's syndrome specifically. Why: Reducing environmental stimuli decreases stress on already-compromised cerebral tissue and reduces the likelihood of triggering increased ICP symptoms or seizures.
  4. Important
    Monitor intake/output, liver function results, and coagulation status; watch for bleeding/bruising.
Classic NCLEX Priority Question When asked for the priority nursing intervention for a child with Reye's syndrome, the tested answer is typically "provide a quiet atmosphere with dimmed lighting" — this reflects the emphasis on minimizing cerebral stimulation given the ICP/cerebral edema pathophysiology, over interventions like repositioning every 2 hours (which addresses skin integrity, not the core threat here).

Treatment

  • Supportive care: airway/oxygen management, neuro monitoring, seizure precautions
  • Treatment of increased ICP as needed
  • Correction of fluid/electrolyte and coagulation abnormalities
  • Goal: maintain effective cerebral perfusion and control rising ICP

Prevention

The single most important prevention message Avoid aspirin (ASA) and aspirin-containing products in children with a febrile or viral illness (including varicella and influenza). No ASA under age 19. Lecture Acetaminophen and ibuprofen are the medications of choice for fever/pain management in children with viral illness.

Patient Education — Split by Audience & Developmental Stage

Parent / Caregiver Education
  • Read all labels of over-the-counter medications and products — many combination products contain aspirin/salicylates without it being obvious from the product name (e.g., some stomach remedies, certain cold products) Lecture
  • No aspirin-containing products for anyone under 19 years old, especially during a febrile or viral illness Lecture
  • Use acetaminophen or ibuprofen instead for fever/pain during viral illness
  • Recognize the early warning signs (persistent vomiting, worsening lethargy after a viral illness seems to be resolving) and seek care immediately if they appear
Pediatric / Child-Directed Education Primarily affects ages 4–14 (school-age through early adolescence)
  • School-age: simple language about why they're getting a different medicine than aspirin when they're sick, framed positively rather than scary
  • Older school-age/early adolescent: can understand the basic aspirin-avoidance rule themselves, which supports self-advocacy as they get older and may self-medicate more independently

Self-Care Strategies

Prevention-focused, ongoing
  • Always check medication labels for aspirin/salicylates before giving any OTC product to a child, especially during a viral illness
  • Keep only acetaminophen and ibuprofen on hand as the go-to fever/pain relievers for children
  • Seek prompt medical evaluation for a child who seems to be recovering from a viral illness but then develops new vomiting, lethargy, or behavior change

Spina Bifida

Spina Bifida

Neural tube defect — failure of the neural tube to close

Pathophysiology & Etiology

The neural tube fails to close early in fetal development (normally closes ~4 weeks post-conception). Lecture Multifactorial etiology involving both genetic and environmental risk factors — most notably, inadequate maternal folic acid intake. Severity and clinical presentation vary widely based on lesion location and how much neural tissue is involved.

  • Inadequate maternal folic acid intake before and during early pregnancy
  • Family history / previous pregnancy affected by a neural tube defect (greatest risk factor — increases recommended folic acid dose to 4 mg daily, vs. 0.4 mg standard, starting at least 1 month before conception)
  • Certain maternal medications (e.g., some antiepileptics) and environmental exposures

Signs & Symptoms

Clinical manifestations (myelomeningocele, most common presentation of concern)
  • Visible sac on the back at the defect site
  • Neurologic deficits below the level of the lesion — flaccid paralysis, sensory deficits
  • Impaired bowel and bladder function (neurogenic bladder is nearly universal with lumbar/sacral lesions)
  • Possible orthopedic deformities: clubfoot, hip dislocation, scoliosis/kyphosis

Complications

  • Hydrocephalus (very commonly associated, especially with Chiari II malformation — see Hydrocephalus section)
  • Neurogenic bladder/bowel with risk of chronic UTIs, renal damage if unmanaged
  • Tethered cord — presents as a change in functional ability (especially lower extremities, bowel, or bladder), often after a growth spurt; detected via MRI, may be surgically repaired but can recur
  • Latex allergy — children with spina bifida are at high risk due to repeated latex exposure during multiple surgeries/procedures; reactions range from urticaria to full anaphylaxis
  • Orthopedic deformities requiring ongoing management
Latex Allergy — Critical, Lifelong Safety Issue Because of repeated early and ongoing latex exposure, children with spina bifida should be managed in a latex-free environment from birth onward. Screen for latex sensitivity at every preoperative/preprocedural visit — ask about reactions to balloons, adhesive strips, rubber toys, and food cross-reactions (banana, avocado, kiwi, chestnut). Latex-hypersensitive children should carry medical identification.

    Prepare for surgical closure and coordinate the multidisciplinary team (neurosurgery, urology, orthopedics, PT/OT).

Hydrocephalus

Hydrocephalus

CSF accumulation — imbalance of production/absorption

Pathophysiology & Etiology

Accumulation of CSF within the intracranial vault and spinal cord, from an imbalance of CSF absorption or production. Lecture Causes include congenital anomalies, tumors, hemorrhage, infections, or trauma; also commonly occurs after surgical closure of a myelomeningocele (Type II Chiari malformation obstructs CSF flow in most myelomeningocele cases). Lecture

Risk Factors
  • Congenital anomalies (including Chiari II malformation)
  • Myelomeningocele (very high association)
  • Intraventricular hemorrhage, especially in preterm infants
  • CNS infection (meningitis) or tumor

Signs & Symptoms

Presentation depends on the child's age, the cause, and the rate of hydrocephalus development.

Infancy (before suture closure)
  • Abnormal, rapid head growth (increasing OFC — the predominant early sign)
  • Bulging, tense, nonpulsatile anterior fontanel
  • Prominent forehead/frontal bossing
  • Setting-sun sign; sluggish, unequal pupil response
  • Dilated scalp veins, especially with crying
  • Macewen (cracked-pot) sign on percussion
  • Irritable, lethargic, poor feeding
  • Difficulty holding the head upright Lecture
  • Shrill, brief, high-pitched cry if progressing
Later Childhood (after suture closure)
  • Signs and symptoms are those of increased ICP (see ICP section) since the skull can no longer expand to compensate

Complications

  • Increased ICP, herniation risk if untreated
  • Developmental delay from sustained pressure effects
  • Shunt-related complications (infection, malfunction — see VP Shunt content below)
  • Cranial nerve dysfunction from brainstem compression (in Chiari malformation): swallowing difficulty, stridor, apnea, aspiration

Labs & Diagnostics

TestPurpose
CT, MRI, ultrasoundVisualize ventricular size and CSF accumulation Lecture
Serial head circumference measurementTrack rate of head growth — a simple, essential bedside tool Lecture
Notify the Provider Immediately If
  • Head circumference increasing faster than expected trajectory
  • New bulging fontanel, setting-sun sign, or downward eye deviation

  • Any signs of increased ICP

Priority Nursing Interventions — Preoperative and Postoperative

  1. Will Kill First
    Preoperatively: reposition the infant frequently rather than providing an overly stimulating environment; monitor closely for worsening ICP. Why: A large, heavy hydrocephalic head increases pressure injury risk with immobility, and overstimulation can exacerbate irritability and ICP; frequent repositioning is the priority preoperative intervention per Saunders.
  2. Will Kill First
    Postoperatively: assess for increased ICP — monitor vital signs, head circumference, fontanels, and watch for seizures/lethargy. Why: Shunt placement itself can be complicated by bleeding, infection, or malfunction in the immediate postoperative period, all of which can rapidly raise ICP again.
  3. Urgent
    Position the child on the non-operative side and elevate the head no higher than 30 degrees per MD orders (unless otherwise specified). Lecture Why: This positioning protects the operative site while managing ICP; overly rapid CSF drainage from over-elevation can itself cause complications.
  4. Urgent
    Administer preoperative antibiotics; monitor for postoperative signs of infection. Lecture
  5. Important
    Monitor head circumference trends and document serially throughout the hospital stay.

Ventriculoperitoneal (VP) Shunt

Relieves pressure by removing CSF from the head and draining it into the abdomen (peritoneal cavity). Lecture

Clinical Manifestations of Shunt Malfunction — By Age
  • Infant: irritability, lethargy, poor feeding, high-shrill cry
  • Toddler: headache and lack of appetite are the earliest common signs
  • Older child: alteration in level of consciousness is a key indicator
  • All ages: vomiting, swelling/redness along the shunt tract, decreased school performance, periods of confusion, seizures
Critical safety point Monitor for shunt presence/tract behind the ear during assessment — a high, shrill cry in an infant can be an early sign of increased ICP and shunt malfunction, and should never be dismissed as routine fussiness.

Treatment

  • VP shunt placement — the primary surgical treatment
  • Antibiotics for shunt infection
  • Shunt revision or removal/replacement for malfunction Lecture

Parent / Caregiver Education

  • No contact sports once a shunt is in place, given the risk of shunt damage/malfunction from head trauma Lecture

  • Toddler/preschool: simple language about "the tube that helps your brain" without overwhelming detail; comfort through routine and consistent caregivers
  • School-age: teach the child to recognize and report their own early symptoms (headache, feeling "foggy," nausea) so they become an active participant in catching shunt problems early

Cerebral Palsy

Cerebral Palsy

Most common physical disability of childhood — brain injury before development completes

Pathophysiology & Etiology

The most common physical disability of childhood, resulting from brain injury occurring before development is complete (prenatally, perinatally, or postnatally). Lecture Caused by an abnormality in the extrapyramidal or pyramidal motor system. Represents a wide range of disability severity.

Risk Factors — By Timing
  • Prenatal: maternal infection, chemical exposure, trauma, medication, illicit drug use, metabolic insults during pregnancy Lecture
  • Perinatal: birth asphyxia, prematurity, birth trauma
  • Postnatal: CNS infection, head trauma, hypoxic events after birth Lecture

Spastic (pyramidal)Most common type (70–80% of cases). Persistent primitive reflexes, positive Babinski, ankle clonus, exaggerated stretch reflexes, eventual contractures. Subtypes: diplegia (lower > upper extremities), hemiplegia (one side, upper > lower), tetraplegia (all four limbs plus mouth/pharynx/tongue), monoplegia/triplegia.

Clinical manifestations
  • Extreme irritability and crying
  • Feeding difficulties
  • Abnormal motor performance — e.g., preferential unilateral hand use before the developmentally expected age (~6 months), asymmetric or "bunny hop" crawling patterns

  • Contractures and joint deformities over time if spasticity is unmanaged

  • Communication impairment, especially with athetoid-type CP affecting oral/pharyngeal muscles
  • Psychosocial impact of visible physical disability

Labs & Diagnostics

CP is a clinical diagnosis based on history and physical/motor exam findings over time — there is no single confirmatory lab test. Imaging (MRI) may identify an underlying structural cause but is not required for diagnosis.

    Educate parents on maintaining a safe home environment tailored to the child's specific mobility and sensory profile. Lecture

  • Splints and braces; assistive devices
  • Frequent rest periods to reduce muscle spasm Lecture
  • School enrollment and early intervention services Lecture
  • Feeding supervision and support tailored to individual needs Lecture

Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI)

Traumatic Brain Injury

Mechanical force to skull, scalp, meninges, or brain

Pathophysiology & Etiology

Head injury is the pathological result of any mechanical force applied to the skull, scalp, meninges, or brain.

  • Open head injury: occurs with a skull fracture or penetrating object.
  • Closed head injury: results from blunt trauma. More serious than open injury because the "closed" cranial vault traps any resulting swelling/bleeding, increasing ICP risk. Can also result from shaken baby syndrome/abusive head trauma.
Risk Factors
  • Falls (most common mechanism in young children)
  • Motor vehicle/pedestrian/bicycle collisions
  • Sports-related impacts
  • Non-accidental trauma (see Abusive Head Trauma section)

Signs & Symptoms

Manifestations depend on injury type and resulting degree of increased ICP — refer to the Increased ICP section for the full early/late sign list, which applies directly to TBI assessment.

Common post-concussive symptoms
  • Headache
  • Difficulty concentrating
  • A few episodes of vomiting (can be expected/self-limited after concussion)
  • Dizziness, balance problems
  • Sleep pattern changes

Complications

  • Cerebral edema — peaks 24–72 hours post-injury; a major cause of secondary neurologic decline
  • Postconcussion syndrome (PCS): symptoms lasting more than 4 weeks — headaches, visual/balance problems, difficulty concentrating, irritability, sleep changes. Risk factors: personal/family history of mood disorders, migraines.
  • Posttraumatic seizures — often within 24 hours but can occur later
  • Posttraumatic headaches, often migraine-type, occurring 1 week to 3 months post-injury
  • Cervical/spinal cord injury if mechanism involved significant force
Notify the Provider / Seek Immediate Care If (Post-Concussion, at Home)
  • Repeated or worsening vomiting
  • Worsening headache (rather than gradual improvement)
  • New double vision, confusion, or difficulty waking the child
  • Any seizure activity
  • Increasing lethargy or behavior change

Priority Nursing Interventions — Acuity Order (Acute Injury Scene/ED)

  1. Will Kill First
    Assess airway, breathing, and circulation (ABCs) first, exactly as with any trauma. Why: Airway and circulatory compromise kill faster than the head injury itself in the acute trauma setting — the neuro exam always comes after ABCs are secured.
  2. Will Kill First
    Immediately after ABCs: stabilize the neck and spine before any other action, particularly in a mechanism involving significant force (e.g., struck by a vehicle). Why: An unstabilized cervical spine injury can be catastrophically worsened by movement — this must happen before repositioning, transport, or further assessment of other injuries.
  3. Urgent
    Once spine is cleared or stabilized: assess for other injuries, check scalp and back for bleeding, monitor neuro status and vital signs closely. Why: Secondary injury assessment matters but must follow, not precede, spinal precautions.
  4. Important
    Position the head midline, HOB 15–30 degrees once spinal injury is ruled out, to promote venous drainage and reduce ICP (see ICP section for full detail).

Scenario: A 10-year-old is hit by a car while riding his bicycle. The school nurse has already assessed ABCs. What is the next nursing action?

Correct answer: Stabilize the neck and spine. This comes before placing the child on their side, taking blood pressure, or checking for scalp/back bleeding — all reasonable actions, but sequenced after spinal stabilization given the high-force mechanism of injury.

  • Teach the specific home-monitoring signs that require calling the physician immediately versus signs that are part of expected recovery

Self-Care Strategies

Post-concussion recovery
  • Follow the individualized graduated return-to-activity/return-to-school plan provided by the care team
  • Track symptoms daily and report any worsening rather than improvement over the expected timeline
  • Avoid a second head injury during the recovery period — this carries disproportionate risk (second impact syndrome)
  • Get adequate sleep and manage screen time/cognitive load as tolerated during early recovery

Abusive Head Trauma (AHT)

Abusive Head Trauma

"Abusive Head Trauma" is the preferred term (formerly "Shaken Baby Syndrome")
Terminology Lecture "Abusive Head Trauma" (AHT) is the currently preferred clinical term. "Shaken Baby Syndrome" is an older term you may still hear, referring to the non-accidental mechanism. Kentucky mandates AHT training for nurses and foster parents, and requires new parents to view an educational video before hospital discharge.

Pathophysiology & Etiology

Lecture When an infant is shaken forcefully, the brain and its bridging blood vessels move within the skull, but the superior sagittal sinus remains fixed (attached to the dura mater). This differential movement causes shearing and tearing of the bridging vessels, resulting in bleeding into the subdural spaces. Rotational forces and angular deceleration are the specific injury mechanisms — not a simple, direct impact.

Why Infants Are at Higher Risk
  • Skull shape and proportionally large head size relative to body/neck strength Lecture
  • Weak neck muscles unable to control head movement during shaking
  • Higher water content and less myelination of infant brain tissue, making it more susceptible to shearing injury

Signs & Symptoms

Critical concept: absence of external trauma is common Lecture AHT frequently presents with NO visible external signs of injury — this is precisely what makes it dangerous to miss. Presentation varies enormously depending on injury severity, and inconsistencies between the caregiver's history and the physical exam findings are a major red flag.
If Impact Occurred
  • Skull fracture, bruising, swelling Lecture
Other Findings Sometimes Seen With AHT
  • Metaphyseal fractures (highly specific for abuse in a non-mobile infant)
  • Other fractures
  • Bruising of the skin
  • Internal abdominal injuries
  • Or... nothing at all. Signs and symptoms may be entirely vague: fussiness, changes in behavior, lethargy Lecture
Common Presenting Scenarios Lecture
  • Infant with bruises in unusual/inconsistent locations
  • Vomiting without diarrhea
  • Apparent Life-Threatening Event (ALTE)
  • Sudden increase in head circumference
  • Developmental delays
  • Incidental fracture findings on imaging done for another reason

The TEN-4 Rule — Bruising Red Flags Lecture

Be suspicious of ANY bruising on the:
  • Torso
  • Ears
  • Neck

...of a child 4 years or younger

— OR —

ANY bruising, anywhere, on a child 4 months or younger (a pre-mobile infant should have essentially no accidental bruising, since they cannot yet generate the mechanism for one).

Things That Will NOT Cause AHT Lecture

Important for family education and clinical reasoning
  • Bouncing (normal play)
  • Short falls
  • Rough play (normal, developmentally appropriate)
  • Immunizations
  • Toddlers or pets (a common but false caregiver explanation)
  • Birth trauma (with rare exception in specific circumstances)

Knowing this list matters clinically: if a caregiver's explanation attributes significant intracranial injury to one of these mechanisms, that inconsistency is itself a red flag warranting further investigation.

Complications / Outcomes

Sobering statistics Lecture
  • Approximately 1,300 cases of AHT reported annually in the U.S.
  • Of those, approximately 25% die
  • Of survivors, up to 80% suffer lifelong disabilities (ranging from mild to profound)

Disabilities include: learning disabilities, behavior issues, speech and language delays, vision/hearing issues, neuromotor and/or visual impairment.

  • Caucasian families
  • "Intact" (two-parent) families
  • Middle-class, well-educated families
  • Families perceived to be "like our own"
  • Very young infants
  • Infants presenting with non-specific symptoms

This is a crucial clinical judgment point: implicit bias about "what an abusive family looks like" is a documented cause of missed AHT diagnoses. Every infant with concerning findings deserves the same level of suspicion regardless of family appearance or socioeconomic status.

Report / Escalate Immediately If
  • Any bruising meeting the TEN-4 rule criteria
  • Inconsistency between the reported mechanism of injury and the clinical findings
  • Any suspected abuse — mandatory reporting applies regardless of the reporter's certainty level; suspicion alone is the legal and ethical threshold, not proof

Priority Nursing Interventions — Acuity Order

  1. Will Kill First
    Stabilize the child medically first: manage airway/ICP/any acute injury exactly as with any TBI (see ICP and TBI sections) — the medical emergency takes priority over the forensic/reporting process. Why: A child with acute AHT may have life-threatening intracranial bleeding and increased ICP; this must be addressed immediately regardless of how the injury occurred.
  2. Urgent
    Document objectively and thoroughly: signs/symptoms observed, caregiver statements verbatim when possible, and observations of parent-child interaction. Take photographs of any visible injuries per facility protocol. Lecture Why: Careful, objective, timely documentation is critical both for the child's ongoing medical care and for any subsequent legal/protective proceedings — and memory/details fade quickly.
  3. Urgent
    Report suspected abuse to the local child protective services (CPS) agency and/or follow facility-specific reporting guidelines. Lecture Why: Nurses are mandated reporters; a delay in reporting can leave a child at continued risk of repeat, potentially fatal injury.
  4. Important
    Provide nonjudgmental family education and support resources — see Prevention below.

Treatment

  • Medical stabilization per the specific injury pattern (ICP management, seizure control, surgical intervention if indicated)
  • Ongoing developmental and rehabilitative support for survivors given the high rate of lasting disability
  • Coordinated care with CPS, forensic teams, and specialized child abuse pediatrics consultation

Prevention — This Is Where Nursing Education Has the Most Impact Lecture

  • Help parents understand that it is normal for babies to cry, and normal for caregivers to feel frustrated and need help coping — normalizing these feelings reduces shame and opens the door to seeking help before a crisis
  • Be a resource for the family; talk openly about the dangers of shaking a baby
  • Teach parents about the "Period of PURPLE Crying" — a research-based program framing the developmentally normal, unsoothable crying period in early infancy (peaks around 6–8 weeks) as a temporary, expected phase, not a sign of a problem with the baby or the parent's caregiving
  • Educate about crisis resources: 1-800-4-A-CHILD (ChildHelp National Child Abuse Hotline)
  • Report to CPS if abuse is suspected; document thoroughly

Patient/Family Education — Split by Audience & Developmental Stage

Parent / Caregiver Education (Universal — All New Parents)
  • It is never safe to shake an infant, under any circumstance or provocation, even briefly or in frustration
  • Teach concrete strategies for coping with an inconsolable crying baby: place the baby safely in the crib and step into another room for a few minutes to calm down, check on the baby periodically, and call someone for support/relief if needed
  • Teach the Period of PURPLE Crying concept — this normal developmental phase peaks and then resolves; it is not a reflection of parenting failure
  • Provide the ChildHelp hotline number (1-800-4-A-CHILD) and encourage saving it in their phone before it's ever needed
  • Discuss recognizing personal stress/frustration triggers and having a plan (a trusted family member, friend, or hotline to call) before reaching a breaking point
  • Encourage open conversation about the realistic challenges of newborn care without judgment, since isolation and shame increase risk
Pediatric / Child-Directed Education AHT occurs in infancy; the injured child cannot receive direct education — focus is entirely on caregiver prevention education and, for survivors, developmental support
  • Not applicable for the infant victim directly; all preventive education is caregiver-directed
  • For older children in the household: age-appropriate education about safe touch and body safety is part of general child maltreatment prevention (see Child Maltreatment section)

Self-Care / Family Resources

Supporting new parents to prevent AHT
  • Keep the ChildHelp hotline (1-800-4-A-CHILD) accessible
  • Build a support network before the baby's frustrating crying periods begin — identify who can provide a break
  • Use the "put baby down safely and step away" strategy whenever feeling overwhelmed by crying
  • Seek mental health support proactively if experiencing postpartum mood symptoms, since untreated maternal/paternal mental illness is a documented risk factor
  • Review Period of PURPLE Crying resources (purplecrying.info) for concrete coping strategies

Maltreatment of Children

Maltreatment of Children

Abuse and neglect — broader category encompassing AHT

Definition

Includes abuse and neglect of a child less than 18 years of age by anyone in a caregiver or custodial role. Lecture

Nursing Care — Core Responsibilities Lecture

  1. Urgent
    Identify risk factors related to maltreatment during routine assessments — this is an ongoing surveillance responsibility for every pediatric nurse, not a specialized task. Why: Early identification of risk factors (see Caregiver Characteristics under AHT) allows intervention before an injury occurs.
  2. Important
    Teach parents and caregivers appropriate, non-violent discipline strategies; educate about the dangers of alcohol/drug use during pregnancy and discourage caregiver substance use generally.
  3. Important
    Educate children and adolescents about their bodies and personal boundaries — an important preventive, protective education strategy.
  4. Urgent
    Report suspected child abuse to the local law enforcement/CPS agency and/or follow clinical agency guidelines for reporting. This is a mandatory, non-optional nursing responsibility.

Patient Education — Split by Audience & Developmental Stage

Parent / Caregiver Education
  • Teach appropriate, developmentally-based discipline strategies as an alternative to physical punishment
  • Educate on the risks of alcohol/drug use during pregnancy and its lasting effects on the child
  • Discourage caregiver substance use and connect families struggling with substance use to treatment resources
  • Normalize seeking help for parenting stress before it escalates to harm (see AHT prevention content)
Pediatric / Child-Directed Education Body safety education should be developmentally tailored and delivered consistently across childhood
  • Preschool/school-age: simple, concrete teaching about which parts of their body are private, the difference between safe and unsafe touch, and that they can always tell a trusted adult if something feels wrong — use clear, direct anatomical language rather than euphemisms, which supports clearer disclosure if something does happen
  • Adolescent: more nuanced conversations about consent, healthy relationships, and recognizing coercive or abusive dynamics; ensure they know multiple trusted adults they could go to, since disclosure to a parent isn't always possible if the parent is the source of harm

Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)

ADHD

Dopamine and norepinephrine dysregulation — exact cause unknown

Pathophysiology & Etiology

Exact cause is unknown; however, the neurotransmitters dopamine (DA) and norepinephrine (NE) are thought to be involved in the underlying dysregulation. Lecture

Signs & Symptoms

Core symptom domains
  • Inattention
  • Hyperactivity-type behaviors
  • Impulsivity

Labs & Diagnostics

There is no single lab test for ADHD. Diagnosis relies on a comprehensive multi-source evaluation: Lecture

  • Medical and developmental histories
  • Physical examination
  • Vision and hearing assessment (to rule out sensory causes of inattention)
  • Detailed neurologic evaluation
  • Behavioral checklists (from parents, teachers, multiple settings)

Treatment — Three-Pronged Approach Lecture

Behavioral Therapy

Structured behavior modification strategies at home and school

Educational Support

Individualized accommodations (IEP/504 plan) as needed

Medication

Stimulant and non-stimulant options — see Medication Reference section

Priority Nursing Interventions

  1. Important
    Support a comprehensive, multi-source diagnostic evaluation rather than relying on a single observation or setting.
  2. Important
    Coordinate the three-pronged treatment approach (behavioral, educational, medication) and monitor medication effects/side effects.

Patient Education — Split by Audience & Developmental Stage

Parent / Caregiver Education
  • Explain that ADHD involves neurotransmitter-level brain differences, not poor parenting or a lack of discipline — this reframing reduces caregiver guilt and blame
  • Teach consistent behavioral strategies to use at home matching those used at school for continuity
  • If medication is prescribed, teach administration timing, expected effects, and monitoring for side effects (see Medication Reference)
  • Support collaboration with the school for educational accommodations
Pediatric / Child-Directed Education Typically diagnosed in school-age children
  • School-age (concrete thinkers): simple, non-shaming language about how their brain works a little differently and that tools (like medication or classroom strategies) are there to help, not because something is "wrong" with them
  • Adolescent: involve directly in understanding their own diagnosis and treatment, including medication self-administration responsibility as appropriate, and discuss any concerns about peer perception of taking medication at school

Self-Care Strategies

Ongoing management
  • Maintain consistent daily routines and structure
  • Take medication as prescribed and on schedule; report side effects rather than stopping independently
  • Use organizational tools/strategies taught through behavioral therapy consistently across home and school settings

Down Syndrome

Down Syndrome

Trisomy 21 — most common chromosomal abnormality

Pathophysiology & Etiology

The most common chromosomal abnormality: 47 chromosomes present, usually as a full trisomy of the 21st pair (Trisomy 21). This changes the development of the body and brain broadly. Lecture

Signs & Symptoms

Classic physical features
  • Poor muscle tone (hypotonia)
  • Slanting eyes (upward palpebral fissures)
  • Hyperflexibility of the joints
  • Flat bridge of the nose
  • Short neck with extra folds of skin
  • Small stature
  • Low-set ears
  • Simian crease (single palmar crease)
  • Protruding tongue

Complications

  • Congenital heart defects (common comorbidity — screening is standard at diagnosis)
  • Hearing and vision problems
  • Hypothyroidism (higher incidence — periodic screening recommended)
  • Intellectual disability, ranging in severity
  • Increased risk of early-onset Alzheimer disease in adulthood
  • Higher risk of leukemia in childhood compared to the general population

Labs & Diagnostics

TestPurpose
Chromosomal blood test (karyotype)Confirms trisomy 21 Lecture
Prenatal screening (cell-free DNA, ultrasound markers)Can identify increased likelihood prenatally; diagnostic confirmation still requires karyotype/genetic testing

Priority Nursing Interventions

  1. Important
    Assist new parents with information and resources at the time of diagnosis — this is often a highly emotional moment requiring sensitive, factual, and hopeful communication. Lecture
  2. Important
    Initiate early intervention services as soon as possible to optimize developmental outcomes. Lecture
  3. Important
    Screen for and coordinate management of associated comorbidities (cardiac, thyroid, vision, hearing).

Treatment

  • Assist new parents with information and resources Lecture
  • Early intervention services Lecture
  • Ongoing multidisciplinary management of associated conditions (cardiology, endocrinology, audiology, ophthalmology)
  • Educational support tailored to the individual child's cognitive profile

Patient Education — Split by Audience & Developmental Stage

Parent / Caregiver Education
  • Provide accurate, current, hopeful information at diagnosis — avoid outdated stereotypes about capability and quality of life
  • Connect families with early intervention services immediately, as early support significantly improves developmental trajectory
  • Explain the need for ongoing screening: cardiac evaluation, thyroid function, vision, and hearing
  • Connect with Down syndrome-specific support organizations and other families for peer support
Pediatric / Child-Directed Education Diagnosed at birth or prenatally; education is caregiver-directed in infancy, evolving as the child grows
  • Infant/toddler: caregiver-directed; focus on early intervention (PT/OT/speech) participation
  • School-age: use concrete, simple teaching methods matched to the child's individual cognitive level — demonstration and repetition are often more effective than verbal explanation alone; break tasks into small sequential steps
  • Adolescent: support growing independence in self-care and social skills to the extent of individual ability; address transition planning for education/vocational services

Self-Care Strategies

Ongoing family management
  • Keep all recommended screening appointments (cardiac, thyroid, vision, hearing) even when the child seems well
  • Engage consistently with early intervention and, later, educational support services
  • Break new skills into small, concrete steps with repetition and demonstration
  • Connect with support networks and Down syndrome-specific resources for ongoing guidance

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)

Autism Spectrum Disorder

A continuum of disorders — NOT caused by immunizations

Pathophysiology & Etiology

A continuum of disorders involving limitations in social relatedness, verbal and nonverbal communication, and a restricted range of interests and behaviors. Lecture

Etiology — a critical point to state explicitly Cause is unknown. NOT caused by immunizations — your lecture slide states this explicitly and directly, reflecting the strong scientific consensus rejecting the vaccine-autism link. Lecture

Signs & Symptoms

Social & Communication
  • Impairment in social reciprocity
  • Inability to maintain eye contact
  • Impaired communication (may include echolalia, monotone speech, or lack of gestures)
  • Lack of awareness of others' feelings or existence
  • Abnormal or absent comfort-seeking behaviors
  • Impaired peer relationships and social play
Behavior
  • Restrictive or repetitive behaviors, interests, or activities
  • Lack of imaginative play
  • Persistent preoccupation/attachment to objects
  • Repetitive body movements (rocking, head banging)
  • Marked distress with any change in environment/routine
  • Possible self-injurious behaviors

Diagnosis

ToolPurpose
"First Signs" ProgramEarly screening/identification framework Lecture
M-CHAT-R (Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised)Standard toddler screening tool used in primary care
Safety priority Ensuring a safe environment for a child with autism is a priority — given the risk of self-injurious behaviors (head banging, etc.) and potential lack of awareness of environmental danger.

Priority Nursing Interventions

  1. Urgent
    Implement safety precautions as necessary for self-injurious behaviors (e.g., padding, close supervision). Why: Self-injurious behaviors and reduced hazard awareness create real physical safety risks that must be proactively managed.
  2. Important
    Determine the child's routines, habits, and preferences and maintain consistency as much as possible; determine the child's specific communication methods and use them consistently. Lecture
  3. Important
    Be aware of the child's physical boundaries and possible reluctance to be touched by others — approach and physical contact should be adapted to the individual child's sensory profile. Lecture
  4. Important
    Avoid placing unnecessary demands on the child; initiate referrals to specialized programs; provide support to parents.

Treatment

  • Early intervention — the most impactful strategy for improving long-term outcomes Lecture
  • Consistent routine — very important for these patients; unexpected change is a major source of distress Lecture
  • Individualized behavioral, communication, and educational therapies
  • Referrals to specialized programs as needed

Patient Education — Split by Audience & Developmental Stage

Parent / Caregiver Education
  • Directly and explicitly correct the vaccine-autism myth if the family raises it — reassure with current evidence while validating that this is a common and understandable concern to have heard
  • Teach the ALARM framework concept: listen to their observations (parents are often the first to notice something is different), act early rather than adopting a "wait and see" approach, and pursue referrals promptly
  • Teach the importance of maintaining consistent routines at home and coordinating consistency with school/therapy settings
  • Teach recognition of the child's individual communication style and specific comfort/distress signals
  • Discuss safety measures for the home environment given self-injury and elopement risks documented in some children with ASD
Pediatric / Child-Directed Education Typically identified in toddlerhood; adapt all teaching to the individual child's communication style and cognitive profile, not just chronological age
  • Toddler/preschool: use the child's preferred communication method (which may not be verbal) consistently; visual schedules and consistent routines reduce anxiety and support understanding of what to expect
  • School-age: continue routine-based, concrete teaching; respect sensory sensitivities and physical boundary preferences during any procedures or exams
  • Adolescent: individualize based on communication ability and cognitive level; involve directly in self-advocacy skill-building where appropriate

Self-Care Strategies

Ongoing family/patient management
  • Maintain consistent daily routines; prepare the child in advance for any necessary changes
  • Use visual supports/schedules if helpful for the individual child
  • Engage consistently with early intervention and ongoing therapy services
  • Implement home safety measures tailored to the child's specific behaviors and risks
  • Respect the child's sensory preferences and physical boundary needs in daily caregiving
